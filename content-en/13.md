## Probability Basics

#### Probability Models and Random Variables
ONE: A **Probability Model** is a framework that sets out "every outcome that can happen" and "how likely each outcome is," and a **Random Variable** is **a rule that attaches a number to each outcome**.
LIKE: When you roll two dice, the list of 36 possible outcomes along with how likely each is (1/36 each) is a Probability Model. A rule that attaches a number to each outcome, like "the sum of the two dice," is a Random Variable.
STEP:
1. **Sample Space (Ω)**: The set of all possible outcomes. For one coin, {heads, tails}.
2. **Event**: A part of the Sample Space. "The die shows an even number" = {2, 4, 6}.
3. **The three rules (axioms) of probability**: ① every probability is 0 or more ② the probability of the whole Sample Space is 1 ③ the probabilities of events that can't happen at the same time can be added.
4. **Random Variable**: A function that turns outcomes into numbers. Example: X = the sum of two dice. Six outcomes give X = 7, so P(X = 7) = 6/36.
5. **Discrete**: The values can be counted one by one. The sum of dice, the number of visitors per day.
6. **Continuous**: The values are continuous. Height, weight, waiting time.
WHY: To handle things whose outcomes can't be known in advance, like weather, stock prices, and user behavior, mathematically, you need a framework like this. The output of a Machine Learning model is ultimately a probability too.
END: Probability Model = possible outcomes + how likely they are; Random Variable = a number attached to each outcome.

#### Conditional Probability
ONE: **"The probability that A happens, given that you know B happened"** is P(A | B). When new information arrives, the world you consider narrows to the cases that fit that information.
LIKE: "The chance it rains tomorrow" and "the chance it rains tomorrow given that the sky is full of dark clouds today" are different. The information about dark clouds narrows down the cases you consider.
STEP:
1. **Formula**: P(A | B) = P(A ∩ B) / P(B) (provided P(B) > 0).
2. Meaning: Shrink the world to the cases where B happened, and look at the fraction of those in which A also happened.
3. Example: The probability that a die shows "4 or more" given that you know it showed "an even number." Of the even numbers {2, 4, 6}, those 4 or more are {4, 6}, so 2/3.
4. **Multiplication rule**: Rearranging the formula gives P(A ∩ B) = P(A | B) × P(B).
5. **Careful**: Usually **P(A | B) ≠ P(B | A)**. "The chance you use an umbrella if it rains" and "the chance it rains if you use an umbrella" are different. Mixing these two up is a very common mistake.
END: Conditional probability is "probability recomputed after learning some information," and flipping the direction changes the value.

#### Independent and Mutually Exclusive
ONE: **Independent** means one event **doesn't affect the probability of another**, and **Mutually Exclusive** means two events **can't happen at the same time**. They look similar but are close to opposites.
LIKE: Toss two coins separately, and the first coin's result doesn't affect the second (independent). Heads and tails on one coin can't both come up at once (mutually exclusive). But once you know it came up heads, the probability of tails becomes 0, so they're actually strongly linked.
STEP:
1. **Independent**: P(A ∩ B) = P(A) × P(B). Or P(A | B) = P(A). Knowing B leaves A's probability unchanged.
2. **Mutually Exclusive**: P(A ∩ B) = 0. They never happen at the same time.
3. If two events with probability greater than 0 are mutually exclusive, the moment one happens the other's probability becomes 0, so they're **dependent, not independent**.
4. When they're mutually exclusive, the probability of "A or B" is just the sum: P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B).
5. When they're independent, the probability of "A and B" is the product: P(A ∩ B) = P(A)P(B).
END: Independent is "no influence" (multiply), mutually exclusive is "can't happen together" (add). Mutually exclusive events with nonzero probability aren't independent.

#### Bayes' Theorem: Updating Beliefs with Evidence
ONE: **Bayes' Theorem** is the formula for **updating your belief in a hypothesis after seeing new evidence**. Your new belief is proportional to "original belief × how likely the evidence is."
LIKE: A detective multiplies "how likely this person was to be the culprit to begin with" (Prior) by "how likely the culprit would leave footprints like these" (Likelihood) to update to "how likely this person is the culprit after seeing the footprints" (Posterior).
STEP:
1. **Formula**: P(H | E) = P(E | H) × P(H) / P(E). H is the hypothesis, E is the evidence.
2. **Prior P(H)**: Your belief before seeing the evidence.
3. **Likelihood P(E | H)**: How likely this evidence would be if the hypothesis were true.
4. **Posterior P(H | E)**: Your belief after seeing the evidence.
5. **P(E)**: The overall probability of seeing the evidence. It's the value (normalizing constant) that makes the probabilities over all hypotheses add up to 1.
6. **Derivation**: From the multiplication rule of conditional probability, P(H ∩ E) = P(H | E)P(E) = P(E | H)P(H), and dividing gives Bayes' formula.
7. **Example**: Three friends each independently tell the truth with probability 2/3, and all of them said "it's raining." If the usual chance of rain (Prior) is 0.5, the actual chance of rain is 8/9 ≈ 89%, but if the Prior is 0.1, it's about 47%. Even with the same evidence, the conclusion changes a lot depending on the Prior.
USE: Spam email classification (Naive Bayes), interpreting medical test results, robot localization, and Bayesian analysis of A/B Tests.
END: New belief (Posterior) ∝ likelihood of the evidence (Likelihood) × original belief (Prior). Don't look only at the evidence; always look at the Prior too.

#### The Base Rate Fallacy: The Trap of a 99% Accurate Test
ONE: No matter how accurate a test is, **if the disease itself is very rare**, **most people who test positive may actually be healthy**. Ignoring the original rate (Base Rate) is a mistake called the Base Rate Fallacy.
LIKE: There's a rare disease that only 10 in 10,000 people have. The test catches 99% of patients as positive, and it also wrongly marks 1% of healthy people as positive. So among people who test positive, what percent actually have the disease?
STEP:
1. Picture 10,000 people. The disease rate (Base Rate) is 0.1%, so there are **10 patients and 9,990 healthy people**.
2. 99% of the 10 patients test positive → about **10 true positives**.
3. 1% of the 9,990 healthy people wrongly test positive → about **100 false positives**.
4. About 110 people test positive in total. Of them, only 10 actually have the disease.
5. So the probability of actually having the disease given a positive result is 10 / 110 ≈ **9%**. Not 99%!
6. Why: Healthy people vastly outnumber patients, so even a 1% error rate produces far more false positives than there are real patients.
KEY: **Turning probabilities into head counts** like "how many out of 10,000" makes Bayes calculations much easier. It's also why, in real medical practice, a positive result leads to a retest.
USE: It explains why "most alarms are false alarms" in detecting rare fraudulent transactions, testing for rare diseases, and security alert systems. It's also why Machine Learning Precision is heavily affected by the Base Rate (topic 14).
END: Tests that look for rare things produce many false positives even when accurate. Always look at the original rate (Base Rate) too.

#### Bayesian Networks and Reasoning over Time
ONE: A **Bayesian Network** is a probability model that **draws the direct influences between variables as arrows**, and time models like the **Hidden Markov Model** estimate **hidden states that change over time** from observations alone.
LIKE: A Bayesian Network is a cause-and-effect map with arrows like "if it rains → the grass gets wet" and "if the sprinkler runs → the grass gets wet." Seeing that the grass is wet, you can reason backward about how likely it is that it rained.
STEP:
1. **Bayesian Network**: Draw variables as points and direct influences as arrows (a directed graph with no Cycles).
2. Each variable sets its probabilities by **looking only at its parents (the variables sending it arrows)**. That lets the joint probability of hundreds of variables be represented as many small tables.
3. From observed evidence (the grass is wet), you can compute the probability of a hidden cause (did it rain?). When exact computation is hard, you approximate by drawing many samples (Monte Carlo Sampling).
4. **Time models**: Cases where a hidden state changes over time and we only see observations. Example: a robot's actual position (hidden) and its sensor readings (observed).
5. **Hidden Markov Model (HMM)**: When the hidden state is discrete. **Kalman Filter**: For continuous values (position, velocity), used in GPS and self-driving. **Particle Filter**: Approximates complex cases with many samples.
6. What they do: estimating the current state (Filtering), predicting the future (Prediction), re-estimating past states (Smoothing), and finding the most likely sequence of states (Viterbi).
USE: Medical diagnosis systems, speech recognition (historically HMM-based), robot·self-driving localization, weather prediction.
END: A Bayesian Network is a probabilistic cause-and-effect map, and HMMs·Kalman Filters are tools for tracking hidden states over time.

### What are Probability Models and Random Variables?
E: When rolling two dice, the list of 36 possible outcomes and how likely each is form a Probability Model, and a rule that attaches a number to each outcome, like "the sum of the two dice," is a Random Variable.
A: A Probability Model consists of the Sample Space Ω, the set of all possible outcomes; Events, its subsets; and a probability function P satisfying the axioms (Axiom: P ≥ 0, P(Ω) = 1, probabilities of mutually exclusive events add). A Random Variable is a function mapping each outcome in the Sample Space to a real number; if its values can be counted it's Discrete (the sum of dice), and if they're continuous it's Continuous (height).

### What is Conditional Probability?
E: "The chance it rains tomorrow" and "the chance it rains tomorrow given that the sky is full of dark clouds today" are different. When new information arrives, the world you consider narrows to the cases that fit that information.
A: Conditional Probability P(A | B) is the probability that A happens given that you know B happened, P(A | B) = P(A ∩ B) / P(B) (provided P(B) > 0). It's shrinking the Sample Space to B and looking at the fraction A takes up within it, and in general P(A | B) ≠ P(B | A).

### How do Independent and Mutually Exclusive differ?
A: Independent means that even if one event happens, the other's probability doesn't change, that is, P(A ∩ B) = P(A)P(B); Mutually Exclusive means the two events can't happen at the same time, that is, P(A ∩ B) = 0. If two events with probability greater than 0 are mutually exclusive, the moment one happens the other's probability becomes 0, so they're actually strongly Dependent (heads and tails on a coin).

### What are Bayes' Theorem and the Prior·Likelihood·Posterior?
E: A detective multiplies "how likely this person was to be the culprit to begin with" (Prior) by "how likely the culprit would leave footprints like these" (Likelihood) to update to "how likely this person is the culprit after seeing the footprints" (Posterior).
A: Bayes' Theorem is P(H | E) = P(E | H)P(H) / P(E), the rule for updating your belief in hypothesis H after seeing evidence E. P(H) is the Prior, the belief before seeing the evidence; P(E | H) is the Likelihood, how likely the evidence is if the hypothesis is true; P(H | E) is the Posterior, the belief after seeing the evidence; and P(E) is the Normalizing Constant that makes the probabilities add up to 1. It's the foundation of the Naive Bayes Classifier and of interpreting medical tests.

### Three friends all said "it's raining." If each tells the truth with probability 2/3, what's the probability it's actually raining?
E: It's unlikely all three are lying, but if it hardly ever rains in that area to begin with, "all lying" can't be ignored. So the answer is "you need the usual probability of rain (the Prior)."
A: Assuming the friends answer independently and calling the Prior p, P(all yes | rain) = (2/3)³ = 8/27 and P(all yes | no rain) = (1/3)³ = 1/27. By Bayes' Theorem, P(rain | all yes) = 8p / (8p + (1 − p)), which is 8/9 ≈ 88.9% for p = 0.5, about 72.7% for p = 0.25, and about 47.1% for p = 0.1. The key point is that even with the same evidence, the conclusion changes a lot depending on the Prior.

### Why can the probability of actually having a disease be low after a positive result on a 99% accurate test?
E: If only 10 in 10,000 people have the disease, then even if just 1% of the 9,990 healthy people wrongly test positive, that's about 100 people, far more than the 10 real patients.
A: By Bayes' Theorem, the Posterior is proportional not just to the Likelihood but also to the Prior (Base Rate). With a prevalence of 0.1%, a sensitivity of 99%, and a false positive rate of 1%, P(disease | positive) = 0.99×0.001 / (0.99×0.001 + 0.01×0.999) ≈ 9%. The rarer the disease, the more false positives outnumber true positives, and the error of ignoring the Base Rate and looking only at test accuracy is called the Base Rate Fallacy. That's why a retest is done to raise the Prior before judging again.

## Distribution Functions

#### PMF · PDF · CDF
ONE: Three ways to represent a probability distribution. The **PMF** gives the probability of each discrete value, the **PDF** gives the **density** near a continuous value, and the **CDF** gives the probability **accumulated up to some value**.
LIKE: Think of the heights of students in a class. The CDF is the fraction accumulated up to some value, like "the fraction of students 5'7" or shorter," and the PDF is the density, "how tightly packed students are around 5'7"."
STEP:
1. **PMF (Probability Mass Function)**: The actual probability of each value of a discrete variable. p(x) = P(X = x). For a die, each face is 1/6. They add up to 1.
2. **PDF (Probability Density Function)**: The density of a continuous variable. f(x) ≥ 0, and the total area (integral) is 1.
3. For continuous variables, **area is probability**. P(a ≤ X ≤ b) = the area under the PDF from a to b.
4. For continuous variables, **the probability of a single point is 0**. The probability that someone's height is exactly 67.000… inches is 0. You need an interval to get any area.
5. **PDF values can exceed 1**. That's because they're not probabilities but "probability per unit length." Example: a uniform distribution on [0, 0.5] needs a height of 2 for the area to be 1.
6. **CDF (Cumulative Distribution Function)**: F(x) = P(X ≤ x). It's probability accumulated from the left, so it starts at 0 and rises to 1 without ever decreasing.
7. **Relationship**: The PDF is the slope (derivative) of the CDF, and the CDF is the accumulation (integral) of the PDF. P(a ≤ X ≤ b) = F(b) − F(a).
END: Discrete uses the PMF (a probability per value), continuous uses the PDF (density, area is probability), and both use the CDF for "probability accumulated up to here."

### What are the CDF (Cumulative Distribution Function) and PDF (Probability Density Function)? With formulas.
E: In height data, the CDF is the fraction accumulated up to some value, like "the fraction of people 5'7" or shorter," and the PDF is the Density, "how tightly packed people are around 5'7"."
A: The CDF is F(x) = P(X ≤ x); it never decreases and goes to 0 as x → −∞ and to 1 as x → ∞. The PDF f(x) of a Continuous Random Variable is the derivative of the CDF, f(x) = F′(x), with f(x) ≥ 0, ∫ f(x)dx = 1, and P(a ≤ X ≤ b) = ∫ₐᵇ f(x)dx = F(b) − F(a). For Continuous variables, the probability of a single point P(X = a) is 0.

### How are the PMF and PDF different? Why can a PDF value exceed 1?
A: The PMF (Probability Mass Function) p(x) = P(X = x) is the actual probability of each Discrete value, so it's between 0 and 1 and the values add up to 1. The PDF is not a probability but probability per unit length (Density), and only area (the integral) is probability, so as long as the area is 1, the height can exceed 1. For example, the PDF of the Uniform Distribution on [0, 0.5] is 2 everywhere.

## Descriptive Statistics and Measuring Relationships

#### Mean and Median
ONE: The **Mean** is all the values added up and divided by how many there are, and the **Median** is **the middle value** when they're lined up. When there are extreme values, the Median shows a more "typical value."
LIKE: If a company where 9 employees each earn $50,000 hires a CEO who earns $10 million, the mean jumps above $1 million, but the median is still $50,000. Look only at the mean and you'd mistake it for "a company where everyone is rich."
STEP:
1. **Mean**: (sum of all values) ÷ count. It reflects every value.
2. But a single extreme value (**Outlier**) can swing it a lot.
3. **Median**: The middle value when sorted. With an even count, the average of the two middle values.
4. No matter how big an extreme value is, the middle position barely moves, so it holds up against Outliers (**Robust**).
5. **Mode**: The most frequent value. Useful for categorical data like clothing sizes.
6. **Reading the shape of a distribution**: If the Mean and Median are nearly the same, it's close to symmetric; if the Mean is much larger than the Median, it signals a long right tail (income, home prices).
USE: Income·home price statistics are usually reported as the Median. Response times are also viewed with the Median (p50) and p99 rather than the mean (topic 11).
END: Symmetric with no extreme values, use the Mean; skewed or with extreme values, use the Median.

#### Variance · Standard Deviation and n−1
ONE: The **Variance** measures **how spread out** values are from the mean, using squares, and the **Standard Deviation** is its square root. When computing variance from a Sample, you have to **divide by n−1** to be accurate.
LIKE: Two classes can have the same average score of 70, but one class might all be between 65 and 75 while the other mixes 30s and 100s. Variance puts that spread into a number.
STEP:
1. **Deviation**: Each value − the mean.
2. If you just add up the deviations, you always get 0 (the positives and negatives cancel). So you **square them** before adding.
3. **Variance**: The average of the squared deviations. Var(X) = E[(X − μ)²].
4. **Standard Deviation**: √Variance. It's back in the original units (points, inches), so it's easy to interpret.
5. **The problem with Sample Variance**: You don't know the true mean μ, so you use the Sample mean x̄. But x̄ is the value fitted closest to that very Sample, so the sum of squared deviations comes out **smaller** than it would with the true mean.
6. So you **divide by n − 1** instead of n to correct it upward slightly. Then, on average, it equals the true variance (an **Unbiased Estimator**, Bessel's Correction).
7. n − 1 is called the **Degrees of Freedom**. Once the mean is fixed, only n − 1 of the n values can be chosen freely.
END: Variance = the average squared distance from the mean, Standard Deviation = its square root. When computing from a Sample, divide by n−1 to correct.

#### Covariance and the Correlation Coefficient
ONE: **Covariance** is **the direction and degree to which two variables move together**, and the **Correlation Coefficient** turns that into **a score from −1 to 1** regardless of units.
LIKE: It's how much two values move together, like ice cream sales rising as the temperature rises. Covariance changes size depending on the units (°F, units sold), while the Correlation Coefficient is a score with those units removed.
STEP:
1. **Covariance**: Cov(X, Y) = E[(X − μₓ)(Y − μᵧ)]. If both are above their means or both below, the product is positive; if they move in opposite directions, it's negative.
2. Positive means they change together in the same direction; negative means in opposite directions. But switching from inches to feet changes the value by a factor of 12, so you can't tell "how strong the relationship is" from its size alone.
3. **Correlation Coefficient**: ρ = Cov(X, Y) / (σₓ × σᵧ). Dividing by the standard deviations removes the units.
4. 1 means a perfect positive linear relationship, −1 a perfect negative linear relationship, and 0 no linear relationship.
5. **Careful**: The correlation coefficient measures **only linear relationships**. A correlation coefficient of 0 does not mean "no relationship at all (independence)."
6. Example: If X is −2, −1, 0, 1, 2 and Y = X², Y is completely determined by X, yet the correlation coefficient is 0 (it's U-shaped, not a line).
END: Covariance is the direction of moving together, and Correlation is that standardized to a −1 to 1 score. Even at 0, there can be a curved relationship.

#### Correlation and Causation
ONE: **Correlation** only means two variables **change together**, while **Causation** means **changing one changes the other**. Correlation doesn't guarantee a cause.
LIKE: Ice cream sales and swimming accidents rise together. But ice cream doesn't cause accidents; both are due to a hidden cause, "hot weather."
STEP:
1. **Confounder**: A hidden cause that affects both variables at once. "Hot weather" in the example above.
2. **Reverse Causation**: Cause and effect are flipped. "People who go to the hospital often are sicker" isn't because hospitals make people sick; it's because sick people go to the hospital.
3. **Chance**: If you dig through enough data, you're bound to find pairs that look correlated purely by chance.
4. **How to confirm causation**: An experiment that **randomly** splits people into two groups and treats only one (an **RCT**; on the web, an **A/B Test**). Random assignment spreads hidden causes evenly across both groups.
5. When experiments are hard, statistical methods that control for Confounders (**Causal Inference**) are used.
WHY: If you see data saying "users who used this feature stay longer" and force the feature on everyone, it might turn out that those users were already enthusiasts, which is why they used the feature.
END: Moving together doesn't mean causing. To claim causation, you need a randomized experiment (A/B Test).

### When should you use the Mean, and when the Median?
E: If a company where 9 employees each earn $50,000 hires a CEO who earns $10 million, the mean jumps above $1 million, but the median is still $50,000.
A: The Mean reflects every value and is efficient for symmetric distributions without Outliers, but swings a lot with extreme values. The Median is the middle value when sorted, so it's Robust to Outliers, and is used to represent the "typical value" of Skewed distributions like income·home prices or data with Outliers. The fact that the two values differ a lot is itself a sign that the distribution is skewed.

### What are Variance and Standard Deviation? Why divide by n−1 instead of n for Sample Variance?
A: Variance is the average squared distance from the mean, Var(X) = E[(X − μ)²], and the Standard Deviation is its square root, which is in the same units as the original data and easy to interpret. Sample Variance uses the Sample Mean computed from the same data instead of the true mean μ, and since the Sample Mean is the value that minimizes the sum of squared deviations, dividing by n systematically underestimates the variance. Dividing by n−1 (the Degrees of Freedom) makes it an Unbiased Estimator of the population variance (Bessel's Correction).

### What are Covariance and the Correlation Coefficient? With formulas.
E: It's how much two values move together, like ice cream sales rising as the temperature rises. Covariance changes size depending on the units (°F, units sold), while the Correlation Coefficient is a score with those units removed.
A: Covariance is Cov(X, Y) = E[(X − μₓ)(Y − μᵧ)] = E[XY] − E[X]E[Y]; positive means they change together in the same direction and negative in opposite directions, but its size depends on the units. The Correlation Coefficient is ρ = Cov(X, Y) / (σₓσᵧ), between −1 and 1, and measures only the strength and direction of a linear relationship. So a correlation coefficient of 0 only means "no linear relationship," not Independence (e.g., if X is symmetric around 0 and Y = X², the correlation coefficient is 0 but they're completely dependent).

### Why are Correlation and Causation different?
E: Ice cream sales and swimming accidents rise together. But ice cream doesn't cause accidents; both are due to a hidden cause, "hot weather."
A: Correlation only means two variables change together, while Causation means changing one variable changes the other. Correlation can also arise from Confounders (hidden common causes), Reverse Causation, or chance, so claiming causation requires a randomized experiment (RCT, A/B Test) or Causal Inference methods that control for Confounders.

## Key Probability Distributions

#### Discrete Distributions: Bernoulli · Binomial · Categorical · Multinomial · Poisson
ONE: Distributions that deal with countable outcomes. **One success/failure (Bernoulli)** repeated many times gives **Binomial**, more than two outcomes gives **Categorical·Multinomial**, and the number of occurrences in a fixed time is **Poisson**.
LIKE: Tossing a coin once (Bernoulli), tossing a coin 10 times and counting heads (Binomial), rolling a die once (Categorical), rolling a die 10 times and counting how often each face came up (Multinomial), and the number of calls coming into a call center in an hour (Poisson).
STEP:
1. **Bernoulli(p)**: A single trial with success (1) probability p and failure (0) probability 1 − p.
2. **Binomial(n, p)**: The number of successes in n independent Bernoulli trials. Mean np, variance np(1 − p). Example: the number of questions you get right by guessing on 10 test questions.
3. **Categorical**: A single trial with K possible outcomes. It's the K-outcome version of Bernoulli. The output of a Classification model follows this distribution.
4. **Multinomial**: The counts of each outcome over n Categorical trials. It's the K-outcome version of Binomial.
5. **Poisson(λ)**: How many times events that occur independently at a constant rate happen in a fixed interval. **Both its mean and variance are λ**. Example: the number of calls coming in during 1 hour, the number of typos on a page.
6. A Binomial with large n and small p is almost the same as Poisson(np).
| Distribution | What it represents | Relationship |
| Bernoulli | One success/failure | A Binomial with 1 trial |
| Binomial | Number of successes in n trials | Sum of n Bernoullis |
| Categorical | One trial with one of K outcomes | The K-outcome version of Bernoulli |
| Multinomial | Counts of each outcome in n trials | The K-outcome version of Binomial |
| Poisson | Number of events in an interval | Approximation of a Binomial with large n, small p |
END: A single trial is Bernoulli·Categorical, counts over many trials are Binomial·Multinomial, and counts over time are Poisson.

#### The Normal Distribution
ONE: A **bell-shaped** distribution, symmetric around the mean. The result of adding up many small factors usually takes this shape, which makes it the most important distribution in statistics.
LIKE: Measure the heights of thousands of students and plot them, and you get a bell shape with the most people near the average height and very tall or very short people being rare.
STEP:
1. **N(μ, σ²)**: The mean μ sets the center, and the standard deviation σ sets the spread (the width of the bell).
2. **The 68-95-99.7 rule**: About 68% falls within mean ± 1σ, about 95% within ± 2σ, and about 99.7% within ± 3σ.
3. **Standard Normal N(0, 1)**: The normal distribution with mean 0 and standard deviation 1.
4. **Z-score**: z = (x − μ) / σ. It's "how many standard deviations away from the mean." Used to compare scores on different tests.
5. Why does it show up so often? Because of the **Central Limit Theorem**, which says adding up many independent values gives something close to a normal distribution regardless of the original shape (see Limit Theorems below).
6. The sum of independent normally distributed variables is also normally distributed.
KEY: Not all data is normally distributed. Plenty of data has a very long right tail, like income, city populations, and social media follower counts. Always check with a plot before assuming a normal distribution.
END: The normal distribution is a bell shape centered on the mean, with 68% within ±1σ and 95% within ±2σ.

#### Distributions for Statistical Testing: Chi-Square · t · F
ONE: Distributions made by **squaring and adding (Chi-Square)** or **dividing (t, F)** values drawn from a normal distribution. They're used in **statistical tests** that estimate variance or compare groups.
LIKE: If the normal distribution is the basic ingredient, Chi-Square·t·F are dishes made by squaring and dividing that ingredient. Each dish is used to answer a particular question (What's the variance? Is there a difference in means? How do variances compare?).
STEP:
1. **Chi-Square(k)**: The distribution of the sum of k Standard Normal values, each squared. Used for estimating variance and in goodness-of-fit tests that check "do the observed frequencies match the expected frequencies?"
2. **t(k)**: Standard Normal ÷ √(Chi-Square / k). It's the distribution that arises when you use a variance estimated from a Sample because the population variance is unknown.
3. The t distribution has **thicker tails** than the normal distribution (Heavy Tail). With a small Sample, the variance estimate is unstable, so it allows a greater chance of extreme values. As k (the degrees of freedom) grows, it becomes nearly identical to the normal distribution.
4. **t-Test**: Used to test whether two groups' means differ.
5. **F(d₁, d₂)**: (Chi-Square₁/d₁) ÷ (Chi-Square₂/d₂). The ratio of two variances.
6. **ANOVA**: Uses the F distribution to compare the means of three or more groups. And t² equals F(1, k).
END: Sums of squares are Chi-Square, testing means is t, and comparing variances and multiple groups is F.

#### Beta · Gamma · Exponential Distributions
ONE: **Beta** is a distribution of values between 0 and 1, that is, **"a distribution over a probability itself,"** **Exponential** is **the waiting time until the next event**, and **Gamma** is a distribution of positive values that generalizes it.
LIKE: Beta is a graph of my belief about "what's this coin's probability of heads, roughly?" Exponential is the time you wait at a call center until the next call comes in.
STEP:
1. **Beta(α, β)**: A distribution of values between 0 and 1. It represents beliefs about "probabilities" like click-through rates and conversion rates.
2. Beta(1, 1) is the uniform distribution on 0–1 (the state of knowing nothing).
3. **Conjugate Prior**: If you use Beta as the Prior and observe Bernoulli·Binomial data, the Posterior is also Beta. You just add 1 to α for each success and 1 to β for each failure, so updating is very easy (for K categories, the Dirichlet distribution).
4. **Exponential(λ)**: When events happen at an average of λ per unit time, the waiting time until the next event. The mean waiting time is 1/λ.
5. **Memoryless**: Even if you've already waited 10 minutes, the distribution of how much longer you'll wait is the same as at the start. "I've waited a long time, so it must be coming soon" doesn't hold.
6. **Gamma(k, θ)**: A distribution of positive values, such as the waiting time until the k-th event. Exponential is Gamma with k = 1, and Chi-Square(k) is also a special case, Gamma(k/2, 2).
USE: Beta is used in Bayesian analysis of A/B Tests and in exploration for recommendation systems (Thompson Sampling), and Exponential for modeling the intervals between server requests and between failures.
END: Beliefs about probabilities are Beta, waiting time until the next event is Exponential, and its general form is Gamma.

### What are the Bernoulli / Binomial / Categorical / Multinomial / Normal / t / Chi-Square / F / Beta / Gamma Distributions, and how are they related?
E: Toss a coin once (Bernoulli) many times and count the heads, and you get Binomial; roll a die instead of a coin, and you get Categorical and Multinomial. Add up a great many such counts and you get a bell shape (Normal), and squaring and adding or dividing values drawn from a Normal Distribution gives Chi-Square·t·F.
| Distribution | What it models | Relationship to other Distributions |
| Bernoulli(p) | One success/failure | A Binomial with 1 trial, a Categorical with 2 categories |
| Binomial(n, p) | Number of successes in n trials, mean np, variance np(1−p) | Sum of n independent Bernoullis, approximately Normal for large n |
| Categorical(p₁..pₖ) | One trial with one of K outcomes | The K-outcome version of Bernoulli |
| Multinomial(n, p₁..pₖ) | Counts of each category in n trials | The K-outcome version of Binomial |
| Normal N(μ, σ²) | Bell shape around the mean, measurement error | The limit in the Central Limit Theorem; sums of independent Normals are Normal |
| Chi-Square(k) | Sum of k squared Standard Normals | Variance estimation·goodness-of-fit tests, a special case of Gamma |
| t(k) | Standard Normal ÷ √(Chi-Square/k) | Testing means when the population variance is unknown, Heavy Tail, Normal as k→∞ |
| F(d₁, d₂) | (Chi-Square₁/d₁) ÷ (Chi-Square₂/d₂) | Comparing variances, ANOVA, t² is F(1, k) |
| Beta(α, β) | Values between 0 and 1, "a distribution over a probability" | Conjugate Prior of Bernoulli·Binomial, Beta(1,1) = Uniform |
| Gamma(k, θ) | Positive waiting times·sizes | Exponential is k = 1, Chi-Square(k) is Gamma(k/2, 2) |
A: Bernoulli is a single binary trial; collecting n of them gives Binomial, and extending to K outcomes gives Categorical and Multinomial. Adding up many independent values gives a Normal Distribution by the Central Limit Theorem; the sum of squared Standard Normals is Chi-Square, a Normal standardized by a Chi-Square is t, and the ratio of Chi-Squares is F. Beta is a distribution over Bernoulli's probability p itself and is used for Bayesian Updates (Dirichlet for Multinomial), and Gamma includes the Exponential Distribution and Chi-Square as special cases.

### When do you use the Poisson Distribution and the Exponential Distribution?
E: The number of calls coming into a call center in an hour is Poisson, and the time you wait until the next call comes in is Exponential.
A: Poisson(λ) models how many times events that occur independently at a constant rate happen in a fixed interval, and both its mean and variance are λ. A Binomial with large n and small p is close to Poisson(np). The Exponential Distribution is the waiting time between those events, with mean 1/λ, and has the Memoryless Property: no matter how long you've already waited, the distribution of the remaining wait is the same.

## Limit Theorems

#### The Law of Large Numbers
ONE: A theorem saying that **the more times** you repeat the same experiment, **the closer** the Sample mean gets **to the true mean**.
LIKE: Toss a coin 10 times and heads might come up 7 times, but toss it 10,000 times and the fraction of heads gets very close to 0.5.
STEP:
1. Draw Samples independently from the same distribution and take their mean.
2. With few Samples, the mean swings a lot by chance.
3. As the number of Samples grows, chance deviations cancel each other out, and the Sample mean approaches the true mean (Population Mean).
4. **Why casinos make money in the long run**: A customer might win any single game, but over millions of games, results converge to the average that favors the casino.
5. **Monte Carlo Simulation**: The method of estimating hard-to-compute values by averaging over a huge number of random simulations relies on this law.
KEY: "Heads has come up a lot lately, so tails is due next" is wrong (the gambler's fallacy). The law of large numbers doesn't "make up for" earlier results; it dilutes them among many trials.
END: The more you repeat, the closer the average gets to the true value. But it doesn't change the result of the next single trial.

#### The Central Limit Theorem
ONE: Whatever the shape of the original distribution, **the distribution of Sample means gets closer to a normal distribution as the Sample size grows**. That's why you can do statistical calculations with the normal distribution even without knowing the distribution.
LIKE: People's daily coffee spending has a strange shape, with lots of $0 and occasional big spikes. But if you repeatedly pick 100 people at random and take their average, those averages gather into a neat bell shape.
STEP:
1. The original distribution (Population) can be skewed; it just needs a finite variance.
2. Repeat many times: draw a Sample of size n and take its mean.
3. The distribution of those means gets closer to **a normal distribution with mean μ and variance σ²/n** as n grows.
4. **Standard Error**: The standard deviation of the Sample mean = σ/√n. Making the Sample 4 times larger cuts the error in half.
5. So even without knowing the Population's shape, you can compute Confidence Intervals and Hypothesis Tests using the normal distribution.
6. **Difference from the LLN**: The law of large numbers says the mean "gets closer" to the true value, and the central limit theorem says the wobble of that mean is "normally shaped."
USE: Deciding whether the difference in conversion rates between two buttons in an A/B Test is chance, and calculating the margin of error in opinion polls, are both thanks to this theorem.
END: Average anything and you get a bell shape. That's why the normal distribution becomes statistics' all-purpose tool.

### What is the Law of Large Numbers?
A: It's the theorem that the more Samples you draw independently from the same distribution, the closer the Sample Mean gets to the Population Mean. Toss a coin 10 times and the fraction of heads might be 0.7, but toss it 10,000 times and it gets very close to 0.5. It's why casinos make money in the long run and the reason Monte Carlo Simulation works.

### Why is the Central Limit Theorem useful?
E: An individual's daily coffee spending has a strange shape, with lots of $0 and occasional big spikes, but if you repeatedly pick 100 people at random and take their average, those averages gather into a neat bell shape.
A: The Central Limit Theorem says that for a distribution with finite variance, the mean of independently drawn Samples approaches a Normal Distribution with mean μ and variance σ²/n as n grows, regardless of the original distribution's shape. Thanks to it, even without knowing the Population's distribution, you can compute Confidence Intervals and Hypothesis Tests with the Normal Distribution using the Standard Error σ/√n, and most practical statistics, like comparing conversion rates in A/B Tests, rely on it.

## Estimation

#### Likelihood and Probability
ONE: It's the same formula seen from different viewpoints. **Probability** **fixes the parameter and asks how likely the data is**, while **Likelihood** **fixes the data and asks which parameter is more plausible**.
LIKE: Probability asks, "If this coin is fair, how likely is it to come up heads 7 times out of 10?" Likelihood asks, "It came up heads 7 times. Which explanation is more plausible: that this coin's probability of heads is 0.5, or that it's 0.7?"
STEP:
1. **Parameter θ**: A number that defines a distribution. Like a coin's probability of heads, p.
2. **Probability P(data | θ)**: Fix θ and weigh the probabilities of various possible data. Summed over all data, it's 1.
3. **Likelihood L(θ | data)**: The same formula, but you fix the data you've already observed and vary θ.
4. The Likelihood doesn't have to sum to 1 over θ. It's not a probability but "a score for comparing θ values."
5. Example: 7 heads out of 10. Comparing the Likelihood at p = 0.5 and p = 0.7, the one at 0.7 is larger. 0.7 explains the data better.
END: Probability is "given this parameter, what about the data?"; Likelihood is "given this data, which parameter is plausible?"

#### MLE and MAP
ONE: **MLE** picks the parameter that **best explains the data (maximizes the Likelihood)**, and **MAP** **also multiplies in prior knowledge (the Prior)** to pick the most plausible parameter.
LIKE: You tossed a coin 3 times and got heads all 3 times. MLE concludes, "100% heads!" MAP uses the prior knowledge that "coins are usually fair" and cautiously concludes something like "this coin seems to come up heads a bit more often."
STEP:
1. **MLE (Maximum Likelihood Estimation)**: Pick the θ that maximizes the Likelihood. With 7 heads out of 10 tosses, p̂ = 0.7.
2. In practice, you usually maximize the Log-Likelihood to turn products into sums.
3. With little data, MLE can reach extreme conclusions (3 out of 3 → 100%).
4. **MAP (Maximum A Posteriori)**: Pick the θ that maximizes the Posterior P(θ | data) ∝ P(data | θ) × P(θ). The Prior prevents extreme estimates.
5. If the Prior is uniform (no preference), MAP = MLE.
6. **Connection to Machine Learning**: MAP with a normal (Gaussian) Prior on the Weights is the same as **L2 Regularization**, and MAP with a Laplace Prior is the same as **L1 Regularization** (topic 14).
END: Trust only the data and it's MLE; also use prior knowledge and it's MAP. MAP's Prior is exactly Regularization.

#### Bayesian and Frequentist
ONE: **Frequentists** see probability as "**the frequency if the same experiment were repeated infinitely many times**," and **Bayesians** see probability as "**the degree of belief I hold right now**."
LIKE: A Frequentist interprets "70% chance of rain tomorrow" as "if today's weather repeated countless times, it would rain on 70% of those days," while a Bayesian interprets it as "given the information I have now, my belief that it will rain is 70%."
STEP:
1. **Frequentist**: A parameter (e.g., the true mean) is a single fixed value; we just don't know it. What's random is the data.
2. So they don't say "the probability that the parameter is in this interval." Instead they use p-values and Confidence Intervals.
3. **Bayesian**: Treats the parameter itself as uncertain (a Random Variable) and finds the Posterior by updating the Prior with the data.
4. So they can directly state a **Credible Interval**: "there's a 95% probability that the parameter is in this interval."
5. **Pros and cons of Bayesian**: It incorporates prior knowledge and expresses uncertainty naturally. But choosing the Prior is subjective and computation is heavy.
6. **Pros and cons of Frequentist**: The procedures are objective and clear. But the interpretation of results (p-values, confidence intervals) runs against intuition and is easy to misunderstand.
END: Frequentist is the frequency of repetition; Bayesian is the degree of belief. In practice, both are used depending on the situation.

#### The Bias and Variance of an Estimator
ONE: Estimation error splits into **how much it leans to one side on average (Bias)** and **how much it wobbles from Sample to Sample (Variance)**, and the total error is **MSE = Bias² + Variance**.
LIKE: When throwing darts, how much the darts cluster to one side of the bullseye is the Bias, and how scattered the darts are from each other is the Variance. Even if they lean slightly to one side, if they're tightly grouped they can actually be closer on average.
STEP:
1. **Estimator**: A formula that estimates a parameter from a Sample. Example: estimating the true mean with the Sample mean.
2. **Bias**: E[θ̂] − θ. If you drew countless Samples, estimated each time, and averaged, how far it would be from the true value. If 0, it's Unbiased.
3. **Variance**: How much the estimate wobbles when the Sample changes.
4. **MSE (Mean Squared Error)** = Bias² + Variance.
5. So allowing a little Bias in exchange for greatly reducing Variance can make the MSE smaller. That's exactly the principle behind Regularization.
6. This leads to Machine Learning's **Bias-Variance Trade-off**. Models that are too simple have high Bias, and models that are too complex have high Variance (topic 14).
END: Error = lean (Bias)² + wobble (Variance). Leaning a little but wobbling less can be more accurate.

### What is the difference between Likelihood and Probability?
E: Probability asks, "If this coin is fair, how likely is it to come up heads 7 times out of 10?", and Likelihood asks, "It came up heads 7 times. Which explanation is more plausible: that this coin's probability of heads is 0.5, or that it's 0.7?"
A: Probability P(data | θ) fixes the Parameter θ and weighs the possible data, so it sums to 1 over the data. Likelihood L(θ | data) is the same formula viewed as a function of θ with the observed data held fixed, so it doesn't need to sum or integrate to 1 over θ, and it's used as a score for comparing Parameters.

### What are MLE (Maximum Likelihood Estimation) and MAP (Maximum A Posteriori)?
A: MLE picks the θ that maximizes the Likelihood of the observed data; if a coin comes up heads 7 times out of 10, p̂ = 0.7. MAP picks the θ that maximizes the Posterior, the Likelihood multiplied by the Prior, P(θ | data) ∝ P(data | θ)P(θ), so prior knowledge prevents extreme estimates when there's little data, and if the Prior is Uniform it's the same as MLE. In Deep Learning, MAP with a Gaussian Prior corresponds to L2 Regularization, and a Laplace Prior to L1 Regularization.

### How do the Bayesian and Frequentist positions differ?
E: To a Frequentist, probability is "the frequency if the same experiment were repeated infinitely many times," and to a Bayesian, probability is "the degree of belief I hold right now," which gets revised as new evidence comes in.
A: Frequentists see a Parameter as a fixed unknown value and interpret probability as the long-run frequency of repeated experiments, using p-values·Confidence Intervals. Bayesians treat the Parameter itself as a Random Variable, update the Prior with data to get the Posterior, and can directly state a Credible Interval: "there's a 95% probability the Parameter is in this interval." The Bayesian approach incorporates prior knowledge and expresses uncertainty naturally, but choosing the Prior is subjective and computation is heavy; the Frequentist approach has clear, objective procedures, but the interpretation of results often runs against intuition.

### What are the Bias and Variance of an Estimator?
E: When throwing darts, how much the darts cluster to one side is the Bias, and how scattered they are from each other is the Variance.
A: The Bias of an Estimator θ̂ is E[θ̂] − θ, how far it is from the true value on average, and its Variance is how much the estimate wobbles when the Sample changes. The two errors combine as MSE = Bias² + Variance, so an Estimator that allows a little Bias in exchange for greatly reducing Variance (Regularization) can be more accurate, which leads to Machine Learning's Bias-Variance Trade-off.

## Hypothesis Tests and Interval Estimation

#### The Logic of Hypothesis Tests and the Two Kinds of Errors
ONE: A **Hypothesis Test** starts by assuming "**there's no difference (the Null Hypothesis)**," and discards that assumption if the data **fits it too poorly**. There are two kinds of errors that can happen along the way.
LIKE: In a trial, "the defendant is innocent" is the Null Hypothesis. A guilty verdict is given only when there's enough evidence. Convicting an innocent person is a Type I Error, and letting the real culprit go free is a Type II Error.
STEP:
1. **Null Hypothesis H₀**: The default claim that "there's no difference·effect." Example: "the new button and the old button have the same click-through rate."
2. **Alternative Hypothesis H₁**: The claim you want to prove. "The new button's click-through rate is different."
3. Assume H₀ is true, and compute how rare the current data is (the p-value).
4. If it's rare enough (smaller than the threshold α), reject H₀ and accept H₁. Otherwise, say "there isn't enough evidence to reject H₀" (that doesn't prove H₀ is true).
5. **Type I Error (False Positive)**: Concluding there's a difference when there actually isn't. Its probability is capped by the **Significance Level α** (usually 0.05).
6. **Type II Error (False Negative)**: Failing to find a difference that actually exists. Its probability is β.
7. With a fixed Sample size, lowering α (being stricter) makes β larger. To reduce both, you have to increase the Sample.
END: Assume "no difference," and reject it if the data is too rare. Claiming something that isn't there is Type I; missing something that is there is Type II.

#### Understanding the p-value Correctly
ONE: The **p-value** is "**if the Null Hypothesis were true**, the probability of getting a result as extreme as this one or more so." The smaller it is, the worse the data fits the Null Hypothesis.
LIKE: A friend says a coin is fair, but you toss it 10 times and get heads 9 or more times. Computing "how rare would this be if the coin really were fair?" gives 11/1024 ≈ 1.1%. Something that rare makes you doubt the claim that "it's fair." This 1.1% is the p-value.
STEP:
1. Assume the Null Hypothesis is true (the coin is fair).
2. Under that assumption, compute the probability of a result as extreme as the current one (9 or more heads). 9 heads: 10 ways, 10 heads: 1 way, total 11/1024.
3. If the p-value is smaller than the threshold α chosen in advance (usually 0.05), reject the Null Hypothesis.
4. **Common misconception ①**: "p = 0.03 means there's a 3% probability the Null Hypothesis is true" → Wrong. The p-value is computed **assuming** the Null Hypothesis.
5. **Common misconception ②**: "A small p-value means a large effect" → Wrong. With a huge Sample, even a tiny difference gives a small p-value.
6. That's why you always look at the **Effect Size** and the **Confidence Interval** along with the p-value.
END: p-value = "if the Null Hypothesis is right, how rare is this result?" It's neither the probability that the Null Hypothesis is true nor the size of the effect.

#### Confidence Interval
ONE: A **95% Confidence Interval** means "if you repeated the process of building intervals this way, **95% of them would contain the true value**." Saying "there's a 95% probability the true value is in" a single interval you've already built is strictly wrong.
LIKE: Imagine playing ring toss blindfolded. "Throwing this way, 95 out of 100 rings land on the peg" describes how good the throwing method is. A single ring that's already been thrown and landed either caught the peg or didn't.
STEP:
1. Compute an estimate (e.g., the mean) from the Sample, and attach a margin of error around it to build an interval.
2. A 95% interval for the mean is roughly **Sample mean ± 1.96 × Standard Error** (1.96 is the value that covers the middle 95% of a normal distribution).
3. **Correct interpretation**: If you repeated drawing a Sample and building an interval the same way 100 times, about 95 of the intervals would contain the true value.
4. From the Frequentist viewpoint, the true value is a fixed constant, so a particular interval you've already computed either contains it or doesn't.
5. If you want to say "there's a 95% probability the true value is in this interval," use the Bayesian **Credible Interval**.
6. The narrower the interval, the more precise the estimate. Increasing the Sample narrows the interval.
KEY: The "margin of error ±3 percentage points at a 95% confidence level" in opinion polls is exactly this concept.
END: The 95% in a confidence interval is "the method's success rate," not the probability for one interval you've already built.

#### Statistical Power and Sample Size
ONE: **Power** is **the probability of detecting an effect when there really is one** (1 − β), and you need to calculate in advance **how big a Sample you need** to get the Power you want.
LIKE: It's the probability that a metal detector beeps when there really is a coin in the ground. The bigger the coin and the longer you hold the detector over it, the more reliably it beeps.
STEP:
1. **Power = 1 − β**: The probability of correctly rejecting the Null Hypothesis when the Alternative Hypothesis is true. The usual target is **0.8 or higher**.
2. **What raises Power**: A larger effect, a larger Sample, smaller variance in the data, and a larger α (being less strict).
3. **Sample Size calculation**: Decide α (usually 0.05), the target Power (usually 0.8), the smallest effect size δ you want to detect, and the data's standard deviation σ, then work backward.
4. For comparing the means of two groups, n per group ≈ 2(1.96 + 0.84)² × σ²/δ² ≈ 15.7 × σ²/δ².
5. **An important intuition**: To detect an effect **half as large**, you need **4 times** the Sample (because n is inversely proportional to δ²).
6. Estimating a proportion to within ±3 percentage points takes about 1,068 people (which is why opinion polls survey around 1,000).
WHY: With too small a Sample, you miss the effect even when it's real and wrongly conclude "no effect." It has to be calculated before the experiment.
END: Power is "the probability of finding an effect that's there," and detecting a small effect takes a Sample that grows with the square.

#### A/B Tests and the Multiple Comparisons Problem
ONE: An **A/B Test** is an experiment that splits users **randomly** into two groups, shows them something that differs in just one way, and compares the results. When you run many tests, watch out for the multiple comparisons problem, where **chance "significant" results pile up**.
LIKE: If you toss each of 20 coins 10 times, even if they're all fair, one of them will probably show "suspiciously many heads" just by chance. You shouldn't look at that and declare, "This coin is rigged!"
STEP:
1. **Decide the hypothesis and key metric in advance**: Example: "the new checkout button increases the purchase conversion rate."
2. **Random assignment**: Split users randomly into A (existing) and B (new). Hidden differences spread evenly across both sides.
3. **Calculate the Sample Size and duration in advance**. To avoid day-of-week effects, tests usually run in whole weeks.
4. **No Peeking**: If you keep checking results midway and stop the moment they become significant, you're effectively picking a moment when they became significant by chance, which greatly increases False Positives.
5. **The multiple comparisons problem**: If you run 20 independent tests at α = 0.05, the probability that at least one is significant by chance is 1 − 0.95²⁰ ≈ **64%**.
6. **Corrections**: Bonferroni (divide α by the number of tests m, strict), Holm (a less strict step-by-step method), Benjamini-Hochberg (controls the FDR, the fraction of false results among significant ones).
END: An A/B Test is random assignment + planning in advance + no peeking. When testing many things, correct the threshold.

### What are the Null Hypothesis and Alternative Hypothesis, and Type I Error and Type II Error?
E: In a trial, "the defendant is innocent" is the Null Hypothesis. A guilty verdict is given only when there's enough evidence. Convicting an innocent person is a Type I Error, and letting the real culprit go free is a Type II Error.
A: The Null Hypothesis H₀ is the default claim that "there's no difference·effect," and the Alternative Hypothesis H₁ is the claim you want to prove. A Type I Error is rejecting a true H₀ (a False Positive), and its probability is capped by the Significance Level α; a Type II Error is failing to reject a false H₀ (a False Negative), and its probability is β. With a fixed Sample size, there's a Trade-off: lowering α makes β larger.

### How would you explain the p-value to someone who doesn't know it?
E: A friend says a coin is fair, but you toss it 10 times and get heads 9 or more times. Computing "how rare would this be if the coin really were fair?" gives 11/1024 ≈ 1.1%. Something that rare makes you doubt the claim that "it's fair." This 1.1% is the p-value (One-Sided).
A: The p-value is the probability, assuming the Null Hypothesis is true, of getting a result as extreme as the one observed or more so. If the p-value is smaller than the Significance Level (usually 0.05), the Null Hypothesis is rejected. Be careful: the p-value is neither "the probability that the Null Hypothesis is true" nor the Effect Size, and with a very large Sample even a tiny difference becomes significant, so you look at the Effect Size and Confidence Interval together.

### What is the definition of a Confidence Interval? Is it correct to say "there's a 95% probability the true value is inside"?
E: Imagine playing ring toss blindfolded. "Throwing this way, 95 out of 100 rings land on the peg" describes how good the throwing method is. A single ring that's already been thrown and landed either caught the peg or didn't.
A: A 95% Confidence Interval means a procedure such that, if you repeated drawing a Sample and building an interval the same way, 95% of those intervals would contain the true value. From the Frequentist viewpoint, the true value is a fixed constant, so saying "there's a 95% probability the true value is inside" a particular interval you've already computed is strictly a wrong interpretation (that interval either contains the true value or doesn't). If you want that kind of probabilistic interpretation, use a Bayesian Credible Interval.

### What is Statistical Power?
E: It's the probability that a metal detector beeps when there really is a coin in the ground. The bigger the coin and the longer you hold the detector over it, the more reliably it beeps.
A: Statistical Power is 1 − β, the probability of correctly rejecting the Null Hypothesis when the Alternative Hypothesis is true, that is, the ability to detect a real effect. It grows with a larger Effect Size, a larger Sample, smaller variance in the data, and a larger α, and experiments are usually designed to target 0.8 or higher.

### How do you calculate the required Sample Size?
A: Decide the Significance Level α, the target Power 1 − β, the smallest Effect Size δ you want to detect, and the data's Standard Deviation σ, then work backward. For comparing the means of two groups, n per group ≈ 2(z₁₋α/₂ + z₁₋β)²σ²/δ², which is about 15.7σ²/δ² for α = 0.05 (1.96) and Power 0.8 (0.84). For estimating a proportion to within ±E, n = z²p(1−p)/E², which is about 1,068 people for p = 0.5 and E = 3 percentage points. To detect an effect half as large, you need 4 times the Sample.

### How do you design an A/B Test? What is the Multiple Comparison problem?
A: Decide the hypothesis and key Metric (e.g., purchase conversion rate) in advance, assign users randomly to A·B (Randomization), and calculate the Sample Size and duration ahead of time so you don't peek at results midway (Peeking) and stop. The Multiple Comparison problem is that running many tests increases chance significant results; if you run 20 independent tests at α = 0.05, the probability that at least one is a False Positive is 1 − 0.95²⁰ ≈ 64%. Bonferroni (α/m), Holm, and Benjamini-Hochberg (FDR) corrections guard against it.

## Statistical Interpretation of Regression

#### R² and Adjusted R²
ONE: **R²** is **the fraction** of the total variation in the outcome **that the model explains**. Adding a variable never lowers R², even if it's useless, so models are compared with **Adjusted R²**, which penalizes the number of variables.
LIKE: Students' scores vary, and R² is what percent of those differences can be explained by "hours studied." 70% means 70% of the differences in scores are explained by hours studied.
STEP:
1. **SS_tot**: How spread out the actual values are from their mean (total variation).
2. **SS_res**: The differences between the model's predictions and the actual values (remaining error).
3. **R² = 1 − SS_res / SS_tot**. A perfect model gives 1, and one no better than just guessing the mean gives 0.
4. When OLS with an intercept is evaluated on its training data, it's between 0 and 1.
5. **The problem**: Add one variable, even if it's random noise, and R² won't go down (it gives a little more room to fit).
6. **Adjusted R²** = 1 − (1 − R²)(n − 1)/(n − p − 1). It applies a penalty as the number of variables p grows. Adding a useless variable actually lowers it.
KEY: A high R² doesn't guarantee causation or good predictive performance. Performance on new data is checked separately with a Test Set or Cross-Validation (topic 14).
END: R² is "the fraction explained," and Adjusted R² is a fair comparison score that also accounts for the number of variables.

### What does R² mean? Why is Adjusted R² needed?
E: Students' scores vary, and R² is what percent of those differences can be explained by "hours studied."
A: R² = 1 − SS_res / SS_tot, the fraction of the total variation in the Target y that the model explains, and it's between 0 and 1 on the training data of OLS with an Intercept. But adding a variable never lowers R², even a useless one, so models are compared with Adjusted R² = 1 − (1 − R²)(n − 1)/(n − p − 1), which penalizes the number of variables p. A high R² doesn't guarantee Causation or good predictive performance.

## Sampling and Resampling

#### Sampling: Guessing the Whole from a Part
ONE: **Sampling** is **drawing a part** of the Population (the whole) to guess about the whole. Draw well, and even a small number lets you guess the whole accurately.
LIKE: When you taste soup, you don't drink the whole pot; you take one spoonful. But you have to stir it well first for that spoonful to represent the whole pot.
STEP:
1. **Population**: The whole you want to learn about. Example: all U.S. voters.
2. **Sample**: The part you actually survey. Example: 1,000 people.
3. **Simple Random Sampling**: Draw so that everyone has the same chance of being picked. It's like stirring the soup well before taking a spoonful.
4. **Stratified Sampling**: Split into groups (Strata) in advance, like gender·region·age, and draw from each group in proportion. Even small groups are included without fail, so the estimate is more stable.
5. **Bad Sampling**: Surveying only online leaves out people who don't use the internet (**Selection Bias**). Representativeness matters more than size.
USE: In Machine Learning, a **Stratified Split** that preserves Class proportions when splitting Train/Test follows the same principle. If only 1% of transactions are fraudulent and you split randomly, the Test set might have almost no fraudulent transactions.
END: Draw after mixing well (random) or by splitting into groups (stratified), and even a small Sample represents the whole.

#### Resampling and the Bootstrap
ONE: **Resampling** is drawing **again and again from a Sample you already have** to see how much an estimate wobbles, and the best-known example is the **Bootstrap, which rebuilds the Sample thousands of times by drawing with replacement**.
LIKE: It's taking one spoonful of soup, then tasting that spoonful in several portions to see "how much my judgment wobbles."
STEP:
1. You can't actually draw a fresh Sample thousands of times. It's expensive and slow.
2. **The Bootstrap idea**: Treat the Sample you have as a miniature of the Population, and build new Samples of the same size from it by **drawing with replacement** (putting back what you drew and drawing again).
3. Repeat this thousands of times, and compute the statistic you want (mean, Median, model performance, etc.) on each one.
4. Estimate the **Standard Error and Confidence Interval** from the spread of those thousands of values.
5. Because it draws with replacement, a single Bootstrap Sample contains only about **63.2%** (1 − 1/e) of the original data. The rest that's left out (**OOB, Out-of-Bag**) can be used for validation.
6. Other Resampling methods: Jackknife (compute leaving one out at a time), Permutation Test (shuffle the labels and compare with chance), Cross-Validation (topic 14).
PRO: It makes few assumptions about the distribution, and even for statistics whose variance is hard to derive with formulas, like the Median or complex model performance, computation reveals the uncertainty.
USE: **Bagging** in Random Forests is also a method that trains one Tree on each Bootstrap Sample (topic 14).
END: The Bootstrap measures how much an estimate wobbles by repeatedly "drawing again from the Sample you have."

### What are Sampling and Resampling, and what are the advantages of Resampling?
E: When you taste soup, taking one spoonful is Sampling, and tasting that spoonful in several portions to see "how much my judgment wobbles" is Resampling.
A: Sampling is drawing a part of the Population, and Resampling is drawing Samples again and again from a Sample you already have, including the Bootstrap, Jackknife, Permutation Test, and Cross-Validation. Its advantages are that it makes few assumptions about the distribution and can estimate, through computation, the uncertainty of statistics whose variance is hard to derive with formulas (the Median, model performance).

### What does the Bootstrap mean in statistics?
A: The Bootstrap builds thousands of new Samples of size n from a Sample of size n by Sampling with Replacement, computes the statistic on each, and estimates the Standard Error and Confidence Interval from that distribution. The idea is "treat the Sample as a miniature of the Population and draw from it again"; a single Bootstrap Sample contains only about 63.2% (1 − 1/e) of the original data, so the rest (OOB, Out-of-Bag) can be used for validation. Bagging in Random Forests also uses this method.

### What is Stratified Sampling?
A: It's a method that splits the Population into Strata such as gender·region·Class and then draws from each stratum in proportion, so even small groups are included without fail and the variance of the estimate shrinks. In Machine Learning, a Stratified Split that preserves Class proportions when splitting Train/Test follows the same principle.

## Parametric·Non-Parametric Methods

#### Parametric vs Non-Parametric
ONE: **Parametric** methods **assume the data follows a specific distribution (e.g., the normal distribution)** and estimate a few parameters. **Non-Parametric** methods **make no such assumption, or use ranks (Rank)**.
LIKE: Parametric methods are off-the-rack clothes: perfect if they fit your build, awkward if they don't. Non-Parametric methods are stretchy clothes: they roughly fit any build but never quite feel tailored.
STEP:
1. **Parametric**: Under an assumption like "the data is normally distributed," estimate only a few parameters like the mean·variance. Examples: t-Test, ANOVA, Linear Regression.
2. If the assumption holds, Power is high (it finds differences well) and interpretation is clear.
3. If the assumption is wrong, the results can be wrong.
4. **Non-Parametric**: Assumes nothing about the shape of the distribution. It often uses **ranks** instead of values, so it holds up against Outliers. Examples: Mann-Whitney U (comparing two groups), Wilcoxon (paired comparison), Kruskal-Wallis (comparing several groups).
5. **When to go Non-Parametric?**: When the Sample is small and normality is hard to check, when there are many Outliers, or when only the order of the values is meaningful (satisfaction on a 1–5 scale).
6. **The meaning in Machine Learning**: Models like KNN and Decision Trees, whose complexity grows as the data grows, are called Non-Parametric Models. Models with a fixed number of Weights, like Linear Regression, are Parametric.
END: If the assumption holds, Parametric (powerful); if the assumption is uncertain, Non-Parametric (sturdy).

#### Building a Prediction Model with Very Little Data
ONE: With only a few dozen data points, a complex model **memorizes them right away (Overfitting)**. So the approach is **a simple model + prior knowledge + careful evaluation**.
LIKE: If you study for a test by looking at only 5 practice questions, you might memorize the answers to those 5 without understanding the principles. In that case, you should study the textbook (prior knowledge) alongside them and focus on simple principles.
STEP:
1. **A simple model**: Use a model with few Weights, like Linear·Logistic Regression with Regularization.
2. **Few Features**: Use domain knowledge to pick just a few Features that really matter.
3. **Use prior knowledge**: Put in a Prior with a Bayesian Model, or use **Transfer Learning**, which brings in a model pretrained on a similar problem.
4. **Data Augmentation**: For images, increase the data by flipping·rotating.
5. **Careful evaluation**: With so little data that setting aside a separate Test Set is wasteful, use **LOOCV (Leave-One-Out)**, which validates by leaving one out at a time, or repeated k-Fold.
6. Report results with an **uncertainty range** from the Bootstrap rather than a single number.
END: With little data, keep it simple, borrow prior knowledge, and state the uncertainty too.

### When do you use a Parametric Method, and when a Non-Parametric Method?
E: Parametric methods are off-the-rack clothes: perfect if they fit your build, awkward if they don't. Non-Parametric methods are stretchy clothes: they roughly fit any build but never quite feel tailored.
A: A Parametric Method assumes the data follows a specific form like the Normal Distribution and estimates a few Parameters, so if the assumption holds, Power and interpretability are high (t-Test, ANOVA, Linear Regression). A Non-Parametric Method makes no assumption about the shape of the distribution or uses Ranks, and is used when the Sample is small and normality is hard to check, when there are many Outliers, or when the scale is Ordinal (Mann-Whitney U, Wilcoxon, Kruskal-Wallis). In Machine Learning, models like KNN·Decision Trees whose complexity grows with the data are called Non-Parametric Models.

### How would you build a prediction model when there's very little data (a few dozen points or fewer)?
A: A complex model overfits right away, so use a simple model like Regularized Linear·Logistic Regression and a few Features chosen with domain knowledge. A Bayesian Model that incorporates prior knowledge, Transfer Learning that brings in a pretrained model, and Data Augmentation help; evaluate with LOOCV (Leave-One-Out) or repeated k-Fold, and report the uncertainty from the Bootstrap alongside the results.

## Data Quality

#### Handling Missing Values
ONE: How to handle empty values depends on **why they're empty**. First check whether they went missing at random (MCAR), in a way related to other information (MAR), or because of the value itself (MNAR).
LIKE: A survey that got rained on so random boxes smeared (MCAR), older respondents leaving the online-related questions blank more often (MAR, since age is known), and higher earners being more likely to leave the income box blank (MNAR) all call for different responses.
STEP:
1. **MCAR (Missing Completely At Random)**: Missing completely at random. Deleting those Rows introduces no Bias; it just shrinks the Sample.
2. **MAR (Missing At Random)**: Missing depending on other observed variables. **Model-Based Imputation**, which predicts the values from other variables, or **Multiple Imputation**, which fills them in several times to reflect the uncertainty too, is appropriate.
3. **MNAR (Missing Not At Random)**: The reason it's missing is the value itself. Simply filling it in leaves Bias. Add the fact that "it was empty" as a variable (**Missing Indicator**), or model it with domain knowledge.
4. **Mean Imputation (filling with the mean)**: Simple, but it shrinks the variance and distorts the relationships between variables.
5. Some models, like XGBoost, can handle missing values directly.
KEY: "Being empty" is often information in itself. If people who left the income box blank show a particular tendency, that helps prediction.
END: First look at why it's empty (MCAR·MAR·MNAR), then choose deleting·filling·flagging accordingly.

#### Judging Outliers
ONE: An Outlier is a value **far away from the others**. Find them with a rule (IQR, z-score), but judging with domain knowledge **whether it's an input error or a genuinely rare value** matters more.
LIKE: If you survey students' heights and find "670 inches," that's a typo. But "7 feet" might really be a tall basketball player. Both are far from the rest, but they need to be handled differently.
STEP:
1. **The IQR method (Box Plot)**: When you split the data into quarters, the distance between Q1 (25%) and Q3 (75%) is the IQR. Values smaller than Q1 − 1.5×IQR or larger than Q3 + 1.5×IQR are treated as Outliers.
2. **The z-score method**: If the data is close to normal, values with |z| > 3 (3 or more standard deviations from the mean) are treated as Outliers.
3. **Looking at several variables together**: Sometimes each value is normal but the combination is odd (4'11" tall and 265 lb). Methods like Mahalanobis Distance, Isolation Forest, and LOF are used.
4. **Input errors**: Fix them or delete them.
5. **Genuinely rare values**: These might be exactly what you need to find, like fraudulent transactions. Don't delete them carelessly.
6. **Alternatives**: Rather than deleting, use Outlier-resistant statistics like the Median, or reduce their influence with a Log transformation.
END: Find them by rule, judge them by domain. Fix errors, keep genuinely rare values, and reduce their influence with Robust statistics or transformations.

#### Controlling Bias
ONE: **Systematic skew (Bias)** that distorts the conclusions of a data analysis comes from how the Sample is drawn, hidden causes, and how things are measured. Each kind is prevented differently.
LIKE: If you look only at the bullet holes on planes that came back from war and say "let's reinforce where the bullets are," you're wrong. The planes hit in the truly fatal spots never came back. That's Survivorship Bias.
STEP:
1. **Selection Bias**: The Sample doesn't represent the whole. Reduced with random·stratified sampling and Weighting.
2. **Confounding**: A hidden cause distorts the result. Reduced with randomized experiments, putting the Confounder into the model, and pairing similar subjects (Matching).
3. **Measurement / Observer Bias**: The measurement method or the observer's expectations affect the result. Reduced with standardized measurement and **Double-Blind** designs.
4. **Survivorship Bias**: Drawing conclusions from only the data that survived. Looking only at successful startups and saying "do this and you'll succeed" also falls here.
END: How you draw (Selection), hidden causes (Confounding), how you measure (Measurement), and looking only at what survived (Survivorship). Always suspect these four Biases.

#### When a Log Transformation Is Useful
ONE: Log turns **multiplication into addition and a large range into a small one**. It makes skewed data closer to symmetric and lets you compute very small probabilities safely.
LIKE: Earthquake magnitude (the Richter scale) and loudness (decibels) use Log. Even when the actual energy differs by thousands or tens of thousands of times, it makes the numbers easy to handle, like 3, 4, 5.
STEP:
1. **Straightening skewed data**: Applying Log to data with a long right tail, like income·home prices, brings it closer to a bell shape, so you can use methods that assume normality.
2. **Compressing a wide range**: If values span from 10 to 100 million, everything gets buried under the large values. Applying Log (base 10) compresses them to about 1–8.
3. **Turning multiplicative relationships into additive ones**: log(a × b) = log a + log b. You can handle multiplicative relationships with a Linear Model, and coefficients are interpreted as "percent change."
4. **Preventing Underflow**: Multiplying small probabilities hundreds of times becomes 0 on a computer. **Adding** Log probabilities is safe.
5. **Log-Likelihood**: Products become sums, so it's easy to differentiate. MLE and Cross-Entropy Loss both use Log.
KEY: You can't take the Log of 0 or negative numbers. For data with zeros, use log(1 + x).
END: Log straightens skew, compresses ranges, turns products into sums, and computes small probabilities safely.

### Should you fill in Missing Values? (MCAR·MAR·MNAR)
E: A survey that got rained on so random boxes smeared at random (MCAR), older respondents leaving the online questions blank more often (MAR, since age is known), and higher earners being more likely to leave the income box blank (MNAR) all call for different responses.
A: First look at the cause of the missingness. If it's MCAR (Missing Completely At Random), deleting introduces no Bias and just shrinks the Sample; if it's MAR (Missing At Random, missing depending on other observed variables), Model-Based Imputation, which predicts from other variables, or Multiple Imputation is appropriate; and if it's MNAR (Missing Not At Random, missingness depends on the value itself), simple Imputation leaves Bias, so you add whether it's missing as a variable (Missing Indicator) or model it with domain knowledge. Mean Imputation is simple but shrinks the variance and distorts relationships, and some models, like XGBoost, handle missing values directly.

### What are the criteria for judging an Outlier?
A: For a Univariate case, values smaller than Q1 − 1.5×IQR or larger than Q3 + 1.5×IQR (Box Plot), or values with |z| > 3 if the data is close to Normal, are commonly treated as Outliers. For a Multivariate case, methods like Mahalanobis Distance, Isolation Forest, and LOF are used. More important than the rule is judging by domain whether it's an input error or a genuinely rare value (something you actually need to find, like a Fraud transaction), and rather than deleting blindly, consider Robust Statistics like the Median or a Transformation.

### How do you control Bias?
A: Selection Bias, where the Sample doesn't represent the Population, is reduced with random·stratified sampling and Weighting; Confounding, where a hidden cause distorts the result, with randomized experiments, putting the Confounder into the model, or Matching; and Measurement/Observer Bias with standardized measurement and Double-Blind designs. Always suspect Survivorship Bias too, which looks only at the data that survived.

### When is the Log function useful?
A: It makes Right-Skewed data like income·home prices closer to symmetric, compresses the range when values span from tens to hundreds of millions, and turns multiplicative relationships into additive ones so they can be handled with a Linear Model (coefficients interpreted as "percent change"). Also, multiplying small probabilities countless times gets treated as 0 on a computer (Underflow), so Log Probabilities are added, and the Log-Likelihood turns products into sums, making differentiation easy. For data with zeros, use log(1 + x).

## Information Theory

#### Entropy and Information Gain
ONE: **Entropy** measures **how hard an outcome is to predict (uncertainty)**, and **Information Gain** is **how much the uncertainty dropped** after asking some question.
LIKE: If tomorrow's weather is almost always sunny, like in a desert, there's nothing to be surprised about (low Entropy). If sunny and rainy are 50/50, it's the hardest to predict (high Entropy). If asking some question cut the uncertainty a lot, it was a good question, and that reduction is the Information Gain.
STEP:
1. **Information content**: The rarer an event, the more surprising it is and the more information it carries. Information content of an event = −log₂ p(x).
2. **Entropy H(X)** = −Σ p(x) log₂ p(x). It's the information content of every outcome, averaged with the probabilities as weights.
3. If the outcome is certain (one outcome has probability 1), the Entropy is 0.
4. Entropy is largest when every outcome is equally likely (Uniform). A fair coin is 1 Bit.
5. **Information Gain**: When splitting data by some Feature, the Entropy before the split minus the weighted average of each group's Entropy after the split.
6. A **Decision Tree** splits the data by picking the question (Feature) with the largest Information Gain first. It's like asking the question that rules out the most possibilities first in Twenty Questions (topic 14).
USE: Decision Tree training, the theoretical limit of data compression (you can't compress shorter than the Entropy), Feature selection.
END: Entropy = uncertainty, Information Gain = uncertainty reduced by a question. A good question has a large Gain.

#### Cross-Entropy and KL Divergence
ONE: **Cross-Entropy** is **the average cost of representing the true distribution p with a predicted distribution q**, and **KL Divergence** is **the extra cost caused by predicting wrong**. Cross-Entropy is used as the Loss for Classification models.
LIKE: If, instead of a codebook tailored to the actual weather distribution p, you use a codebook tailored to a wrongly predicted distribution q, messages get longer on average. That average length is the Cross-Entropy, and the amount it grew is the KL Divergence.
STEP:
1. **Cross-Entropy** H(p, q) = −Σ p(x) log q(x).
2. **KL Divergence** D_KL(p‖q) = Σ p(x) log(p(x)/q(x)) = H(p, q) − H(p).
3. KL is always 0 or more, and 0 only when p = q. It's also asymmetric (D_KL(p‖q) ≠ D_KL(q‖p)), so it's not a distance in the strict sense.
4. **In Classification**: p is the answer (One-Hot, with 1 only for the correct Class), and q is the model's predicted probability. H(p) is fixed because it's the answer, so reducing the Cross-Entropy is the same as reducing the KL.
5. With One-Hot, the Loss is **−log q(correct Class)**. Giving the correct answer probability 0.9 makes the Loss about 0.1, and giving it 0.01 makes it about 4.6.
6. So the **more confident a wrong answer, the bigger the penalty**. And this is the same as the Negative Log-Likelihood, so minimizing Cross-Entropy is MLE.
END: Cross-Entropy is "the cost of explaining the answer with the predicted distribution," and since it's −log of the probability on the correct answer, it heavily penalizes confident wrong answers.

### Please explain Entropy and Information Gain.
E: If tomorrow's weather is almost always sunny, like in a desert, there's nothing to be surprised about (low Entropy), and if sunny and rainy are 50/50, it's the hardest to predict (high Entropy). If asking some question cut the uncertainty a lot, it was a good question, and that reduction is the Information Gain.
A: Entropy H(X) = −Σ p(x) log₂ p(x) is the average uncertainty (average information content) of a Random Variable; it's 0 if the outcome is certain, largest for a Uniform Distribution, and 1 Bit for a fair coin. Information Gain is, when splitting data by some Feature, the Parent Node's Entropy minus the weighted average of the Child Nodes' Entropy, and Decision Trees (ID3·C4.5) Split on the Feature where this value is largest.

### What are Cross-Entropy and KL Divergence? Why are they used as Classification Loss Functions?
E: If you use a codebook tailored not to the actual weather distribution p but to a wrongly predicted distribution q, messages get longer. That average length is the Cross-Entropy, and the amount it grew is the KL Divergence.
A: Cross-Entropy is H(p, q) = −Σ p(x) log q(x), and KL Divergence is D_KL(p‖q) = Σ p(x) log(p(x)/q(x)) = H(p, q) − H(p), which is always 0 or more, 0 only when p = q, and asymmetric, so it's not a distance (Metric). In Classification, p is the answer (One-Hot), q is the model's predicted probability, and H(p) is fixed, so minimizing Cross-Entropy minimizes the KL Divergence. With One-Hot, the Loss is −log q(correct Class), the same as the Negative Log-Likelihood and thus equivalent to MLE, and the more confident a wrong answer, the bigger the penalty.
