## NLP Overview

#### What Is NLP? Turning Language into Numbers
ONE: **NLP (Natural Language Processing)** is the field that has computers **turn human speech and writing into numbers so they can understand it, and also produce it**. Translation, search, chatbots, spam filters, and voice assistants are all NLP.
LIKE: It's just like learning a foreign language. Read sentences by breaking them into words (Tokenization), learn word meanings from the surrounding words (Embedding), tell parts of speech and names apart (Sequence Labeling), and practice predicting what comes next an enormous number of times (Language Model), and eventually you become able to write.
STEP:
1. **Computers only compute numbers.** So text first has to be turned into numbers.
2. **Break it into pieces**: Split sentences into processing units (Tokens).
3. **Turn it into numbers**: Turn each Token into a Vector (a list of numbers). Words with similar meanings should get similar Vectors.
4. **Solve the task**: Classify whole sentences (spam/legitimate, positive/negative), tag each word (part of speech, person names), or create new text (translation, summarization, dialogue).
5. **Evaluation and responsibility**: Check whether the answer Labels are consistent, whether performance was measured properly, and whether it's unfair to particular groups.
END: NLP = splitting language into Tokens and turning them into numbers to classify, tag, and create text.

#### Why Natural Language Is Hard for Computers
ONE: Human language is hard because it's **ambiguous (Ambiguity)**, **endlessly varied in expression (Variability)**, most words **appear rarely (Sparsity)**, and meaning **leans heavily on context and common sense**.
LIKE: "I saw the man with the telescope" is ambiguous: was I holding the telescope, or was the man? People figure it out from the situation, but computers don't know the situation.
STEP:
1. **Ambiguity - words**: Does "bank" mean a riverbank or a financial institution?
2. **Ambiguity - structure**: The same sentence can be parsed with several structures (the telescope example).
3. **Variability**: "I'm hungry," "I could eat," and "Want to grab a bite?" mean about the same thing. New slang·typos·dialects keep appearing too.
4. **Sparsity (Zipf's Law)**: In word frequencies, only a few words are very common, and most words and expressions are very rare. So expressions never seen in the training data keep showing up.
5. **Context·common sense·intent**: Whether "Great job" is praise or sarcasm depends on knowing the situation.
END: It's hard because it's ambiguous, varied, sparse, and context-dependent. Every NLP technique is a way of fighting these four.

#### How NLP Evolved: Rules → Statistics → Neural Networks → LLMs
ONE: NLP evolved from **an era of people writing rules** → **an era of learning probabilities from data** → **an era of Neural Networks learning even the representations** → **the era of huge Pre-trained Models (LLMs)**.
LIKE: It's like how ways of learning a foreign language changed. Memorizing grammar books (rules) → seeing lots of example sentences and picking up "this word is usually followed by that word" (statistics) → getting a feel for word meanings (Embedding) → reading an enormous number of books and becoming able to say anything (LLM).
STEP:
1. **Rule-Based (~1980s)**: People build grammars·rules·dictionaries. Accurate, but rules have to be added endlessly, so it doesn't scale. Example: ELIZA.
2. **Statistical (1990s–2000s)**: Estimates probabilities from a body of text (Corpus). n-gram, Naive Bayes, HMM, CRF. But people still designed the Features.
3. **Neural (early 2010s)**: word2vec (2013) learned word meanings as Vectors, and LSTMs and Attention processed sentences.
4. **Transformer + Pre-training (2017 onward)**: The Transformer (2017) made large-scale parallel training possible, and BERT·GPT (2018) made it standard to learn first from unlabeled text and then adapt to a task.
5. **LLMs (2020s)**: Adding instruction-following training (Instruction Tuning) and learning from human feedback (RLHF) to scaled-up models let them solve many tasks with just a Prompt.
END: Rules → statistics → neural networks → LLMs. The parts people built by hand have steadily turned into parts learned from data.

### Why is natural language hard for computers?
E: "I saw the man with the telescope" is ambiguous: was I holding the telescope, or was the man? People figure it out from the situation, but computers don't know the situation.
A: First, Ambiguity: one word has several meanings (bank: riverbank·financial institution), and the same sentence can be parsed with several structures. Second, Variability: the same meaning is expressed in countless different ways, and new slang·typos·dialects keep appearing. Third, Sparsity: word frequencies follow Zipf's Law, so most words·phrases appear rarely; and finally, meaning leans heavily on context·common sense·the speaker's intent (sarcasm).

### What was the progression from Rule-Based → Statistical → Neural → LLM?
| Period | Approach | Examples |
| ~1980s | Human-made grammars·rules·dictionaries (Rule-Based) | ELIZA, rule-based translation |
| 1990s–2000s | Statistical Models that estimate probabilities from a Corpus | n-gram, Naive Bayes, HMM, CRF, Statistical Machine Translation |
| Early 2010s | Word Embeddings and Recurrent Neural Networks | word2vec (2013), LSTM seq2seq, Attention |
| 2017 onward | Transformers and Pre-training | Transformer (2017), BERT·GPT (2018) |
| 2020s | LLMs + Instruction Tuning·Human Feedback | GPT-3, ChatGPT, Open LLMs |
A: The era of people writing rules was accurate but didn't scale; the statistical era learned probabilities from data but had people design the Features; and the Neural Network era learned even the representations with Embeddings. Once the Transformer made large-scale parallel training possible, adapting a model Pre-trained on unlabeled text to each task became standard, and scaled-up LLMs became able to solve many tasks with just a Prompt.

## Text Normalization

#### Tokenization and Regular Expressions
ONE: **Tokenization** is the first step, **splitting text into processing units (Tokens)**. A **Regular Expression** is a **language for describing string patterns**, used for splitting·extraction·cleaning.
LIKE: It's like drawing slashes between the words when you first read a foreign-language sentence. A Regular Expression is a search spell like "find everything shaped like 3 digits-3 digits-4 digits."
STEP:
1. **Basics**: Split on spaces and punctuation. "I love NLP." → I / love / NLP / .
2. **Tricky decisions**: Is "don't" one Token or two (do + n't)? Is "New York" one unit? The period in "3.14" isn't the end of a sentence. Emails·URLs·emoji need separate handling too.
3. **Languages like Korean**: Particles attach directly to words (the Korean for "at school" is the word "school" with a particle meaning "at" glued on), so **Morphological Analysis**, which splits words into meaningful units, is needed. school + at.
4. **Regular Expression example**: `\d{3}-\d{3}-\d{4}` finds phone numbers shaped like "3 digits-3 digits-4 digits."
5. **Where it's used**: Token boundary rules, extracting dates·phone numbers·emails, cleaning like stripping HTML tags, and simple rule-based classifiers.
END: Tokenization is breaking text up to read it; Regular Expressions are pattern-finding spells.

#### Stemming and Lemmatization
ONE: Two ways of **grouping** the many forms of a word **into one**. **Stemming** **chops off the ending** by rule, and **Lemmatization** **looks up the base form** in a dictionary.
LIKE: Stemming is snipping off the end of a word with scissors; Lemmatization is looking it up in a dictionary and writing down the base form.
STEP:
1. **Purpose**: Group "study, studies, studying" into one, shrinking the Vocabulary and treating them as the same meaning.
2. **Stemming**: Chops off suffixes like "-ing, -es, -ed" by rule (Porter Stemmer). Fast, but it sometimes produces non-words like "studies → studi."
3. **Lemmatization**: Uses a dictionary and part-of-speech information to find the actual base form (Lemma). "studies → study," "better (adjective) → good." Accurate but slow.
4. Today's Neural Network models often skip this step by using Subword Tokenization.
| Comparison | Stemming | Lemmatization |
| Method | Chop off by rule | Find the base form with a dictionary + part of speech |
| studies | studi | study |
| better | better | good (as an adjective) |
| Speed | Fast | Slow |
END: Chopping quickly is Stemming; looking things up accurately in a dictionary is Lemmatization.

#### Subword Tokenization (BPE)
ONE: Splits words into **frequently used pieces (Subwords)**. Common words stay as one chunk, and rare words are broken into known pieces, so it **handles even never-seen words**. The best-known method is **BPE**.
LIKE: It's memorizing frequently used words whole, and reading never-seen words by breaking them into known pieces. Even if you don't know "unhappiness," you can read it as "un + happi + ness."
STEP:
1. **The problem with word units**: The Vocabulary grows to hundreds of thousands, and never-seen words (OOV, Out-of-Vocabulary) can't be handled.
2. **The problem with character units**: The Sequences to process get far too long, and a single character carries almost no meaning.
3. **Training BPE (Byte Pair Encoding)**: Start by splitting every word into characters.
4. Find **the adjacent pair that appears together most often** in the Corpus and merge it into one new symbol. Example: "e" + "s" → "es".
5. Repeat the merging until reaching the chosen Vocabulary size.
6. **Applying to new text**: Apply the learned merge rules in the same order.
7. **Result**: Common words become a single Token and rare words become several pieces, so OOV disappears.
8. The GPT family uses Byte-Level BPE, which starts from Bytes, and BERT uses the similar WordPiece.
END: Common things whole, rare things in pieces. It balances Vocabulary size against sequence length.

### What is Tokenization? Where are Regular Expressions used?
A: Tokenization is splitting text into processing units (Tokens) such as words·Subwords·punctuation, which involves decisions like handling "don't," "New York," "3.14," and emails·URLs·emoji, and languages where particles attach to words, like Korean, need Morphological Analysis. A Regular Expression is a language for describing string Patterns, used for Token boundary rules, extracting dates·phone numbers·emails, text Cleaning, and simple Rule-Based Classifiers.

### What is the difference between Stemming and Lemmatization?
E: Stemming is snipping off the ending with scissors; Lemmatization is looking it up in a dictionary and writing down the base form.
A: Stemming chops off Suffixes by rule, like the Porter Stemmer, so it's fast but sometimes produces non-words like "studies → studi." Lemmatization uses a dictionary and part-of-speech (POS) information to find the base form (Lemma) that's in the dictionary, like "studies → study" and "better → good" (adjective), so it's more accurate but slow. Both aim to group word variants into one and shrink the Vocabulary.

### Why is Subword Tokenization like BPE needed, and how does it work?
E: You memorize frequently used words whole, and read never-seen words by breaking them into known pieces. Even if you don't know "unhappiness," you can read it as "un + happi + ness."
A: Word units make the Vocabulary too large and can't handle never-seen words (OOV, Out-of-Vocabulary), while character units make Sequences too long. BPE (Byte Pair Encoding) starts from characters and repeatedly merges the adjacent pair that appears together most often in the Corpus into one new symbol (Merge) until reaching the chosen Vocabulary size, then applies the learned Merges to new text in the same order. So common words become a single Token and rare words become several pieces, making OOV disappear; the GPT family uses Byte-Level BPE, and BERT uses the similar WordPiece.

## Text Classification

#### Bag-of-Words and n-gram Features
ONE: **Bag-of-Words (BoW)** is the simplest representation, turning a document into **a Vector of word counts**. It has the weakness of **ignoring order**, so runs of consecutive words (**n-grams**) are added as Features.
LIKE: BoW is cutting a sentence into word cards, dumping them into a bag, and just counting them. Then "not good" and "good, not" become the same, so you also throw in cards that bundle two or three words (n-grams).
STEP:
1. **Build the Vocabulary**: Number every word in the training data.
2. **Document → Vector**: In a Vector the size of the Vocabulary, record how many times each word appeared (or whether it appeared). It's a Sparse Vector, mostly 0s.
3. **Weakness**: It ignores order, so "dog bites man" and "man bites dog" become the same.
4. **n-gram Features**: Add each run of n consecutive words as a Feature. Having the Bigram "not good" lets it catch negation.
5. **The price of n-grams**: As n grows, the number of Features explodes and most become rare (Sparsity).
END: BoW is counting words; n-grams are the fix that also captures short-range order.

#### Naive Bayes vs Logistic Regression for Text Classification
ONE: The two basic models for text classification. **Naive Bayes** **just counts**, so it's fast and strong when data is scarce, and **Logistic Regression** learns Weights, so it's **usually more accurate when data is plentiful**.
LIKE: Naive Bayes is an accountant who records in a ledger how many times each word appeared in spam and legitimate mail and compares. Logistic Regression is a student who adjusts each word's "spam score" a little at a time through practice problems.
STEP:
1. **Training Naive Bayes**: Just count. P(Class) = the fraction of documents in that Class. P(word | Class) = the fraction of that Class's words that are that word (+1 Laplace Smoothing to prevent 0).
2. **Predicting with Naive Bayes**: Pick the Class with the largest log P(Class) + Σ log P(word | Class).
3. **Why Log?** Multiplying small probabilities hundreds of times becomes 0 on a computer (Underflow). Taking the Log turns multiplication into addition, and since Log is an always-increasing function, the largest Class stays the same.
4. **Logistic Regression**: Has a Weight for each word and learns them with Gradient Descent (topic 14).
5. **Key difference**: Naive Bayes assumes "words are independent of each other," so for words that always appear together like "San" and "Francisco," it counts the evidence twice (Double Counting). In Logistic Regression, the two Weights split the evidence between them, avoiding the duplication.
| Comparison | Naive Bayes | Logistic Regression |
| Kind | Generative | Discriminative |
| Training | Just count and done | Gradient Descent |
| With little data | Strong | Relatively weak |
| Correlated Features | Counted twice | Weights split the evidence |
END: Fast and little data, Naive Bayes; lots of data and accuracy matters, Logistic Regression.

#### Sentiment Analysis
ONE: The task of judging whether text is **positive or negative (or which emotion it expresses)**. Accuracy improves in the order word lists → statistical classifiers → Pre-trained Models.
LIKE: It's reading a movie review and guessing the star rating. If you see "best" or "moving," it's positive; "boring" or "worst," negative. But "I expected the best, but it wasn't" fools you if you look only at the words.
STEP:
1. **The Lexicon approach**: Score with lists of positive·negative words. The simplest.
2. **The classifier approach**: Train Naive Bayes or Logistic Regression on BoW·n-gram Features using labeled reviews.
3. **Tip ① Binary NB**: For sentiment, **whether** a word appeared matters more than how many times, so counts are clipped to 1.
4. **Tip ② Handling negation**: Mark the words after "not" with NOT_. "not good" → "NOT_good," distinguishing it from the positive word good.
5. **Pre-trained Models**: Fine-tuning a model like BERT catches even sarcasm and context far better.
END: Lexicon → classifier → Pre-trained Model. Catching negation and sarcasm is the key.

### What are Bag-of-Words and n-gram Features?
E: BoW is cutting a sentence into word cards, dumping them into a bag, and just counting them. Then "not good" and "good, not" become the same, so you also throw in cards that bundle two or three words (n-grams).
A: BoW (Bag-of-Words) is a representation that makes a document into a Vector the size of the Vocabulary holding each word's count (or presence), ignoring order. n-gram Features add runs of n consecutive words (the Bigram "not good") as Features to capture local order and Negation, but as n grows the dimensions explode and become Sparse.

### How does a Naive Bayes Text Classifier train and predict? Why use Log Probabilities?
A: Training is just counting: compute the Prior P(c) = number of Class c documents / total documents and the Likelihood P(w | c) = (count(w, c) + 1) / (total words in Class c + |V|) (Laplace Smoothing). Prediction is ĉ = argmax_c [log P(c) + Σᵢ log P(wᵢ | c)], adding up the probabilities of each word in the document and picking the largest Class. Multiplying small probabilities hundreds of times becomes 0 through Floating-Point Underflow, so the Log is taken to turn products into sums, and since Log is Monotonic, the argmax result is the same.

### How does a Logistic Regression Classifier differ from Naive Bayes?
A: Naive Bayes is a Generative Model that models P(x | c)P(c), assumes the Conditional Independence of words, and trains with just Counts, so it's fast and strong when data is scarce. Logistic Regression is a Discriminative Model that models P(c | x) directly and learns Weights with Gradient Descent; it splits the Weights of mutually correlated Features ("San" and "Francisco") to avoid Double Counting, and it's usually more accurate when data is plentiful.

### How do you do Sentiment Analysis?
A: Most simply, score with a Lexicon of positive·negative words, and if you have Labeled data, train Naive Bayes or Logistic Regression on BoW·n-gram Features. For sentiment, whether a word appeared matters more than how many times, so Binary Naive Bayes, which clips Counts to 1, works well, and Negation is handled by adding a NOT_ prefix to words after "not." These days, Fine-tuning a Pre-trained Model like BERT catches even sarcasm·context better.

## Data Annotation

#### Labeling Guidelines and Agreement
ONE: Models learn **the Labels people attach as the answers**, so the Labels have to be consistent. **Guidelines** unify the criteria, and **Inter-Annotator Agreement** measures how much people agree with each other.
LIKE: Without a grading rubric, the same answer gets different scores from different teachers. The model learns those inconsistent scores as-is. So you make a rubric and check how similarly the teachers grade.
STEP:
1. **What goes in a Guideline**: The definitions of the Labels, how to handle ambiguous cases, examples and counterexamples.
2. **The refinement process**: Label a portion in advance → analyze where people disagreed → revise the Guideline, and repeat.
3. **If the Labels are inconsistent**: The ceiling on model performance drops, and evaluation results can't be trusted either.
4. **Inter-Annotator Agreement**: The degree to which several people attach the same Label to the same data. Human agreement becomes the realistic ceiling on model performance.
5. **Why not a simple agreement rate?** Even if two people answer at random, some of their answers will match by chance. If they pick Yes/No 50/50, half will match by chance.
6. **Cohen's Kappa**: κ = (pₒ − pₑ) / (1 − pₑ). pₒ is the actual agreement rate, and pₑ is the probability of agreeing by chance. It subtracts chance to measure real agreement.
7. **Example**: If pₒ = 0.8 and pₑ = 0.5, κ = (0.8 − 0.5) / (1 − 0.5) = 0.6. 1 is perfect agreement, and 0 is chance level. For three or more people, use Fleiss' Kappa.
END: Unify with a rubric (Guideline), and check Label quality with agreement minus chance (Kappa).

#### Annotation Bias
ONE: When the **perspectives and preconceptions** of the people attaching Labels get into the Labels, the model **learns that Bias as-is**.
LIKE: If you gather only graders who find a particular regional dialect "rude," a student who learns from that rubric will also come to judge the dialect as rude.
STEP:
1. **Case**: A toxic-language classifier trained on data that labeled text written in a particular dialect as more toxic blocks that group's harmless text more often.
2. **Annotation Artifacts**: If gaps in the Guideline create surface cues that let you get the answer just by spotting certain words, the model learns that shortcut (Shortcut) instead of real understanding.
3. **Mitigation**: Annotators from diverse backgrounds, clear Guidelines, and evaluating broken down by group.
END: Human preconceptions pass through the Labels to become model Bias. Prevent it with diverse people, clear criteria, and per-group evaluation.

### Why are Labeling Guidelines important?
E: Without a grading rubric, the same answer gets different scores from different teachers. The model learns those inconsistent scores as-is.
A: Labels are both the answers the model learns and the standard for Evaluation, so a Guideline records the definitions of the Labels, how to handle ambiguous cases, and examples and counterexamples, so that multiple Annotators Label consistently. If Labels are inconsistent, the ceiling on model performance drops and evaluation results can't be trusted, so the Guideline is refined by repeating preliminary Labeling → disagreement analysis → Guideline revision.

### What are Inter-Annotator Agreement and Cohen's Kappa (κ)?
E: Even if two people attach Labels at random by flipping coins, half will match by chance. Kappa subtracts this "matching by chance" part to measure how much they truly agree.
A: Inter-Annotator Agreement is the degree to which several people attach the same Label to the same data; it reflects the task's difficulty and Label quality, and human agreement becomes the realistic ceiling on model performance. Cohen's Kappa, for two Annotators, is κ = (pₒ − pₑ) / (1 − pₑ), where pₒ is the actual agreement rate and pₑ is the chance-agreement probability computed from each person's Label proportions. 1 is perfect agreement and 0 is chance level; for example, if pₒ = 0.8 and pₑ = 0.5, κ = 0.6. For three or more people, use Fleiss' Kappa.

### How does Annotation Bias affect a model?
A: Models learn the patterns in the Labels as-is, so the Annotators' backgrounds·perspectives·preconceptions become the model's Bias. For example, a Toxicity Classifier trained on data that labeled text written in a particular dialect (African American English) as more toxic blocks that group's text more often, and the model learns as a Shortcut the surface cues that arise from gaps in the Guideline (Annotation Artifacts, where spotting certain words reveals the answer). It's mitigated with a diverse set of Annotators, clear Guidelines, and per-group Evaluation.

## Language Models

#### Language Models and n-grams
ONE: A **Language Model** **computes as a probability how natural a sentence is**, or **predicts the next word**. An **n-gram Model** guesses the next word by looking at **only the previous n−1 words**.
LIKE: It's like your phone's autocomplete suggesting "dinner" after "What's for": guessing the next word from just the last few words.
STEP:
1. **Sentence probability**: P(what's, for, dinner, tonight) = P(what's) × P(for | what's) × P(dinner | what's for) × …. Multiply the probability of each next word conditioned on all the words before it.
2. **The problem**: Counting every combination of preceding words is impossible. Long sentences never appear in the Corpus even once.
3. **Markov Assumption**: Simplify by assuming "the next word depends only on the last few words" (the Markov Chain of topic 14).
4. **Bigram (n = 2)**: Looks only at the one previous word. P(dinner | for) = count of "for dinner" / count of "for".
5. **Trigram (n = 3)**: Looks at the two previous words.
6. **The trade-off in n**: A larger n sees more context, but the sparsity problem, where most n-grams never appear even once, grows.
END: Language Model = next-word probability. n-grams compute it just by counting, under the assumption "look only at the last few words."

#### Perplexity and Smoothing
ONE: **Perplexity** is a metric of **how confused** a Language Model is when it sees real sentences (lower is better). **Smoothing** **hands out small probabilities** even to word combinations never seen before, keeping probabilities from becoming 0.
LIKE: A Perplexity of 10 means that each time it picks the next word, it's "as confused as guessing on a 10-option multiple-choice question." Smoothing is setting aside a few points on the theory that "questions that never appeared on past tests could still show up."
STEP:
1. **Perplexity**: PP = P(sentence)^(−1/N). The inverse of the probability given to the sentence, normalized by the number of words.
2. It equals the exponential of the average Cross-Entropy. The higher the probability the model gives real sentences, the lower it gets.
3. **Caution**: It can only be compared between models with the same Vocabulary and Tokenization, and low Perplexity doesn't guarantee real task performance.
4. **The zero-probability problem**: If a Test sentence contains even one Bigram unseen in training, its probability becomes 0, the whole product becomes 0, and Perplexity becomes infinite.
5. **Laplace (Add-1) Smoothing**: Adds 1 to every Count. Simple, but it gives away too much to unseen combinations.
6. **Better methods**: Add-k (adding less than 1), Backoff (falling back to a shorter n-gram if the longer one is missing), Interpolation (mixing several n), and Kneser-Ney (the best-performing classic method).
END: Perplexity is the degree of confusion (lower is better); Smoothing is the device that prevents zero probabilities for unseen combinations.

### What are n-gram Language Models and the Markov Assumption?
E: It's like your phone's autocomplete suggesting "dinner" after "What's for": guessing the next word from just the last few words.
A: A Language Model computes the probability of a word sequence P(w₁, …, wₙ) = Π P(wᵢ | w₁, …, wᵢ₋₁), and an n-gram Model uses the Markov Assumption to look only at the previous n−1 words instead of the whole history. A Bigram is learned as a ratio of Corpus Counts (MLE), as in P(wᵢ | wᵢ₋₁) = C(wᵢ₋₁ wᵢ) / C(wᵢ₋₁). A larger n sees more context, but the Sparsity problem, where most n-grams never appear even once, grows.

### What does Perplexity measure?
E: It's "on average, as confused as guessing one out of how many options" when picking the next word. 10 means it's as confused as answering a 10-option multiple-choice question every time.
A: Perplexity, for a Test sentence W, is PP(W) = P(w₁ … wₙ)^(−1/N), the inverse of the probability the model gives the word sequence, normalized by its length, and it equals the exponential of the average Negative Log-Likelihood (Cross-Entropy). Lower is better, meaning the model is less surprised by real text; it can only be compared between models with the same Vocabulary and Tokenization, and low Perplexity doesn't guarantee performance on the real task (Downstream Task).

### Why is Smoothing (Laplace, etc.) needed?
A: n-grams that weren't in the training Corpus get probability 0 under MLE, and if a sentence contains even one such n-gram, the sentence probability becomes 0 and Perplexity becomes infinite. Smoothing takes a bit of probability from seen events and hands it out to unseen ones; Laplace is P(wᵢ | wᵢ₋₁) = (C(wᵢ₋₁ wᵢ) + 1) / (C(wᵢ₋₁) + V), and because it gives away too much, Add-k, Backoff, Interpolation, and Kneser-Ney are better for Language Models.

## Neural Network-Based NLP

#### How Neural Networks Beat n-grams
ONE: Neural Networks represent words as **similar Vectors when their meanings are similar**, so they **generalize even to never-seen combinations**, and RNNs·LSTMs summarize **context longer than a fixed Window**.
LIKE: Even if an n-gram model memorizes "the cat is sleeping," "the dog is sleeping" is a never-seen combination, so it doesn't know it. A Neural Network has similar Vectors for cat and dog, so it judges "the dog is sleeping" to be natural too.
STEP:
1. **Classifying with a Feedforward network**: Average the word Embeddings of a sentence (Pooling) into a fixed-length Vector, then classify through a Hidden Layer → Softmax. No hand-made Features are needed, but averaging loses the order.
2. **n-gram limitation ①**: It can't see context outside the fixed Window.
3. **n-gram limitation ②**: It treats words as unrelated symbols, so similar words can't share knowledge.
4. **n-gram limitation ③**: Increasing n makes the number of combinations to count grow exponentially.
5. **RNN·LSTM Language Models**: In principle, the Hidden State summarizes the entire preceding context, and the number of Parameters stays the same as the context length grows (topic 15).
6. **Limitations**: Training is slow (sequential computation), and very long contexts are still hard. The Transformer solved this problem.
END: Similar words have similar Vectors, so they generalize, and the recurrent structure summarizes long context.

### How do you classify text with a Feedforward Neural Network?
A: Turn the sentence into a fixed-length Vector and feed it in. Average·sum the Word Embeddings (Pooling) or use a BoW·TF-IDF Vector as input, pass it through a Hidden Layer (ReLU), and train with a Softmax Output Layer and Cross-Entropy; it learns representations without hand-made Features and can capture nonlinear combinations, but Pooling loses the word order.

### What makes RNN·LSTM Language Models better than n-grams?
A: n-grams can't see context outside the fixed Window, treat words as unrelated symbols so they can't generalize to never-seen combinations, and their Parameters grow exponentially as n increases. RNN·LSTM Language Models summarize, in principle, the whole history with the Hidden State, give similar words similar Embeddings so that after seeing "the cat is sleeping" they also give "the dog is sleeping" a high probability, and keep the same number of Parameters as the context length grows. In exchange, training is slow, and very long contexts are still hard.

## Word Embeddings

#### The Limits of One-Hot and the Distributional Hypothesis
ONE: A **One-Hot Vector** has a 1 in just one slot per word, so **every word is equally different from every other** and it carries no meaning. The idea that **"words that appear in similar contexts have similar meanings" (the Distributional Hypothesis)** is the starting point for meaningful Vectors (Embeddings).
LIKE: Even for a word you've never seen, if it shows up in sentences like "people drink it hot in the morning and it contains caffeine," you can guess it's something like coffee. A word's meaning shows in the friends it keeps (the surrounding words).
STEP:
1. **One-Hot**: With a 50,000-word Vocabulary, it's a 50,000-dimensional Vector with a 1 only in its own slot.
2. **The problem**: Every pair of words is mutually Orthogonal, so the similarity of "hotel" and "motel" and the similarity of "hotel" and "banana" are both equally 0.
3. **Distributional Hypothesis**: "You shall know a word by the company it keeps." Statistics about the surrounding words reveal the meaning.
4. **Embedding**: A **Dense, low-dimensional** Vector built on this idea. Usually a few hundred dimensions, with a meaningful number in every slot.
5. Words with similar meanings gather close together in the Vector space.
| Comparison | One-Hot | Embedding |
| Dimensions | Vocabulary size (tens of thousands) | A few hundred |
| Values | Almost all 0 (Sparse) | All meaningful numbers (Dense) |
| Similarity | 0 for every pair | Close if the meanings are similar |
END: One-Hot makes everything equally different, so it has no meaning. Embeddings, which learn meaning from surrounding words, place similar words close together.

#### TF-IDF and PPMI: Finding Important Words
ONE: **TF-IDF** gives high scores to words that **appear often in this document but rarely in other documents**. **PPMI** measures **how much more often than chance** two words appear together.
LIKE: In a soccer article, "goal" and "player" appear often and are rare in other articles, so they describe this article well (high TF-IDF). "And" and "is" appear in every article, so their scores are low.
STEP:
1. **TF (Term Frequency)**: How many times a word appears in this document. The more, the more important.
2. **IDF (Inverse Document Frequency)**: log(total number of documents / number of documents containing the word). The fewer documents it appears in, the larger it is.
3. **TF-IDF = TF × IDF**: It lowers common words like "the" and "and" and raises words that characterize the document. A classic technique for search engines and document classification.
4. **PMI (Pointwise Mutual Information)**: log₂ [P(w, c) / (P(w)P(c))]. How many times more likely the two words are to appear together than if they were independent (the same idea as Lift in topic 14).
5. **PPMI**: Negative PMI values are hard to trust, so they're clipped to 0.
6. PPMI tends to overrate rare words, so the context probabilities are raised to the 0.75 power to correct for it.
END: TF-IDF finds "words unique to this document," and PPMI finds "words that occur together more than chance."

#### Word2Vec and GloVe
ONE: **Word2Vec** learns word Vectors by solving **a guess-the-neighboring-words quiz**, and **GloVe** learns Vectors from **a table of co-occurrence counts across the whole Corpus**.
LIKE: Skip-gram is a quiz where you see one word and guess the friends around it, and CBOW is a quiz where you see the friends and guess the blank in the middle. Negative Sampling turns the quiz into an easy true/false question: "are these two real friends, or a stranger picked at random?"
STEP:
1. **The idea**: If you train it to solve a prediction quiz well, the Weights obtained along the way become Vectors that carry the words' meanings.
2. **Skip-gram**: Center word → predict the neighboring words.
3. **CBOW (Continuous Bag-of-Words)**: Neighboring words → predict the center word.
4. **The problem**: Computing a Softmax over the whole Vocabulary (tens of thousands of words) every time is too expensive.
5. **Negative Sampling**: Turns it into a small binary classification that labels real (word, neighbor) pairs as 1 and k randomly drawn fake pairs as 0, which makes training much faster.
6. **GloVe**: First builds a table of "how many times words A and B appeared together" across the whole Corpus. Then it trains so that the Dot Product of the two Vectors approaches the Log of that count.
7. **The difference**: Word2Vec predicts while scanning local Windows (Prediction-Based), and GloVe uses the global statistics directly (Count-Based). Actual performance is similar, depending on the task.
END: Word2Vec captures word meaning in Vectors through a neighboring-words quiz, and GloVe through a table of co-occurrence counts.

#### Cosine Similarity and Word Analogies
ONE: How similar two word Vectors are is measured by **how much their directions match (Cosine Similarity)**. In Embedding space, **relationships can show up as directions**, which makes calculations like **king − man + woman ≈ queen** work.
LIKE: If two people are pointing in the same direction, you say they're "pointing at the same place" even if their arms are different lengths. Cosine Similarity compares the direction a Vector points, not its length.
STEP:
1. **Cosine Similarity**: cosθ = a·b / (‖a‖‖b‖). 1 is the same direction, 0 is unrelated, and −1 is opposite.
2. **Why ignore length?** Common words tend to have longer Vectors, so you have to remove length to compare meaning alone.
3. **Analogy**: The direction of the difference "man → king" is similar to the direction of the difference "woman → queen."
4. So if you find the Vector closest to vec(king) − vec(man) + vec(woman) (excluding the input words), you get queen.
5. **Caution**: It works well only for some relationships, and it partly relies on the "exclude the input words" rule.
END: The same direction means similar meaning, and relationships can show up as directions too.

#### Static vs Contextual Embeddings
ONE: **Static Embeddings** (word2vec, GloVe) have **exactly one Vector** per word, so they can't tell apart the senses of a word with multiple meanings. **Contextual Embeddings** (BERT, etc.) make **a different Vector for each sentence**.
LIKE: A Static Embedding is a dictionary that lists only one meaning per word, so the "bank" in "sit on the river bank" and "deposit money at the bank" become the same. A Contextual Embedding creates a fresh meaning that fits the sentence every time it reads one.
STEP:
1. **Static**: After training, the Vector for "bank" is fixed as one. The riverbank·financial-institution meanings get mixed into one Vector (the Polysemy problem).
2. **Contextual**: Passes the whole sentence through a model (ELMo, BERT), so even the same "bank" gets a different Vector depending on the context.
3. **Pros**: It distinguishes word senses and is far more accurate.
4. **Cons**: Unlike Static, where you just look up a table for each word, it has to run the whole model every time, so it's expensive.
END: One per word is Static; made fresh for each sentence is Contextual. Contextual is accurate but expensive.

#### Social Bias in Embeddings
ONE: Embeddings **learn even the stereotypes** in their training text. You **measure** it with differences in similarity between sets of words, and since erasing alone isn't enough, you have to **re-evaluate on real tasks**.
LIKE: Someone who grew up reading only old newspapers might learn the stereotype "nurses are women, engineers are men." Embeddings also learn the prejudices of the text they read, as-is.
STEP:
1. **Case**: Analogy results like "man : computer programmer :: woman : homemaker" have been reported.
2. **Measuring with WEAT**: Compare the differences in Cosine Similarity between target word sets (male·female names) and attribute word sets (career·family).
3. **Measuring with the Gender Direction**: Project occupation words onto the he − she direction and see whether they lean to one side.
4. **The limits of Debiasing**: Removing the Gender Direction reduces the visible metrics, but research shows the Bias remains in other forms.
5. So you have to re-evaluate per group on the real task (Downstream Task).
END: Embeddings learn the prejudices in text. Measure them, don't rely on erasing alone, and recheck on the real task.

### What are the limits of One-Hot Vectors, and what is the Distributional Hypothesis?
E: Even a word you've never seen, if it shows up in sentences like "people drink it hot in the morning and it contains caffeine," you guess is something like coffee. A word's meaning shows in the friends it keeps (the surrounding words).
A: A One-Hot Vector is a Sparse Vector with tens of thousands of dimensions, as many as the Vocabulary size, that's almost all 0s, and every pair of words is Orthogonal, so the similarity of "hotel" and "motel" and that of "hotel" and "banana" are both 0, meaning it carries no meaning. The Distributional Hypothesis is the hypothesis that words appearing in similar contexts have similar meanings (Harris, Firth: "You shall know a word by the company it keeps"), and it's the basis of every method that builds Dense, low-dimensional Vectors (Embeddings) from surrounding-word statistics.

### How do TF-IDF and PPMI measure the importance of words?
A: TF-IDF gives high weight to words that appear often within a document (TF, Term Frequency) while appearing in only a few of all documents (IDF = log(N/df), Inverse Document Frequency), lowering common words like "the" and raising words that characterize the document. PPMI (Positive Pointwise Mutual Information) measures how much more often than chance a word w and a context c appear together, as PMI = log₂ [P(w, c) / (P(w)P(c))], with negative values clipped to 0; it's used to build Word-Context Co-occurrence Matrices, and because it tends to overrate rare words, the context probabilities are corrected by raising them to the power α = 0.75.

### What are Word2Vec (Skip-gram, CBOW) and Negative Sampling?
E: Skip-gram is a quiz where you see one word and guess the friends around it, and CBOW is a quiz where you see the friends and guess the blank in the middle. Negative Sampling turns the quiz into an easy true/false question by asking only "are these two real friends, or a stranger picked at random?"
A: Word2Vec uses the Weights obtained while solving a prediction task as word Vectors: Skip-gram predicts the neighboring words from the center word, and CBOW (Continuous Bag-of-Words) predicts the center word from the neighboring words. Because a Softmax over the whole Vocabulary every time is expensive, Negative Sampling turns it into a Logistic Regression problem that labels real (word, context) pairs as 1 and k fake pairs drawn from the frequency-to-the-0.75-power distribution as 0, greatly speeding up training.

### How does GloVe differ from Word2Vec?
A: Word2Vec is a Prediction-Based method that trains on prediction problems within local Windows while scanning the Corpus, while GloVe is a Count-Based method that first builds a Word-Word Co-occurrence count Matrix for the whole Corpus and then trains so that the Dot Product of two word Vectors approaches the Log of the Co-occurrence count (Weighted Least Squares). The difference is that it uses Global statistics directly, and actual performance is similar, depending on the task.

### What are Cosine Similarity and Word Analogies (king − man + woman ≈ queen)?
A: The similarity of two word Vectors is measured with Cosine Similarity cosθ = a·b / (‖a‖‖b‖), which removes the effect of length (frequent words tend to have longer Vectors). In Embedding Space, relationships sometimes show up as directions, so if you find the Vector closest to vec(king) − vec(man) + vec(woman) (excluding the input words), you get queen. However, such Analogies work well only for some relationships and partly rely on the rule of excluding the input words.

### What is the difference between Static Embeddings and Contextual Embeddings (BERT, etc.)?
E: A Static Embedding is a dictionary that lists only one meaning per word, so the "bank" in "sit on the river bank" and "deposit money at the bank" become the same. A Contextual Embedding creates a fresh meaning every time it reads a sentence.
A: Static Embeddings like word2vec·GloVe have one Vector per word type, so they can't distinguish the senses of words with multiple meanings (Polysemy), while Contextual Embeddings like ELMo·BERT pass the whole sentence through a model and make a different Vector for each Token depending on the context. Contextual Embeddings are far more accurate but costly, since the whole model has to be run.

### How do you measure the social Bias contained in Embeddings?
A: Embeddings learn the stereotypes in their training text too, and Analogy results like "man : computer programmer :: woman : homemaker" have been reported. Measurement methods include WEAT (Word Embedding Association Test), which compares differences in Cosine Similarity between Target word sets (e.g., male·female names) and Attribute word sets (e.g., career·family), and projecting occupation words onto the Gender Direction (he − she). Research shows that Debiasing by removing the Gender Direction reduces surface metrics but leaves the Bias in other forms, so you have to re-evaluate per group on the Downstream Task.

## Evaluation and Fairness

#### NLP Evaluation and Statistical Significance
ONE: NLP tasks where positives are rare (spam, hate speech) are evaluated with **Precision·Recall·F1**. Whether a score difference between two models is **not just chance** is checked with tests like the **Paired Bootstrap**.
LIKE: If you randomly redraw the Test questions and rerun the exam thousands of times and B almost always beats A, you can call it a real difference in skill. If B only wins sometimes, it might be luck.
STEP:
1. **Precision**: The fraction correct among things called positive. **Recall**: The fraction found among actual positives. **F1**: The harmonic mean of the two (topic 14).
2. **Macro Average**: Averages the per-Class scores equally, so small-Class performance shows.
3. **Micro Average**: Computes over all decisions pooled together, so it's dominated by large Classes.
4. **The significance problem**: Even if B scores 1 point higher than A on the Test Set, it could flip on a different Test Set.
5. **Paired Test**: You're comparing on the same Test Set, so use a paired (Paired) test.
6. **Paired Bootstrap Test**: Draw from the Test Set with replacement to create thousands of virtual Test Sets, and compute the probability (p-value) that the score difference would be that large by chance (topic 13).
7. **Others**: Permutation Test, McNemar's Test. Training with several Seeds and reporting the spread also matters.
END: Rare positives are evaluated with Precision·Recall·F1, and whether a difference is real is checked with the Paired Bootstrap.

#### Bias and Fairness in NLP
ONE: NLP models can behave unfavorably toward **particular dialects·identity terms·genders**. Measure it with **per-group evaluation**, and respond with data augmentation·better Guidelines·**documentation**.
LIKE: If a translator renders sentences from a language without gendered pronouns as "he is a doctor, she is a nurse," it has inserted a stereotype that wasn't in the original.
STEP:
1. **Case ① Toxicity classification**: Judges harmless sentences containing a particular dialect or identity terms as toxic.
2. **Case ② Translation**: Translates gender-neutral pronouns into he/she according to stereotypes about the occupation.
3. **Case ③ Coreference**: Links pronouns to different genders depending on the occupation.
4. **Measurement**: Compare performance on evaluation data broken down by group.
5. **Counterfactual Augmentation**: Add data where the gendered words in sentences are swapped, so the model doesn't judge differently based on gender.
6. **Documentation**: Record the limitations with a Model Card (the model's intended use·limitations·evaluation results) and a Datasheet (the data's sources·composition).
END: Measure broken down by group, augment with swapped data, and document the limitations.

### What are Precision·Recall·F1 and Macro·Micro Average in evaluating NLP Classification?
A: Precision is the fraction correct among things the model called positive, Recall is the fraction found among actual positives, and F1 is their Harmonic Mean; in tasks where positives are rare, like spam·hate speech, they matter more than Accuracy. In Multi-Class, the Macro Average averages per-Class scores equally, revealing small-Class performance, while the Micro Average computes over all decisions pooled together and is dominated by large Classes (see 14 Machine Learning).

### How do you check whether the difference between two models is chance?
E: If you randomly redraw the Test questions and rerun the exam thousands of times and B almost always beats A, you call it a real difference in skill.
A: The two models are compared on the same Test Set, so a Paired Test is used. The Paired Bootstrap Test draws from the Test Set with replacement to create thousands of virtual Test Sets and computes the p-value as the probability that the score difference would grow that large by chance; the Approximate Randomization (Permutation) Test and McNemar's Test are also used. Training with several Seeds and reporting the spread also matters.

### What are some cases of Bias and Fairness problems in NLP models, and how do you respond?
A: Cases include Toxicity Classifiers judging harmless sentences containing a particular dialect or identity terms ("gay," "Muslim") as toxic, translations inserting stereotypes like "he is a doctor, she is a nurse" when translating gender-neutral pronouns, and Coreference models linking genders differently depending on the occupation. Measure with Evaluation Sets broken down by group, and respond with Data Augmentation·Counterfactual Augmentation (swapping gendered words), improved Guidelines, and documenting limitations with Model Cards·Datasheets.

## Sequence Labeling

#### POS Tagging · NER · BIO Tagging
ONE: **Sequence Labeling** is the task of attaching **a tag to each word** of a sentence. **POS Tagging** finds parts of speech, and **NER** finds names like people·companies·places. Multi-word names are marked with **BIO Tagging**.
LIKE: It's attaching a colored-pencil tag to each word in a sentence. POS Tagging uses "noun·verb·adjective" tags, and NER uses "person·company·place" tags.
STEP:
1. **POS (Part-of-Speech) Tagging**: The/determiner cat/noun sleeps/verb.
2. **NER (Named Entity Recognition)**: Finds the **span** of Entities like people (PER), organizations (ORG), and places (LOC) and labels their type.
3. **The problem**: How do you mark a multi-word Entity like "New York" with word-level Labels?
4. **BIO Tagging**: **B** = the start of an Entity (Begin), **I** = inside an Entity (Inside), **O** = outside an Entity (Outside).
5. **Example**: "Jane Villanueva of United Airlines" → B-PER I-PER O B-ORG I-ORG.
6. **Example**: "I flew to New York" → O O O B-LOC I-LOC.
END: Attach a tag to each word, and mark multi-word names with B (start)·I (inside)·O (outside).

#### HMMs: Hidden States and Observations
ONE: An **HMM (Hidden Markov Model)** is a model that assumes **hidden states that can't be seen** (parts of speech) change according to a Markov Chain, and each state emits **a visible observation** (a word).
LIKE: You can't see your friend's mood (hidden state); you only see the emoji they send (observations). Their mood changes depending on yesterday's mood (Transition), and each mood has a tendency toward certain emoji (Emission). It's guessing their mood each day from the emoji alone.
STEP:
1. **Hidden State**: The part-of-speech Tag. It isn't written in the sentence, so it can't be seen directly.
2. **Observation**: The actual word. It can be seen.
3. **Transition Probability** P(next Tag | previous Tag): Nouns often follow determiners (the).
4. **Emission Probability** P(word | Tag): The probability of "cat" appearing given a noun.
5. **Training**: Just count in a Corpus tagged with parts of speech. How many times "noun after determiner," how many times "cat as a noun."
6. **Prediction**: Given the words, find the most likely sequence of Tags (Viterbi).
END: Hidden states chain together through Transitions, and each state emits words through Emissions.

#### The Viterbi Algorithm and CRFs
ONE: **Viterbi** quickly finds **the most probable** of all possible Tag sequences using Dynamic Programming. A **CRF**, unlike an HMM, is a Discriminative model that **can freely use many kinds of Features**.
LIKE: When finding the shortest route on a road with many forks, if you remember only "the best way to get here" at each fork, you don't have to compare every possible route.
STEP:
1. **Looking at every path**: With N Tags and a sentence of length T, there are Nᵀ possible Tag sequences, which is infeasible.
2. **The key observation**: "The best path ending in Tag s at time t" is the maximum, over each Tag at time t−1, of the best path to that Tag multiplied by the Transition·Emission probabilities (Optimal Substructure).
3. The same subproblems keep getting reused, so it's **Dynamic Programming** (topic 07).
4. While filling in the table, leave arrows for "where it came from" (Backpointers), and at the end follow them backward to recover the path.
5. **Complexity**: At each time step (T), for each Tag (N), it compares N previous Tags, so it's **O(N²T)**.
6. **CRF (Conditional Random Field)**: An HMM makes the strong assumption that "the current word depends only on the current Tag." A CRF models P(Tag sequence | word sequence) directly, so it can freely use overlapping Features like capitalization, suffixes, and neighboring words. It's usually more accurate.
7. CRFs also use Viterbi for prediction (Decoding), but training is heavier. BiLSTM-CRF, which puts a CRF on top of a Neural Network, was also used.
END: Viterbi remembers only "the best so far" to solve it in O(N²T), and a CRF is an evolution of the HMM that uses more Features.

### What are POS Tagging and NER (Named Entity Recognition)? What is BIO Tagging?
E: It's attaching a colored-pencil tag to each word in a sentence. POS Tagging uses "noun·verb·adjective" tags, and NER uses "person·company·place" tags.
A: POS (Part-of-Speech) Tagging is the task of attaching a part of speech to each Token, and NER is the task of finding the span (Span) of Entities like people (PER)·organizations (ORG)·places (LOC) in a sentence and labeling their type. BIO Tagging is used to turn multi-word Entities into Token-level Labels: B is the start of an Entity (Begin), I is inside an Entity (Inside), and O is outside an Entity (Outside), so "Jane Villanueva of United Airlines" becomes B-PER I-PER O B-ORG I-ORG.

### What are the Transition Probability and Emission Probability of an HMM (Hidden Markov Model)?
E: You can't see your friend's mood (hidden state); you only see the emoji they send (observations). Their mood changes depending on yesterday's mood (Transition), and each mood determines which emoji they tend to send (Emission).
A: An HMM is a Generative Model that assumes unobservable Hidden States (part-of-speech Tags) change according to a Markov Chain and each State emits an observation (a word). The Transition Probability P(tᵢ | tᵢ₋₁) is the probability of moving from the previous Tag to the next (nouns often follow determiners), and the Emission Probability P(wᵢ | tᵢ) is the probability of that word appearing given that Tag; both are estimated from Counts in a Tagged Corpus.

### Why is the Viterbi Algorithm Dynamic Programming, and what is its complexity?
A: Viterbi is the Algorithm for finding the most probable Tag sequence; it has Optimal Substructure, in that the best path ending in State s at time t is the maximum, over each State at time t−1, of the best path to that State multiplied by the Transition·Emission probabilities, and the same Subproblems keep getting reused, so it's Dynamic Programming (see 07 Algorithm). It fills in a table and recovers the path with Backpointers, and with N Tags and a sentence of length T, it takes O(N²T) instead of the O(Nᵀ) of looking at every path.

### How does a CRF differ from an HMM?
A: An HMM is a Generative Model that models P(words, Tags), so it makes the strong assumption that the current word depends only on the current Tag, while a (Linear-Chain) CRF (Conditional Random Field) is a Discriminative Model that models P(Tag sequence | word sequence) directly, so it can freely use overlapping Features like capitalization·suffixes·neighboring words and is usually more accurate. Decoding still uses Viterbi, and training is heavier, computing Gradients with the Forward-Backward Algorithm. In the Neural Network era, a CRF is sometimes placed on top of a Neural Network's output, as in BiLSTM-CRF.

## Transformers and LLMs

#### BERT and GPT: Filling in Blanks vs Continuing the Text
ONE: **BERT** is a Transformer **Encoder** that **guesses blanks while seeing both sides** of the sentence, making it strong at understanding, and **GPT** is a **Decoder** that **continues the text by seeing only what came before and writing the next word**, making it strong at generation.
LIKE: BERT is a reader solving fill-in-the-blank questions, so it sees both before and after the blank. GPT is a writer continuing a story, so it writes the next word looking only at what it has written so far.
STEP:
1. **BERT's structure**: Uses only the Transformer Encoder. Every Token sees every Token before and after it (Bidirectional).
2. **BERT's training (MLM)**: Masks about 15% of the Tokens and guesses them. "I went to [MASK] today" → school.
3. **BERT's strengths**: **Understanding** tasks like sentence classification, NER, and search.
4. **GPT's structure**: Uses only the Transformer Decoder. Each Token sees **only the Tokens before it** (Causal Attention). Seeing what comes after would be peeking at the answer, so it's masked.
5. **GPT's training**: Next Token Prediction. "I went to" → school.
6. **GPT's strengths**: Text **generation**. Scaled up, it solves many tasks with just a Prompt, so it's the mainstream architecture for today's LLMs.
7. **Encoder-Decoder**: T5, which uses both, and the original Transformer are used for translation·summarization.
| Comparison | BERT | GPT |
| Structure | Encoder | Decoder |
| Direction it sees | Bidirectional | Only what came before (Causal) |
| Training objective | Guess masked Tokens (MLM) | Next Token prediction |
| Strengths | Understanding (classification, NER, search) | Generation (dialogue, writing) |
END: BERT, which sees both sides, is strong at understanding; GPT, which sees only the past and continues the text, is strong at generation.

#### How Does an LLM Write? Next Tokens and Sampling
ONE: An LLM writes by computing **a probability distribution over the next Token**, **picking one and appending it**, feeding it back into the input, and **repeating**. How it picks (**Sampling**) changes the character of the text.
LIKE: It's like a game of building a sentence one word at a time, where each turn you look at "a table of likely next words and their probabilities" and pick one. Always picking first place is safe but monotonous; sometimes picking second or third place is more varied but can go off the rails.
STEP:
1. **Input**: Split the Prompt into Tokens and feed them to the model.
2. **Compute probabilities**: The model outputs the probability of the next Token over the whole Vocabulary (Softmax).
3. **Pick (Decoding)**: Picking only the most probable Token is **Greedy**; drawing at random according to the probabilities is **Sampling**.
4. **Repeat**: Append the picked Token to the end of the input and go back to step 2. Repeat until an end Token appears or the length limit is reached (Autoregressive).
5. **Temperature**: Divide the scores (Logits) by T before the Softmax. T < 1 concentrates more on first place, making it stable; T > 1 spreads it out evenly, making it varied but possibly off the rails.
6. **Top-k / Top-p**: Draw only from the top k candidates by probability, or from the candidates up to cumulative probability p, preventing wildly off Tokens.
7. **KV Cache**: Stores and reuses the Keys·Values of earlier Tokens, so it doesn't recompute from scratch for every Token (topic 15).
KEY: An LLM doesn't "look up facts"; it "picks a plausible next Token," so it can fluently make up things it doesn't know. This is the root cause of Hallucination.
END: Compute the probability table, pick one, append it, repeat. Temperature and Top-p set the balance between stability and variety.

#### Pre-training · Fine-tuning · Prompting
ONE: An LLM **first learns general knowledge from large amounts of unlabeled text (Pre-training)**, **is adapted to specific uses with a small amount of data (Fine-tuning)**, and in use, **instructions and examples are put in the input (Prompting)** to draw out the desired behavior.
LIKE: It's reading tens of thousands of books in a foreign language to get a feel for it (Pre-training), getting separate on-the-job interpreter training (Fine-tuning), and at actual work, receiving a request like "Please summarize this document in a formal tone; here's an example" (Prompt).
STEP:
1. **Pre-training**: Learns from web·book text with Self-Supervised objectives like next-Token prediction or guessing masked Tokens. Since people don't need to attach answers, it can learn from enormous amounts of data.
2. **Fine-tuning**: Additional training on a small labeled dataset to adapt to a specific task.
3. **Instruction Tuning**: Trains on "instruction → good response" pairs to make a model that follows instructions.
4. **RLHF**: Builds a reward model from data where people picked the better of several responses, then uses reinforcement learning to make the model give the responses people prefer (topic 14).
5. **Prompting**: Changes only the input, not the Weights. Giving just an instruction with no examples is **Zero-Shot**, and showing a few examples is **Few-Shot** (In-Context Learning).
6. **Efficient Fine-tuning**: Instead of adjusting the whole huge model, train only a small number of added Parameters, as in LoRA.
END: Read a lot (Pre-training), get on-the-job training (Fine-tuning), and work from a request (Prompting).

#### Hallucination and RAG
ONE: **Hallucination** is when an LLM produces content that's **fluent but not true**. **RAG** reduces it by **first finding material** relevant to the question, **putting it in the Prompt**, and having the model answer based on it.
LIKE: Hallucination is a student who relies only on memory making up a plausible answer even to questions they don't know. RAG is like an open-book exam: finding the relevant material, laying it open, and answering from it.
STEP:
1. **The cause of Hallucination**: Next-Token prediction learns "plausibility," not "truth." It tries to answer even about knowledge that's missing from or outdated in its training data.
2. **RAG step 1 - Preparation**: Split documents into chunks, turn them into Embedding Vectors, and store them in a Vector DB.
3. **RAG step 2 - Retrieval**: Turn the question into a Vector too and find the most similar document chunks (Vector Search). Keyword-matching BM25 is sometimes used alongside it.
4. **RAG step 3 - Generation**: Put the retrieved documents in the Prompt and say "answer based on this material" (Grounding).
5. **Pros**: It reflects up-to-date information·internal company documents and can show its sources. There's no need to retrain the model.
6. **Limitations**: If retrieval goes wrong or the model misreads the documents, it's still wrong.
7. **Safeguards**: Evaluating retrieval quality, citing sources (Citation), and having the model answer "I don't know" when there's no supporting evidence.
END: Relying only on memory leads to making things up. RAG, which finds material and lays it open to answer from, produces grounded answers.

### What is the difference between BERT (Encoder) and GPT (Decoder)?
E: BERT is a reader solving fill-in-the-blank questions, so it sees both sides of the blank, and GPT is a writer continuing a story, so it writes the next word looking only at what it has written so far.
A: BERT uses only the Transformer Encoder and sees the whole sentence with Bidirectional Attention, and it's Pre-trained with MLM (Masked Language Modeling), which masks some Tokens (about 15%) and guesses them, making it strong at understanding tasks like Classification·NER·Search. GPT uses only the Decoder and trains by predicting the next Token (Next Token Prediction) with Causal Attention, which uses a Mask so each Token sees only earlier Tokens; it's strong at text Generation, and scaled up it can solve many tasks with just a Prompt, making it the mainstream architecture for today's LLMs. T5 and the original Transformer, which use both an Encoder and a Decoder, are used for translation·summarization.

### What are Pre-training, Fine-tuning, and Prompting?
A: Pre-training is the stage of learning Self-Supervised objectives like Next Token Prediction·Mask prediction on large-scale unlabeled text to acquire general knowledge of language, and Fine-tuning is the stage of further training that model on a small Labeled dataset to adapt it to a specific task (including building conversational models with Instruction Tuning·RLHF). Prompting is drawing out the desired behavior by putting instructions and examples in the input without changing the Weights; with no examples it's called Zero-Shot, and showing a few is called Few-Shot (In-Context Learning).

### What are RAG (Retrieval-Augmented Generation) and Hallucination?
E: Hallucination is a student who relies only on memory making up a plausible answer even to questions they don't know, and RAG is like an open-book exam: finding the relevant material, laying it open, and answering from it.
A: Hallucination is when an LLM generates content that's fluent but untrue or unsupported, and it happens because Next Token Prediction learns "plausibility" and the model tries to answer even about knowledge that's missing from or outdated in its training data. RAG finds documents relevant to the question with Embedding-based Vector Search or BM25, puts them in the Prompt, and has the model answer using that content as its basis (Grounding), letting it reflect up-to-date·internal knowledge and present sources. However, if Retrieval goes wrong or the model misreads the documents, it can still be wrong, so safeguards like evaluating Retrieval quality, citing sources (Citation), and answering "I don't know" when it doesn't know are used alongside it.

### How do LLMs generate text? What are Temperature and Top-p?
E: It's like a game of building a sentence one word at a time, where each turn you look at "a table of likely next words and their probabilities" and pick one. Lowering the Temperature makes it a model student who always picks first place; raising it makes it a free-spirited writer who sometimes picks an off-the-wall word.
A: LLMs are Autoregressive: for the input Token sequence, they compute the probability distribution of the next Token with Softmax, pick one, and append it to the end of the input, repeating until an end Token or the length limit. Picking only the most probable Token is Greedy Decoding, and drawing from the distribution is Sampling; Temperature T divides the Logits by T, so T < 1 makes the distribution peaked (stable) and T > 1 makes it flat (varied). Top-k samples only from the top k candidates, and Top-p (Nucleus Sampling) only from the candidates up to cumulative probability p, preventing off-the-wall Tokens, and the KV Cache, which stores the Keys·Values of earlier Tokens, reduces repeated computation.
