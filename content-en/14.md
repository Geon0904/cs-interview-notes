## AI Basics: Agent · Search · Reasoning

#### AI and the Rational Agent
ONE: The most useful way to look at AI is as a **Rational Agent**. It's something that **perceives (Sensor)** its surroundings and **acts (Actuator)**, choosing **the action that maximizes a performance measure**.
LIKE: Picture a robot vacuum. It senses dust and obstacles with its sensors (perception), moves with its wheels and brushes (action), and tries to maximize the goal score of "clean the house in a short time." Whether it thinks like a person doesn't matter; it just has to end up acting correctly.
STEP:
1. **Agent**: Anything that perceives an environment and acts. Robots, game AI, recommendation systems, chatbots.
2. **Rational**: Choosing **the action with the largest expected performance measure** given the information learned so far. It's different from all-knowing omniscience.
3. **Properties of the environment**: Is it fully or only partly observable, are outcomes determined or random, does the current action affect the future, is it alone or are there opponents?
4. Chess is an easy environment that's "fully observable, with determined outcomes, divided into squares." Self-driving is at the opposite end (partly observable, random, continuous, with other cars around).
5. **Kinds of Agents (simple → complex)**: Reacting by rule only to what's visible now (Simple Reflex) → also tracking states it can't see (Model-Based) → planning the sequence that reaches a goal (Goal-Based) → choosing the most satisfying of several ways (Utility-Based) → improving itself through experience (Learning Agent).
6. **Machine Learning** is the way to make an Agent "improve itself through experience." The rest of this topic is that story.
END: AI = a Rational Agent that perceives and acts to maximize a goal. The harder the environment, the more sophisticated the Agent it needs.

#### Search: Solving Problems as Route Finding
ONE: Many problems can be turned into **"finding a sequence of actions from a start state to a goal state."** Depending on what information is used, search strategies split into **BFS·DFS·UCS·A\*** and others.
LIKE: Say you're finding your way to a destination in an unfamiliar city. With no information, you could check every nearby alley first (BFS), follow one road to its end (DFS), go by only the distance walked so far (UCS), or also use the hint "straight-line distance to the destination" (A*).
STEP:
1. **Defining the problem**: Once you set the start state, possible actions, results of actions, goal test, and path cost, it becomes a search problem. Examples: puzzles, route finding, scheduling.
2. **BFS (Breadth-First)**: Layer by layer, nearest first. If all costs are equal, it guarantees the shortest path, but it uses a huge amount of Memory.
3. **UCS (Uniform-Cost)**: Lowest cost so far first. Optimal even when costs differ (the same idea as Dijkstra).
4. **DFS (Depth-First)**: One path all the way to the end. Little Memory, but no guarantee of optimality, and in an endless space it may never get out.
5. **Iterative Deepening**: Repeats DFS while raising the limit: to depth 1, to depth 2, to depth 3… It gets DFS's low Memory and BFS's completeness together.
6. **Greedy Best-First**: The smallest "estimate of the remaining distance to the goal (Heuristic)" first. Fast, but no guarantee of optimality.
7. **A\***: The smallest **cost so far + estimate of the remaining distance** first. If the estimate **never overestimates the real cost (Admissible)**, it guarantees the optimal path.
| Strategy | Information used | Optimality | Memory |
| BFS | None | When costs are equal | Very large |
| UCS | Cost so far | Yes | Large |
| DFS | None | No | Small |
| Iterative Deepening | None | When costs are equal | Small |
| Greedy Best-First | Remaining-distance estimate | No | Large |
| A* | So far + estimate | If the estimate doesn't overestimate | Large |
KEY: A good Heuristic comes from **the answer to an easier problem with some of the original constraints removed**. If you assume puzzle pieces can pass through other pieces, the sum of each piece's distance to its goal position becomes a good estimate that never exceeds the real cost.
END: Turn a problem into states and actions, and it becomes a search problem. A*, which uses a hint (Heuristic) well, is both optimal and fast.

#### Local Search: Finding Just One Good Answer
ONE: When you need only **one good final state** rather than a path, these methods improve the current answer by **fixing it a little at a time**. Hill Climbing, Simulated Annealing, and Genetic Algorithms are the main ones.
LIKE: You're looking for the highest point on a foggy mountain without a map. If you keep stepping toward higher ground (Hill Climbing), you can get stuck on top of a small hill. So you either allow going downhill now and then at first (Simulated Annealing), or have many people spread out to search and then blend the approaches of those who did well (Genetic Algorithm).
STEP:
1. **Hill Climbing**: Keep moving to a neighboring state that's better than the current one. Simple, but it gets stuck at a **Local Optimum** (a spot that's best only among its neighbors).
2. **Simulated Annealing**: The name comes from annealing, slowly cooling metal. At first (when the temperature is high), it sometimes allows moving in a worse direction, and it gradually lowers that probability. It gives a chance to escape small hills.
3. **Genetic Algorithm**: Keeps many answers (individuals) at once, and repeatedly mixes good answers together (crossover), changes them randomly now and then (mutation), and lets only good answers survive.
4. **In continuous spaces**: The same idea is **Gradient Descent**. It moves a little at a time down the slope in the direction that lowers the Loss (see Optimization below).
USE: Building timetables, optimizing delivery routes, circuit layout, Neural Network training (Gradient Descent).
END: If you don't need a path, fix the current answer a little at a time. To avoid getting stuck, allow worse moves now and then, or mix several answers.

#### Game Search: Minimax · Alpha-Beta · MCTS
ONE: In games with an opponent, you use **Minimax**, which chooses moves assuming **"the opponent also plays their best."** You look farther ahead with **Alpha-Beta Pruning**, which cuts branches you don't need to look at, and **MCTS**, which plays out many random games.
LIKE: In tic-tac-toe, it's thinking all the way through: "If I play here, my opponent will play where it's worst for me, and then I'll…" And once you know "this move is definitely worse than one I already looked at," there's no need to look any deeper.
STEP:
1. **Game Tree**: A tree that unfolds my move, the opponent's move, my move… in turn.
2. **Minimax**: Assumes that on my turn I pick the move with the **maximum** score, and on the opponent's turn they pick the move with the **minimum** score (for me), and computes scores by passing them up from the bottom.
3. **Alpha-Beta Pruning**: Skips branches that can't affect the result. If you look at good moves first, you can see about **twice as deep** in the same time.
4. **Depth limit + evaluation function**: Chess can't be searched to the end, so at a certain depth you estimate a "current position score" (e.g., the sum of piece values).
5. **MCTS (Monte Carlo Tree Search)**: In games where an evaluation function is hard to build, it simulates a huge number of random games after each move and rates the moves by their win rates.
6. The Go AI **AlphaGo** combined MCTS with Neural Networks (evaluating which moves are promising and how good the position is) and defeated Lee Sedol, a 9-dan professional, in 2016.
END: Choose moves by assuming the opponent plays their best (Minimax), cutting unnecessary branches (Alpha-Beta), and, when that's hard, playing many games out (MCTS).

#### CSP (Constraint Satisfaction Problem)
ONE: A problem defined by **variables**, the **range of values** each variable can take, and the **constraints** between variables. Sudoku, timetables, and map coloring are the classic examples.
LIKE: Think of building a timetable. Each class (variable) has times it can go in (values), and there are rules (constraints) like "the same teacher's classes can't overlap." The problem is finding an arrangement that satisfies every rule.
STEP:
1. **Variables**: The things whose values must be decided. The 81 cells of a Sudoku.
2. **Domain**: The values each variable can take. 1–9.
3. **Constraint**: No repeated numbers in the same row·column·3×3 box.
4. The basic solution is **Backtracking**. Assign values one at a time, and back up when a rule is broken (topic 07).
5. **Trick ① MRV**: Assign **the variable with the fewest remaining choices first**. Handling the likely sticking points first discovers failure early.
6. **Trick ② LCV**: Try **the value that rules out the fewest choices for other variables first**. It raises the chance of success.
7. **Trick ③ Constraint propagation (Forward Checking, etc.)**: Each time you assign a value, immediately erase the values that became impossible in neighboring variables. If any variable ends up with 0 choices, back up right away.
USE: Building timetables·work schedules, assigning meeting rooms, Sudoku, circuit design, SAT Solvers (solving logic problems as CSPs).
END: CSP = variables + values + constraints. Add "hardest first, leave room with values, erase immediately" to Backtracking and it gets much faster.

#### Logic and Planning: Reasoning with Rules
ONE: These methods write knowledge down as **logical formulas** and **infer** new facts by rules, or write actions as **"preconditions → effects"** and **plan the sequence of actions** that reaches a goal.
LIKE: If you know "all humans are mortal" and "Socrates is a human," you can derive "Socrates is mortal." Planning is like cooking: chaining "if the water is boiling (precondition), you can add the pasta, and once it's added, the pasta cooks (effect)" to find the sequence that gets you to "pasta's ready."
STEP:
1. **Propositional Logic**: Reasons by combining true/false sentences with AND·OR·NOT·→ (topic 01). It either checks every case or uses an inference rule (Resolution).
2. **SAT Solver**: A tool that answers "is there an assignment that makes this logical formula true?" The problem is NP-Complete (topic 08), but in practice it solves even very large instances well.
3. **First-Order Logic**: It can express quantifiers like "for all" and "there exists" as well as objects·relations, so it's far more expressive, but reasoning is harder.
4. **Planning**: Describes actions by "preconditions" and "effects," and finds a sequence of actions from the initial state to the goal state. It's a search problem, but since a state is a set of facts, stronger Heuristics can be used.
5. Real-world Planning has to deal with time·resource constraints, breaking big tasks into small ones (hierarchical decomposition), and replanning when things don't go as expected.
WHY: Unlike Machine Learning, which learns from data, logic and planning give exact, explainable answers in areas with clear rules (legal regulations, configuration validation, robot task sequences).
END: Deriving new facts from facts and rules is Logic; chaining actions' preconditions·effects to reach a goal is Planning.

### What is a Rational Agent?
E: Like a robot vacuum, it's something that senses its surroundings with sensors, moves with wheels, and chooses the actions that maximize the score "clean in a short time."
A: A Rational Agent perceives its Environment through Sensors (Percept) and acts through Actuators, choosing the action that maximizes the expected Performance Measure based on its percepts and knowledge so far. The standard isn't whether it thinks like a person but whether it ends up acting correctly, and the more the Environment is partially observable·stochastic·dynamic·multi-Agent, the more sophisticated a structure it needs, such as a Model-Based, Goal-Based, Utility-Based, or Learning Agent.

### How does A* Search work, and when does it guarantee the optimal path?
E: It's route finding that explores first the road with the shortest "distance walked so far + straight-line distance to the destination." The straight-line distance can never be longer than the distance you actually have to travel, so it's a trustworthy hint.
A: A* evaluates each Node with f(n) = g(n) + h(n) and expands the smallest first. g(n) is the actual cost from the start to n, and h(n) is the estimated cost (Heuristic) from n to the goal. If h never overestimates the actual cost (Admissible), it's optimal in Tree Search, and in Graph Search optimality is guaranteed when the Heuristic is Consistent. Good Heuristics come from the answers to easier problems with the constraints relaxed.

### What are Minimax and Alpha-Beta Pruning?
E: In tic-tac-toe, thinking all the way through on the assumption that "if I play here, my opponent will play where it's worst for me" is Minimax, and skipping moves already confirmed to be worse without looking at them to the end is Alpha-Beta Pruning.
A: Minimax finds the best move in a Game Tree by assuming my turn (Max) picks the maximum score and the opponent's turn (Min) picks the minimum score, propagating the Leaves' evaluation values upward. Alpha-Beta Pruning carries α, the minimum Max is guaranteed so far, and β, the maximum Min is guaranteed so far, and skips branches that can't affect the result, getting the same result faster. With good move ordering, the amount of search drops to about O(b^(d/2)), so it sees twice as deep in the same time.

### What is a CSP? What are the tricks for speeding up Backtracking?
E: Like building a timetable, it's the problem of choosing a time (value) for each class (variable) while finding an arrangement that keeps every rule (constraint), such as "the same teacher can't overlap."
A: A CSP (Constraint Satisfaction Problem) is defined by variables, each variable's Domain, and the Constraints between variables, with Sudoku·timetables·map coloring as classic examples. The basic method is Backtracking, and it gets much faster when you add MRV (Minimum Remaining Values), which picks the variable with the fewest remaining values first; LCV (Least Constraining Value), which tries first the value that rules out the fewest choices for other variables; and Forward Checking·Arc Consistency (constraint propagation), which erase neighbors' impossible values each time a value is assigned.

## Machine Learning Basics

#### What Is Machine Learning? Data Instead of Rules
ONE: **Machine Learning** is a way of having **rules found from the data itself** instead of people writing the rules by hand. The goal is to **get things right even on data it has never seen (Generalization)**.
LIKE: It's just like studying for an exam. You study with past exam questions (Training Data), check your skills with practice tests (Validation Data), and get your score on the real exam (Test Data). If you just memorize the answers to past questions (Overfitting), you'll bomb the real exam.
STEP:
1. **Traditional programming**: People write the rules. "If the subject line contains 'free,' it's spam."
2. But spam keeps evolving, and writing every rule is impossible.
3. **Machine Learning**: Give it lots of examples of spam/legitimate mail, and the model finds on its own "which features separate spam."
4. **The training process**: The model predicts → computes how wrong it was compared to the answer (**Loss Function**) → adjusts the model's numbers (**Parameters, Weights**) slightly to be less wrong → repeats.
5. **The goal is Generalization**: It shouldn't memorize the training data; it has to get things right on mail it has never seen.
6. **What makes a good model**: Performance on new data (most important), an evaluation metric that fits the business goal, the simpler model when performance is similar (Occam's Razor), as much interpretability as needed, prediction speed and cost, and fairness across groups.
END: Machine Learning = finding rules from data. Adjust the Weights to reduce the Loss, and the goal is getting things right on data it has never seen.

#### Kinds of Learning: Supervised · Unsupervised · Self-Supervised · Reinforcement
ONE: Depending on how answers are given, learning splits into **learning with answers (Supervised)**, **finding structure without answers (Unsupervised)**, **making answers from the data itself (Self-Supervised)**, and **learning by acting and getting rewarded (Reinforcement)**.
LIKE: Supervised is a workbook with an answer key, Unsupervised is grouping similar problems without answers, Self-Supervised is practicing by covering blanks in sentences yourself and filling them in, and Reinforcement is learning by getting treats (Reward) after actions, like training a puppy.
STEP:
1. **Supervised Learning**: Learns from pairs of inputs and answers (Labels). If the answer is a category, it's **Classification** (spam/legitimate); if it's a number, it's **Regression** (home prices).
2. **Unsupervised Learning**: Finds hidden structure in data without answers. Grouping similar customers (**Clustering**), reducing dimensions (**Dimensionality Reduction**).
3. **Semi-Supervised Learning**: Uses a small amount of labeled data together with a large amount of unlabeled data. Useful when labeling is expensive.
4. **Self-Supervised Learning**: Makes problems and answers from the data itself. Predicting the next word in a sentence, predicting a masked word. It can learn from enormous amounts of data without people labeling anything, which is why **LLM Pre-training** works this way.
5. **Reinforcement Learning**: An Agent acts in an environment and receives Rewards, learning the action strategy (Policy) that maximizes cumulative Reward. Games, robot control.
| Kind | Data | Goal | Example |
| Supervised | Inputs + answers | Predict answers for new inputs | Spam classification, home price prediction |
| Unsupervised | Inputs only | Discover hidden structure | Customer clusters, dimensionality reduction |
| Self-Supervised | Answers made from the data | Representation learning | Next-word prediction (LLM) |
| Reinforcement | Actions and rewards | Maximize cumulative reward | Games, robots |
END: With answers it's Supervised, without them Unsupervised, making them yourself Self-Supervised, and learning from rewards Reinforcement.

#### Feature Vector
ONE: A **Feature Vector** is a single data point turned **into a list of numbers describing its features** so a model can understand it. Which Features you create largely determines the performance of traditional ML.
LIKE: When describing a house to a computer, turning it into a list of numbers like "1,100 sq ft, 3 bedrooms, 7 minutes to the subway station, 12th floor" is a Feature Vector.
STEP:
1. **Feature**: A measurable property that helps with prediction. For home prices: area, number of bedrooms, location.
2. **Feature Vector**: A numeric Vector listing those Features in order. x = (1100, 3, 7, 12).
3. **Text**: Turned into word frequencies (BoW, TF-IDF) or meaning-carrying Embedding Vectors (topic 16).
4. **Images**: Pixel values are used as the numbers.
5. **Feature Engineering**: Creating more useful Features from the raw data. "Age" can be more useful than "date of birth," and "distance to the station" more useful than "address."
6. Deep Learning evolved in the direction of having the model learn Features itself instead of people creating them (topic 15).
END: A Feature Vector is data turned into a list of numbers, and good Features make good models.

#### How Machine Learning and Statistics Differ
ONE: They overlap a lot, but **Statistics** puts more weight on "**why is it so (interpretation·causation·uncertainty)**," while **Machine Learning** puts more weight on "**how well does it predict (predictive performance·scalability)**."
LIKE: If a doctor asking "does this drug really work, and why does it work?" is the statistical view, focusing on "accurately predicting whether this patient will be readmitted next month" is the ML view.
STEP:
1. **Statistics**: Makes assumptions about how the data was generated (e.g., a normal distribution) and emphasizes the meaning of Parameters, uncertainty (confidence intervals), and causal relationships.
2. **Machine Learning**: Even with fewer assumptions, it emphasizes predictive performance on new data and scaling to large data·complex models.
3. Even with the same Linear Regression, statistics first asks "is the coefficient significant?" while ML first asks "what's the Test error?"
4. **In practice**: Prediction is done with ML, and statistics complements it for the reasoning behind decisions and interpreting experiments (A/B Tests).
END: Statistics asks "why," ML asks "how well." They're not competitors; they complement each other.

### What are the differences between Supervised·Unsupervised·Semi-Supervised·Reinforcement Learning?
E: Supervised Learning is a workbook with an answer key, Unsupervised Learning is grouping similar problems without answers, Semi-Supervised Learning is a workbook with only a few answers, and Reinforcement Learning is learning by getting treats (Reward) after actions, like training a puppy.
A: Supervised Learning learns Classification·Regression from pairs of inputs and answers (Labels), and Unsupervised Learning finds structure such as Clustering·Dimensionality Reduction without Labels. Semi-Supervised Learning uses a few Labels together with lots of Unlabeled data, and Reinforcement Learning has an Agent interact with an Environment to learn the Policy that maximizes cumulative Reward. There's also Self-Supervised Learning, which makes answers from the data itself, as in LLM Pre-training.

### What is a Feature Vector?
E: When describing a house to a computer, turning it into a list of numbers like "1,100 sq ft, 3 bedrooms, 7 minutes to the subway station, 12th floor" is a Feature Vector.
A: A Feature Vector is a Vector x ∈ Rᵈ listing measurable properties of a single data point as numbers so a model can process it. Text is turned into word frequencies or Embeddings and images into pixel values, and which Features you create and how (Feature Engineering) largely determines the performance of traditional Machine Learning.

### How do the Machine Learning approach and the statistical approach differ?
A: They overlap a lot, but the weight of their goals differs. Statistics makes assumptions about the data-generating process and puts weight on interpreting Parameters·uncertainty·Causal Inference ("why is it so"), while Machine Learning, even with fewer assumptions, puts weight on predictive performance on new data ("how well does it predict") and scalability to large data·complex models. In practice, prediction is done with ML, and Statistics complements it for the reasoning behind decisions and interpreting experiments.

### What is the definition of a good model?
A: The most important thing is Generalization, the ability to keep performing on data not used for training, and that performance has to be measured with a Metric that fits the business goal. On top of that, a good model is the simpler model when performance is similar (Occam's Razor), with as much Interpretability as needed, reasonable prediction speed·cost, Robustness to changes in the data, and Fairness across groups.

## Data Preparation

#### Feature Scaling
ONE: If Features have **very different ranges (units)**, the Feature with big numbers dominates the model. So you do **Scaling** to bring the ranges close together.
LIKE: If you measure distance using height (60–75 inches) and salary (tens of thousands of dollars) as they are, only salary differences show up and height gets ignored. You have to match the units to compare fairly.
STEP:
1. **The problem for distance-based models**: KNN, K-means, and SVM compute distances, and the Feature with the large range takes up almost all of the distance.
2. **The problem for Gradient Descent**: When Feature ranges differ, the Loss landscape becomes a long, narrow valley, and descent zigzags slowly.
3. **The problem for Regularization**: When ranges differ, Weight sizes differ too, so the penalty is unfair across Features.
4. **Min-Max Scaling**: (x − min) / (max − min). Fits values to 0–1. A single Outlier squeezes the rest into a narrow range.
5. **Standardization (Z-score)**: (x − mean) / standard deviation. Fits values to mean 0 and standard deviation 1. The most common choice.
6. **Robust Scaler**: Uses the Median and IQR, so it holds up against Outliers.
KEY: **Fit** the Scaler **only on the Training Data** (computing the mean·standard deviation), and only **transform** Validation·Test with those values. Fitting on all the data leaks Test information in (Data Leakage). Tree models don't need Scaling.
END: If ranges differ, match them (Min-Max, Standardization, Robust). Fit the Scaler only on the Training Data.

#### Categorical Variable Encoding
ONE: **Categorical values** like "New York·Chicago·Houston" have to be turned into numbers before a model can use them. If there's no order, use **One-Hot**; if there's an order, use **Label (Ordinal) Encoding**.
LIKE: If you write favorite fruits as "apple = 1, banana = 2, grape = 3," the computer might misunderstand that "grape is 3 times as big as apple." So you write "is it apple? banana? grape?" separately as 0/1 each.
STEP:
1. **One-Hot Encoding**: Makes a 0/1 Column for each category. New York = (1, 0, 0), Chicago = (0, 1, 0), Houston = (0, 0, 1). Suited to categories with no order (cities, colors).
2. The problem with One-Hot: If there are thousands of categories, the Columns grow to thousands too (dimension explosion).
3. **Label (Ordinal) Encoding**: Turns categories into integers. Suited to categories with an order (beginner < intermediate < advanced).
4. If you use Label Encoding on categories with no order, a Linear Model learns a size relationship that doesn't exist (New York 1 < Chicago 2). Tree models, however, just split on values, so they're often fine.
5. **When there are very many categories (High Cardinality)**: **Target Encoding**, which replaces each category with the average of the answers for it (watch for Leakage); **Hashing**, which shrinks to a fixed size with a Hash; and **Embedding**, which learns meaning.
END: No order, One-Hot; an order, Label; too many categories, Target Encoding·Hashing·Embedding.

#### Class Imbalance
ONE: If one Class is **very rare** (0.1% fraudulent transactions), the model gets high accuracy just by saying "everything is legitimate." So you have to handle **the evaluation metric, the data, the training, and the Threshold** all differently.
LIKE: If 1 out of 1,000 transactions is fraud, just answering "legitimate" for all of them gives 99.9% Accuracy. But it doesn't catch a single fraud. It's like a security guard who's right 99.9% of the time just by saying "nothing's going to happen."
STEP:
1. **Change the evaluation**: Instead of Accuracy, look at Recall (how much fraud was found), Precision (the fraction of things called fraud that really are), F1, and PR-AUC.
2. **Splitting**: Use a Stratified Split that preserves Class proportions when splitting Train/Test.
3. **Adjust the data**: Increase the rare Class (Oversampling; SMOTE synthesizes similar fake Samples) or reduce the common Class (Undersampling). Apply it **only to the Training Data**.
4. **Adjust the training**: Use Class Weights that give a bigger penalty for getting the rare Class wrong.
5. **Adjust the Threshold**: The model doesn't have to call something fraud only when "the fraud probability is 50% or more." If missing fraud is costly, lower the Threshold to 10% to catch more.
END: Don't evaluate rare Classes with Accuracy; find them by adjusting the data·weights·Threshold.

#### Data Leakage
ONE: The problem where **information that can't be known** at prediction time gets mixed into training and **inflates the validation score**. In a real service, performance collapses.
LIKE: It's peeking at the answer key before a practice test. The score comes out great, but you fall apart on the real exam.
STEP:
1. **Preprocessing leakage**: If you do Scaling or fill in missing values on all the data before splitting, Test information (the mean, etc.) gets mixed into training.
2. **Time leakage**: Splitting time-series data randomly amounts to predicting the past with future data. You have to train on the past and validate on the future.
3. **Group leakage**: If the same user's data ends up in both Train and Test, the model memorizes that person and the score looks good.
4. **Features derived from the answer**: Predicting "was a refund requested" with "was the refund completed" amounts to guessing the cause after seeing the result.
5. **How to prevent it**: Bundle preprocessing into a Pipeline fit only on the Training Data, split by time·group, and ask for each Feature, "can this value be known at prediction time?"
KEY: If a validation score is **too good to believe**, suspect Leakage first.
END: When information unknown at prediction time gets mixed in, the score lies. Prevent it with Pipelines and proper splits.

### Why do you need Feature Scaling (normalization)? What are the methods?
E: If you measure distance using height (inches, 60–75) and salary (dollars, tens of thousands) as they are, only salary differences show up and height gets ignored. You have to match the units to compare fairly.
A: When Feature ranges differ greatly, models that use distance like KNN·K-means·SVM get pulled toward the large Feature, Gradient Descent's contours become elongated and convergence slows, and the Regularization Penalty becomes unfair across Features. Min-Max Scaling fits values to 0–1 with (x − min)/(max − min) but is weak to Outliers; Standardization (Z-score) makes the mean 0·standard deviation 1 with (x − μ)/σ; and when there are many Outliers, use the Robust Scaler, which uses the Median·IQR. Fit the Scaler only on the Training Data and only apply it (transform) to Validation·Test; Tree models don't need Scaling.

### What is Categorical Variable Encoding (One-Hot, Label)?
A: One-Hot Encoding makes a 0/1 Column for each category and suits categories with no order (cities, colors), but the dimensions explode when there are many categories. Label (Ordinal) Encoding turns categories into integers, so it's fine for categories with an order (grade level, rating) or for Tree models, but using it in a Linear Model creates a size relationship that doesn't exist (New York = 1 < Chicago = 2). When there are thousands of categories (High Cardinality), use Target Encoding (watch for Leakage), Hashing, or Embedding.

### How do you handle Class Imbalance?
E: If 1 out of 1,000 transactions is fraud, just answering "legitimate" for all of them gives 99.9% Accuracy. But it doesn't catch a single fraud.
A: First, evaluate with Recall·Precision·F1·PR-AUC instead of Accuracy, and use a Stratified Split. At the data level, apply Oversampling of the minority Class (SMOTE, etc.) or Undersampling of the majority Class only to the Training Data; at the model level, use Class Weights; and finally, adjust the Classification Threshold to fit the business cost.

### What is Data Leakage?
E: It's peeking at the answer key before a practice test. The score comes out great, but you fall apart on the real exam.
A: Data Leakage is the problem where information that can't be known at prediction time (Test Data, future information, values derived from the answer) gets mixed into training and inflates Validation performance. Common examples include Scaling·Imputation on all the data before splitting, splitting a Time Series randomly, the same user appearing in both Train·Test, and predicting "refund requested" with "refund completed"; you prevent it by bundling preprocessing into a Pipeline and splitting by time·group.

## Evaluation and Validation

#### Evaluation Metrics: Regression and Classification
ONE: What you grade a model on determines what it gets good at. **Regression** uses MAE·RMSE and **Classification** uses Precision·Recall·F1 and others, chosen based on **which mistake is more expensive**.
LIKE: In cancer screening, Precision is "the fraction of people who tested positive who actually have cancer," and Recall is "the fraction of people who actually have cancer whom the test found." A test that must not miss patients prioritizes Recall, and a test that must not put healthy people on the operating table prioritizes Precision.
STEP:
1. **Regression - MAE**: The average of the absolute errors. In the original units and less sensitive to Outliers.
2. **Regression - MSE / RMSE**: The average of the squared errors (or its square root). Gives a big penalty to big errors.
3. **Classification - Accuracy**: The fraction correct. Trustworthy only when the Classes are balanced.
4. **Precision** = TP / (TP + FP): "Of the things called positive, the truly positive." Important when you hate false alarms.
5. **Recall** = TP / (TP + FN): "Of the truly positive, the fraction found." Important when you hate misses.
6. **F1**: The harmonic mean of Precision and Recall. It comes out low if only one of them is high, so it looks at the balance.
7. **How to choose**: Which is more expensive, FP or FN? For a spam filter, the FP of sending legitimate mail to the spam folder is more expensive; for cancer diagnosis, the FN of missing a patient is.
| Kind | Metric | Characteristic |
| Regression | MAE | Less sensitive to Outliers |
| Regression | RMSE | Big penalty for big errors |
| Classification | Accuracy | Only for balanced data |
| Classification | Precision | When you want fewer false alarms |
| Classification | Recall | When you want fewer misses |
| Classification | F1 | The balance of the two |
END: What you grade determines the model's character. Choose the Metric based on whether FP or FN is the more expensive mistake.

#### The Confusion Matrix and Macro · Micro Average
ONE: The **Confusion Matrix** is a table that lays out actual vs. predicted to show **TP·FP·FN·TN**, and it's the starting point for every Classification Metric. With several Classes, you use the **Macro** (average per Class) and **Micro** (pooled total) averages.
LIKE: It's a grading sheet that counts, cell by cell, "said right and was right, said right but was wrong, said wrong but was right, said wrong and was wrong."
STEP:
1. **TP (True Positive)**: Called positive and actually positive.
2. **FP (False Positive)**: Called positive but actually negative (a false alarm).
3. **FN (False Negative)**: Called negative but actually positive (a miss).
4. **TN (True Negative)**: Called negative and actually negative.
5. Precision, Recall, and Accuracy are all computed from these four cells.
6. **Macro Average for Multi-Class**: Compute the Metric separately for each Class and take a simple average. Small Classes count equally.
7. **Micro Average**: Pool the TP·FP·FN of all Classes, then compute. Dominated by large Classes. In Multi-Class with one answer per Sample, Micro-F1 = Accuracy.
END: The Confusion Matrix's four cells are the ingredients of every Metric, and multiple Classes are averaged by Macro (equal) and Micro (overall).

#### ROC Curve · AUC and PR Curve
ONE: The **ROC Curve** plots the trade-off between **"the fraction found (TPR)" and "the false alarm rate (FPR)"** as the Threshold sweeps from one end to the other, and the **AUC** is the area under it. When positives are very rare, the **PR Curve** is more honest.
LIKE: Turn up the sensitivity of an airport security scanner and it catches more dangerous items (TPR ↑), but it also beeps at more harmless bags (FPR ↑). ROC is the graph of that trade-off as you turn this sensitivity dial from one end to the other.
STEP:
1. A model usually outputs a probability (score), and a Threshold (e.g., 0.5) decides positive/negative.
2. Lowering the Threshold calls more things positive, so both TPR (Recall) and FPR go up.
3. **ROC Curve**: Connects the points for every Threshold, with FPR on the x-axis and TPR on the y-axis.
4. **AUC (Area Under Curve)**: The area under the ROC. It's "the probability that a randomly chosen positive gets a higher score than a randomly chosen negative." 0.5 is a coin toss, and 1 is perfect ranking.
5. It shows the model's "ranking ability" regardless of the Threshold.
6. **The problem when positives are very rare**: There are so many TNs that even a fair number of FPs makes the FPR look small. The ROC looks better than reality.
7. **PR Curve**: Recall on the x-axis, Precision on the y-axis. It gives a more honest picture for rare-positive problems (fraud detection).
END: ROC·AUC show ranking ability independent of the Threshold; when positives are rare, look at the PR Curve and PR-AUC.

#### Cross-Validation
ONE: A method that **splits the data into k groups and takes turns** using one group at a time for validation, then averages. It evaluates more reliably instead of depending on the luck of a single try.
LIKE: If you take a practice test only once, you can't tell whether it was luck or skill. Splitting the workbook into 5 parts, setting aside one part at a time as the test, and averaging over 5 tries is more trustworthy.
STEP:
1. **Splitting Train / Validation / Test**: Train for training, Validation for choosing models and settings, and Test **only once** at the very end to measure final performance.
2. **k-Fold Cross-Validation**: Split the data into k parts (usually 5 or 10). Train on k−1 and validate on the remaining 1, repeat k times, and look at the mean and spread.
3. Every data point is used for validation once, so no data is wasted and luck plays a smaller role in the evaluation.
4. **Stratified k-Fold**: Preserves each fold's Class proportions in Classification.
5. **Group k-Fold**: Keeps the same user's (group's) data from being split across training and validation.
6. **Time Series Split**: Trains on the past and validates on the future. Don't shuffle randomly.
KEY: Cross-Validation is used for choosing models and tuning Hyperparameters. Final performance is measured only once with a Test Set that's never been used. Looking at the Test set many times ends up fitting to the Test set.
END: Validating in turns reduces luck. Use Cross-Validation to choose, and the Test set once for the final check.

### Please explain the Metrics you know. Which Metrics suit Regression·Classification?
E: In cancer screening, Precision is "the fraction of people who tested positive who actually have cancer," and Recall is "the fraction of people who actually have cancer whom the test found." A test that must not miss patients prioritizes Recall, and a test that must not put healthy people on the operating table prioritizes Precision.
| Kind | Metric | Meaning and characteristics |
| Regression | MAE | Mean Absolute Error, same units and less sensitive to Outliers |
| Regression | MSE / RMSE | Mean of squared errors (or its square root), big penalty for big errors |
| Regression | R², MAPE | Fraction of variance explained, percent error (unstable when answers are near 0) |
| Classification | Accuracy | Fraction correct, trustworthy only when Classes are balanced |
| Classification | Precision TP/(TP+FP) | Of the things called positive, the truly positive |
| Classification | Recall TP/(TP+FN) | Of the truly positive, the fraction found |
| Classification | F1 | Harmonic Mean of Precision and Recall |
| Classification | ROC-AUC, PR-AUC, Log Loss | Ranking ability independent of the Threshold, quality of probabilities |
A: For Regression, choose between MAE, which holds up against Outliers, and RMSE, which weighs big errors heavily, depending on the situation; for Classification, use Accuracy for balanced data and Precision·Recall·F1·PR-AUC for imbalanced data. The most important criterion is which is more expensive, a False Positive (FP) or a False Negative (FN): for a spam filter, the FP of sending legitimate mail to the spam folder is more expensive, and for cancer diagnosis, the FN of missing a patient is.

### What are the Confusion Matrix and Macro vs Micro Average?
A: The Confusion Matrix lays out actual Classes vs. predicted Classes in a table showing TP·FP·FN·TN, and it's the starting point for every Classification Metric. In Multi-Class, the Macro Average computes each Class's Metric separately and takes a simple average, so small Classes count equally, while the Micro Average pools the TP·FP·FN of all Classes before computing, so it's dominated by large Classes; in Single-Label Multi-Class, Micro-F1 equals Accuracy.

### What are the ROC Curve and AUC? When do you use the PR Curve?
E: Turn up the sensitivity of an airport security scanner and it catches more dangerous items (TPR ↑), but it also beeps at more harmless bags (FPR ↑). ROC is the graph of that trade-off as you turn the sensitivity from one end to the other.
A: The ROC Curve plots FPR (False Positive Rate) on the x-axis and TPR (True Positive Rate = Recall) on the y-axis as the Classification Threshold changes, and AUC is the area under it: "the probability that a random positive gets a higher score than a random negative." 0.5 is random, and 1 is perfect ranking. When positives are very rare, the enormous number of TNs makes the FPR look small and the ROC optimistic, so the PR Curve, which plots Precision-Recall, and PR-AUC are more honest.

### What is Cross-Validation, and how should you do it?
E: If you take a practice test only once, you can't tell whether it was luck or skill. Splitting the workbook into 5 parts, setting aside one part at a time as the test, and averaging over 5 tries is more trustworthy.
A: k-Fold Cross-Validation splits the data into k groups (Folds), trains on k−1 and validates on the remaining 1, repeats this k times, and looks at the mean and spread, so no data is wasted and luck plays a smaller role in the evaluation. For Classification, use Stratified k-Fold, which preserves Class proportions; when the same user must not appear on both sides, use Group k-Fold; and for Time Series, use TimeSeriesSplit, which trains on the past and validates on the future. Cross-Validation is used for Model Selection·Hyperparameter Tuning, and final performance is measured only once with a Test Set that's never been used.

## Generalization and Regularization

#### The Bias-Variance Trade-off
ONE: A model's error splits into **error from being too simple (Bias)** and **error from being too sensitive (Variance)**. Raising complexity lowers Bias and raises Variance, so you have to find **the balance point in between**.
LIKE: If you draw a winding road with a straight ruler, you're always wrong in about the same way (Bias). If you draw a squiggly line that passes through every point, its shape changes a lot every time a new point arrives (Variance).
STEP:
1. **Test error = Bias² + Variance + irreducible noise**.
2. **Bias**: Systematic error that arises because the model is too simple to capture the real relationship. Like fitting a curved relationship with a straight line.
3. **Variance**: Error that arises because the model is so complex it follows even the noise in the training data, so its predictions swing a lot when the data changes even slightly.
4. **Irreducible noise**: The randomness in the data itself. No model can reduce it.
5. Make the model more complex and Bias↓ Variance↑; make it simpler and Bias↑ Variance↓.
6. So you look for **the complexity with the lowest Validation error**.
END: Too simple means Bias, too complex means Variance. Find the balance point where Validation error is lowest.

#### Overfitting and Underfitting
ONE: **Overfitting** is **memorizing** the training data and doing poorly on new data, and **Underfitting** is being **too simple** to fit even the training data.
LIKE: Overfitting is the student who memorized the answers to past exams wholesale and scores 100 on them but can't solve new problems. Underfitting is the student who didn't study enough and can't even solve the past exams.
STEP:
1. **How to tell**: Low Training error but high Validation error is Overfitting. Both high is Underfitting.
2. **Learning Curve**: Plotting the two errors against the amount of data or the number of training passes (Epochs) shows which state you're in.
3. **Fixing Overfitting**: Collect·expand more data (Augmentation), simplify the model, Regularization (L1·L2·Dropout), stop when Validation error starts rising (Early Stopping), combine several models (Ensemble).
4. **Fixing Underfitting**: A more complex model, add better Features, reduce Regularization, train longer.
END: Memorized means Overfitting (simplify·Regularization); didn't learn means Underfitting (more complex·add Features).

#### L1 · L2 Regularization
ONE: A method that adds **a penalty on the size of the Weights** to the Loss to keep the model from becoming overly complex. **L2** makes all Weights evenly small, and **L1** makes some Weights **exactly 0**.
LIKE: It's taxing the Weights. L2 is a progressive tax where "bigger values pay more," so as a value gets small the tax almost disappears, and it stops just short of 0. L1 is "the same tax per unit no matter how much you have," so useless Weights get cleared out to 0 entirely.
STEP:
1. **The idea**: With large Weights, a small change in the input causes a big change in the output (sensitivity = Variance). The penalty keeps the Weights small.
2. **L2 (Ridge)**: Loss + λΣw². Makes Weights evenly small and stabilizes Features that are similar to each other (correlated).
3. **L1 (Lasso)**: Loss + λΣ|w|. Makes some Weights **exactly 0**, automatically removing unneeded Features (**Feature Selection**, Sparsity).
4. **Why does only L1 make zeros?** The slope of L2's penalty is 2λw, so the push weakens as w approaches 0. L1's slope always has magnitude λ, so it keeps pushing even small Weights all the way to 0.
5. **λ (regularization strength)**: Large gives a simple model (risk of Underfitting); small gives a complex model (risk of Overfitting). Choose it with Cross-Validation.
6. **Elastic Net**: A mix of L1 and L2.
KEY: From the statistical viewpoint, L2 is MAP with a normal-distribution Prior on the Weights, and L1 is MAP with a Laplace Prior (topic 13).
END: L2 makes everything evenly small; L1 makes useless ones 0. Both are taxes on the Weights.

### What is the Bias-Variance Trade-off?
E: If you draw a winding road with a straight ruler, you're always wrong in about the same way (Bias), and if you draw a squiggly line that passes through every point, its shape changes a lot every time a new point arrives (Variance).
A: The expected Test error splits into Bias² + Variance + irreducible noise (Irreducible Error). A simple model can't capture the real relationship, so its Bias is large, and a complex model follows even the noise in the Training Data, so its Variance, the amount its predictions swing when the data changes, is large. Raising complexity lowers Bias and raises Variance, so you look for the point with the lowest Validation error.

### How do you detect and fix Overfitting and Underfitting?
A: Low Training error but large Validation error is Overfitting, and both high is Underfitting, which you check with a Learning Curve (the two errors against the amount of data·Epochs). Overfitting is fixed with more data·Augmentation, simplifying the model, Regularization (L1·L2·Dropout), Early Stopping, and Ensembles; Underfitting is fixed with a more complex model, adding good Features, relaxing Regularization, and training longer.

### What are L1 and L2 Regularization? Why does L1 make Weights 0?
E: It taxes the Weights. L2 is a progressive tax where "bigger values pay more," so as a value gets small the tax almost disappears and it stops just short of 0; L1 is "the same tax per unit no matter how much you have," so useless Weights get cleared out to 0 entirely.
A: L2 (Ridge) adds λΣw² to make Weights evenly small and stabilize correlated Features, and L1 (Lasso) adds λΣ|w| to make some Weights exactly 0, producing a Feature Selection effect (Sparsity). The reason is that L2's slope 2λw weakens as w approaches 0, while L1's slope always has a constant magnitude λ and keeps pushing small Weights all the way to 0; geometrically, the L1 constraint region is a diamond with corners on the axes, so the Loss contours tend to touch it first at a corner (where some coordinates are 0). Mixing the two gives Elastic Net.

## Linear Models

#### Linear Regression (OLS)
ONE: The most basic model, which explains the relationship between inputs and outcome **with a straight line (plane)**. It finds the line with **the smallest sum of squared errors**.
LIKE: It's laying a ruler over data on study hours and test scores, and placing the ruler so that the sum of the squared vertical distances from every point to the ruler is as small as possible.
STEP:
1. **Model**: ŷ = w₁x₁ + w₂x₂ + … + b. Multiply each Feature by a Weight, add them up, and add the intercept b.
2. **Loss**: The sum of squared errors Σ(yᵢ − ŷᵢ)². Minimizing this is **OLS (Ordinary Least Squares)**.
3. **Solving directly with a formula**: Differentiating and setting it to 0 gives the Normal Equation XᵀXβ = Xᵀy, and β̂ = (XᵀX)⁻¹Xᵀy (the Least Squares of topic 12).
4. **With a single Feature**: slope = Cov(x, y) / Var(x), intercept = ȳ − slope × x̄.
5. When the data is very large, it's solved with Gradient Descent instead of the formula.
6. **Gauss-Markov Theorem**: If the errors have mean 0, constant variance, and no correlation with each other, OLS has the smallest variance among linear unbiased estimators (**BLUE**).
PRO: Fast and simple, and the Weights can be interpreted as "how much the outcome rises when this Feature goes up by 1."
CON: It doesn't fit when the real relationship is curved (transform the Features or use another model). It's sensitive to Outliers.
END: Linear Regression is a model that finds the line with the smallest sum of squared errors, and it's a powerful, easy-to-interpret baseline.

#### Logistic Regression
ONE: A model that computes a score with a linear formula, turns it **into a 0–1 probability with a Sigmoid**, and classifies with a Threshold. Its name says Regression, but it's a **Classification** model.
LIKE: Up to computing the score, it's the same as Linear Regression; then it squeezes that score into a 0–100% probability and draws a line like "over 50% means spam."
STEP:
1. **Compute the score**: z = wᵀx + b. It can be infinitely large or small.
2. **Sigmoid**: σ(z) = 1 / (1 + e⁻ᶻ). It squeezes any z into the range 0–1. If z = 0, it's 0.5.
3. **Classify**: If the probability is larger than the Threshold (usually 0.5), it's positive.
4. **Why Regression?** It gets the name because it's structured as a Regression of the Log-Odds log(p / (1 − p)) on a linear formula.
5. **Training**: Minimizes **Binary Cross-Entropy** −[y log p + (1 − y) log(1 − p)]. The lower the probability it gives the correct answer, the bigger the penalty.
6. This Loss is **Convex (bowl-shaped)**, so Gradient Descent finds the global minimum. Using MSE combined with the Sigmoid makes it non-Convex.
USE: A powerful baseline for binary classification like spam filters, click prediction, and loan approval. It's fast even on sparse word Features with hundreds of thousands of dimensions, outputs probabilities so the Threshold can be adjusted, and can explain its reasons with per-word Weights.
END: Logistic Regression = linear score → Sigmoid probability → Threshold classification. Trained with Cross-Entropy.

#### Softmax Regression (Multi-Class)
ONE: With several Classes, a model that computes a score for each Class and turns them **into probabilities summing to 1 with Softmax** to classify. The last layer of a Neural Network Classifier is exactly this structure.
LIKE: It's like a class president election where each candidate gets a support score, and those scores are turned into "chances of winning" that add up to 100%.
STEP:
1. For each Class k, compute a score (**Logit**) zₖ = wₖᵀx + bₖ.
2. **Softmax**: pₖ = e^(zₖ) / Σⱼ e^(zⱼ). Exponentiating makes everything positive, and dividing by the total makes them probabilities.
3. The bigger the score, the bigger the probability, and all the probabilities sum to 1.
4. Pick the Class with the highest probability as the answer.
5. **Training**: Minimizes the Cross-Entropy Loss −log p(correct Class).
6. With 2 Classes, Softmax Regression is the same as Logistic Regression.
END: Softmax = turning scores into probabilities that sum to 1. The foundation of multi-Class classification.

### What is the formula for OLS (Ordinary Least Squares) Regression?
A: OLS finds the β that minimizes the sum of squared Residuals Σ(yᵢ − xᵢᵀβ)² = ‖y − Xβ‖²; differentiating and setting it to 0 gives the Normal Equation XᵀXβ = Xᵀy, and if XᵀX is Invertible, β̂ = (XᵀX)⁻¹Xᵀy (the same formula as Least Squares in 12 Linear Algebra). In Simple Regression, slope = Cov(x, y)/Var(x) and intercept = ȳ − slope·x̄. If the errors have mean 0 and are Homoscedastic·uncorrelated, by the Gauss-Markov Theorem it has the smallest variance among linear unbiased estimators (BLUE).

### Why does Logistic Regression do Classification when it's called "Regression"? What about the Sigmoid and Cross-Entropy?
E: Up to computing the score, it's the same as Linear Regression; then it squeezes that score into a 0–100% probability and draws a line like "over 50% means spam."
A: Logistic Regression regresses the Log-Odds log(p/(1−p)) on a linear formula of the Features wᵀx + b, hence the name Regression, and it makes a probability with the Sigmoid σ(z) = 1/(1 + e⁻ᶻ) and then classifies with a Threshold. Training minimizes Binary Cross-Entropy −[y log p + (1−y) log(1−p)], the Negative Log-Likelihood, and this Loss is Convex, so Gradient Descent reaches the Global Optimum. Using MSE combined with the Sigmoid makes it non-Convex.

### Why is Logistic Regression used so much in spam filters?
A: Training·prediction are very fast even on Sparse word Features with hundreds of thousands of dimensions, and L1·L2 Regularization can prevent Overfitting. Because it outputs probabilities, the Threshold can be set high to avoid "sending legitimate mail to the spam folder," it can explain itself with per-word Weights like "free·winner pulled it toward spam," and it's easy to update with Online Learning every time new mail arrives.

### What is Softmax Regression for Multi-Class Classification?
A: For each Class k, it computes a score (Logit) zₖ = wₖᵀx + bₖ, makes probabilities summing to 1 with Softmax pₖ = e^(zₖ) / Σⱼ e^(zⱼ), and trains with Cross-Entropy. With 2 Classes it's the same as Logistic Regression, and the last Layer of a Neural Network Classifier is exactly this structure.

## Probabilistic Classifiers

#### Naive Bayes
ONE: A model that classifies with Bayes' Theorem but makes the simple (Naive) assumption that **"once the Class is fixed, the Features are independent of each other."** It's fast and strong at text classification.
LIKE: "Free" and "winner" tend to show up together in spam, but Naive Bayes deliberately makes the simplifying assumption that "each word gives no clue about the others." Thanks to that, the computation becomes extremely easy.
STEP:
1. **Goal**: Find P(spam | the words in the mail).
2. **Bayes**: P(spam | words) ∝ P(words | spam) × P(spam).
3. **The Naive assumption**: Treat the words as independent and split P(words | spam) = P(word₁ | spam) × P(word₂ | spam) × ….
4. Each probability just requires counting "how many times did this word appear in spam mail" in the training data.
5. **Laplace Smoothing**: If a word never seen in spam during training shows up, its probability becomes 0, and the whole product becomes 0. So you add 1 to every word Count.
6. Multiplying small probabilities countless times makes them vanish to 0 (Underflow), so in practice Log probabilities are added.
PRO: Training is just Counting, so it's very fast, easily updated with new data, stable even with little data, and a strong baseline on high-dimensional text. Per-word contributions are easy to interpret too.
CON: Because the independence assumption is wrong, the probability values themselves tend to come out extreme. But classification only cares about "which Class's score is bigger," so it works well in practice.
END: Naive Bayes = Bayes + the "Features are independent of each other" assumption. Simple but fast and strong on text.

#### Generative Model vs Discriminative Model
ONE: A **Generative Model** classifies by learning **what each Class's data looks like**, while a **Discriminative Model** learns only **the boundary that separates** the Classes.
LIKE: A Generative Model learns cats and dogs well enough to draw each one, then judges "which drawing is this photo closer to?" A Discriminative Model learns only the boundary line between the two.
STEP:
1. **Generative**: Learns P(x | y) and P(y) to model the distribution of the whole data P(x, y), and computes P(y | x) with Bayes.
2. Since it knows what the data looks like, it can also generate new data. Examples: Naive Bayes, GMM, HMM, and image·text generation models.
3. **Discriminative**: Learns P(y | x) or the boundary directly. Examples: Logistic Regression, SVM, most Neural Network Classifiers.
4. **Tendency**: Generative models make strong assumptions and learn quickly when data is scarce, while Discriminative models tend to have lower classification error when data is plentiful.
END: Learning what the data looks like is Generative (it can also create); learning only the boundary is Discriminative (focused on classification).

### What is the "Naive" assumption of Naive Bayes? Why is Laplace Smoothing needed?
E: "Free" and "winner" tend to show up together in spam, but Naive Bayes deliberately makes the simplifying assumption that "each word gives no clue about the others." Also, if a word never seen in spam during training shows up, its probability becomes 0 and the whole product becomes 0, so every word gets "1 by default" added.
A: Naive Bayes is a Classifier that assumes the Features are Conditionally Independent given the Class and computes P(y | x) ∝ P(y)ΠᵢP(xᵢ | y). Laplace (Add-One) Smoothing adds 1 to every Count, as in P(w | c) = (count(w, c) + 1) / (Σcount(c) + |V|), to keep a word unseen in training from having probability 0 and zeroing out the whole product. The actual computation is done as a sum of Log Probabilities to avoid Underflow.

### Make the case for Naive Bayes, an old technique.
A: Training is just Counting, so it's fast enough to finish in a single pass over the data, and Incremental updates with new data are easy. It has few Parameters to estimate, so it's stable even with little data, and it delivers strong Baseline performance on high-dimensional text with tens of thousands of words. Because the independence assumption is wrong, the probability values themselves tend to be extreme (Poorly Calibrated), but classification only cares about which Class's score is bigger, so it works well in practice, and "which words contributed how much" is easy to interpret.

### What is the difference between a Generative Model and a Discriminative Model?
E: A Generative Model learns cats and dogs well enough to draw each one, then judges "which drawing is this photo closer to?", while a Discriminative Model learns only the boundary line between the two.
A: A Generative Model learns the Joint Distribution P(x, y) = P(x | y)P(y) and classifies with Bayes' Theorem, and can also create new data (Naive Bayes, GMM, HMM), while a Discriminative Model learns P(y | x) or the Decision Boundary directly (Logistic Regression, SVM). In general, Generative Models make strong assumptions and converge quickly when data is scarce, while Discriminative Models tend to have lower classification error when data is plentiful.

## SVM and Kernels

#### SVM: The Boundary with the Widest Margin
ONE: Among the boundaries that separate two Classes, an **SVM** finds the one where **the distance to the nearest points (the Margin) is largest**. The boundary is determined only by the few nearest points (**Support Vectors**).
LIKE: To build the widest possible road between two neighborhoods, you only need to look at the few houses right up against the road. Those houses are the Support Vectors, and the road's width is the Margin. Houses far away don't affect where the road goes.
STEP:
1. There can be countless lines (Hyperplanes in higher dimensions) that separate two Classes.
2. Among them, an SVM picks the line with **the widest Margin**, the distance to the nearest point from either Class.
3. A wide Margin leaves less room for misclassification even if new data wobbles a bit, which helps Generalization.
4. The boundary is determined only by the points touching the Margin (**Support Vectors**). It's less swayed by distant Outliers.
5. **Soft Margin**: For data that can't be separated perfectly, some errors are allowed. A large **C** strictly prevents errors (narrower Margin), and a small one tolerates them generously (wider Margin).
6. It's **Convex Optimization**, so it always finds the global optimum.
END: SVM = building the widest road. Only the houses against the road (Support Vectors) determine the boundary.

#### The Kernel Trick: Separating by Raising the Dimension
ONE: Even data that can't be separated by a straight line can be separated by a plane **once sent to a higher dimension**. The **Kernel Trick** does **the computation in that space directly**, without actually transforming the data.
LIKE: If red balls sit in the middle of a desk and blue balls sit around them in a ring, no single straight line can separate them. Smack the desk so only the red balls in the middle pop up into the air (a new dimension: height), and a single flat sheet of paper can separate them.
STEP:
1. Lots of data can't be separated by a straight line in its original space (like inside a circle vs. outside).
2. Adding Features to send it to a higher dimension (e.g., adding a new Feature x² + y²) often makes it separable by a plane (**Cover's Theorem**).
3. But actually transforming into a high dimension makes the computation explode.
4. **The key observation**: SVM computations only need the Dot Products between data points.
5. A **Kernel Function** K(x, z) computes the Dot Product in the high-dimensional space directly in the original space. You get the effect without the transformation.
6. **Common Kernels**: RBF (Gaussian, the most common, closer means more similar), Polynomial.
PRO: Works well even in high dimensions (text), and draws nonlinear boundaries too.
CON: With hundreds of thousands of Samples or more, Kernel computation is slow, Kernel·C·γ tuning is needed, and it doesn't give probabilities directly.
END: Lift it to a higher dimension and it separates; the Kernel Trick gets that effect without the cost of lifting.

### What are the Margin, Support Vectors, and the Kernel Trick?
E: To build the widest possible road between two neighborhoods, you only need to look at the few houses right up against the road. Those houses are the Support Vectors, and the road's width is the Margin.
A: An SVM (Support Vector Machine) finds, among the Hyperplanes separating two Classes, the one with the largest distance to the nearest point (Margin), and the boundary is determined only by the Support Vectors, the points on the Margin. When the data can't be separated perfectly, a Soft Margin that allows some errors is used, with C controlling how much is allowed. The Kernel Trick computes the Dot Product in a high-dimensional space directly with a Kernel Function K(x, z) (RBF, Polynomial), without actually transforming the data into that space.

### Why does an SVM work by expanding dimensions? Why is SVM good?
E: If red balls sit in the middle of a desk and blue balls sit around them in a ring, no single straight line can separate them. Smack the desk so only the red balls in the middle pop up into the air (a new dimension: height), and a single flat sheet of paper can separate them.
A: Because data that isn't Linearly Separable in its original space becomes more likely to be linearly separable when sent to a higher dimension (Cover's Theorem), and thanks to the Kernel Trick, it doesn't pay that cost directly. It's good because maximizing the Margin helps Generalization, it's Convex Optimization so the Global Optimum is guaranteed, it works well even in high dimensions (text), and it looks only at the Support Vectors so it's less swayed by distant Outliers. Its drawbacks are that Kernel computation is slow with hundreds of thousands of samples or more, Kernel·C·γ tuning is needed, and it doesn't give probabilities directly.

## Trees and Ensembles

#### Decision Tree
ONE: A model that predicts by asking **"yes/no" questions one after another** to split the data. At each step, it picks **the question that splits most cleanly**.
LIKE: It's like Twenty Questions, where you start with the question that splits the candidates most cleanly in one go, like "Is it alive?"
STEP:
1. Starting at the root, try every possible Feature and threshold ("age ≤ 30?").
2. After splitting, measure how "pure (a single Class)" each group became. Pick the question that reduces impurity (**Impurity**) the most (Greedy).
3. **Impurity criteria**: For Classification, **Gini Impurity** 1 − Σpₖ² (CART) or **Entropy** (Information Gain, topic 13); for Regression, reduction in variance (MSE).
4. Repeat the same process for each resulting group.
5. Left alone, it grows until each Leaf holds a single Sample and memorizes the training data (Overfitting).
6. So you limit the maximum depth or the minimum Samples per Leaf, or cut branches after growing it fully (**Pruning**).
PRO: The rules can be read as-is, so it's easy to interpret, needs no Scaling, and handles numeric·categorical Features together.
CON: On its own it overfits easily, and its structure changes a lot when the data changes even slightly (High Variance).
END: Decision Tree = Twenty Questions that asks the best-splitting question first. Easy to interpret but unstable on its own.

#### Ensemble: Bagging · Boosting · Stacking
ONE: Methods that **combine** several models to be more accurate than one. **Bagging** builds many independently and votes, **Boosting** fixes earlier mistakes in sequence, and **Stacking** puts a model that makes the call on top of several models.
LIKE: Bagging is going with the majority answer of many audience members on a quiz show, Boosting is a relay where each next person focuses on the questions the previous person got wrong, and Stacking is having a team lead who hears several experts' opinions and makes the final call.
STEP:
1. **Bagging (Bootstrap Aggregating)**: Trains several models **independently·in parallel** on different data made by drawing with replacement (Bootstrap), and combines them by averaging or voting. Individual errors cancel out, so it mainly **reduces Variance**.
2. **Random Forest**: Bagging + having each Tree split look at only a random subset of Features, making the Trees more different from each other. The more different they are, the bigger the effect when combined.
3. **Boosting**: Adds weak models **sequentially**, fixing what the earlier models got wrong. It mainly **reduces Bias**.
4. AdaBoost raises the weights of misclassified Samples, and in **Gradient Boosting** the next Tree learns the remaining error (the Negative Gradient of the Loss).
5. **Stacking**: Trains a Meta Model that takes several models' predictions as input and makes the final prediction.
6. Combining 50 small Trees is usually better than one big Tree. But you lose the interpretability of reading the rules as-is, as with a single Tree.
| Method | How it trains | Mainly reduces | Example |
| Bagging | Independent·parallel | Variance | Random Forest |
| Boosting | Sequentially fixing errors | Bias | XGBoost, LightGBM |
| Stacking | Meta Model on top of models | Depends on the situation | Top competition solutions |
END: Voting among many independent models is Bagging (Variance↓); fixing mistakes in sequence is Boosting (Bias↓).

#### Why Random Forest and XGBoost Are Strong in Practice
ONE: On tabular (Tabular) data, **Tree-based Ensembles** are strong without Scaling and need little tuning, so they're often better than or comparable to Neural Networks. **Random Forest** is easy to parallelize, and **XGBoost** is highly accurate.
LIKE: For spreadsheet-like data, a team of skilled Twenty Questions players (a Tree Ensemble) is often more efficient than a giant neural-network brain.
STEP:
1. **Random Forest's parallelism**: The Trees are independent of each other, so 100 Servers can each build Trees and just gather them at the end. Almost no communication is needed (Embarrassingly Parallel).
2. A Neural Network's Gradient Descent is sequential, so distributed training has to synchronize Gradients at every step, and it usually needs GPUs.
3. **XGBoost**: An implementation that makes Gradient Boosting fast and accurate. It has an approximation that uses the Loss's second derivative too, Regularization on Tree complexity, a Learning Rate (Shrinkage), Row·Column Sampling, the ability to learn on its own which way to send missing values, and parallel optimization.
4. So it delivers high accuracy on Tabular data quickly and resists Overfitting, which is why it was often used at the top of Kaggle. Today it's used alongside LightGBM and CatBoost.
END: Tree Ensembles are strong on tabular data. Try Random Forest, which is easy to parallelize, and XGBoost, which is highly accurate, first.

### How does a Decision Tree choose its Split criterion?
E: It's like Twenty Questions, where you start with the question that splits the candidates most cleanly in one go, like "Is it alive?"
A: At each Node, it tries every possible Feature and threshold and Greedily picks the Split that most reduces the weighted average Impurity of the resulting Child Nodes. For Classification it uses Gini Impurity 1 − Σpₖ² (CART) or Entropy (Information Gain, ID3·C4.5), and for Regression, reduction in Variance (MSE). Left alone, it grows all the way and overfits, so Max Depth·Min Samples limits or Pruning are applied.

### What Ensemble methods are there?
E: Bagging is going with the majority answer of many audience members on a quiz show, Boosting is a relay where each next person focuses on the questions the previous person got wrong, and Stacking is having a team lead who hears several experts' opinions and makes the final call.
A: Bagging (Bootstrap Aggregating) trains several models independently·in parallel on Bootstrap samples and averages·votes, so it mainly reduces Variance, and Random Forest adds having each Split look at only a random subset of Features, lowering the correlation between Trees further. Boosting adds weak models (Weak Learners) sequentially, fixing the earlier models' errors, so it mainly reduces Bias (AdaBoost raises the weights of misclassified samples, and Gradient Boosting learns the Negative Gradient of the Loss), and Stacking trains a Meta Model with several models' predictions as input.

### Are 50 small Decision Trees better than one big Decision Tree?
A: Usually, yes. One big Tree is a High-Variance model that easily overfits the Training Data and whose structure changes a lot when the data changes slightly, but pooling the predictions of 50 Trees built on different data·Features lets individual errors cancel out and reduces Variance (Bagging), and stacking small Trees sequentially can reduce Bias too (Boosting). However, if the Trees are similar to each other, the effect is small, and you lose the Interpretability of reading the rules as-is, as with a single Tree.

### With 100 Servers, why use Random Forest rather than a Neural Network?
A: Random Forest's Trees are trained independently of each other, so it's a parallel task needing almost no communication (Embarrassingly Parallel): 100 Servers can each build Trees and just gather them at the end. A Neural Network's Gradient Descent is sequential, so Distributed Training has to synchronize Gradients at every step, and it usually needs GPUs. Also, on tabular (Tabular) data, Tree-based models are strong without Scaling and need little tuning, so they're often better than or comparable to Neural Networks.

### Do you know XGBoost? Why is it famous on Kaggle?
A: XGBoost is an implementation that makes Gradient Boosting fast and accurate, featuring an approximation that uses the Loss's second derivative too, Regularization on the number of Leaves and the Weights, a Learning Rate (Shrinkage) and Row·Column Sampling, Splits that learn the default direction for missing values on their own, and parallel·Cache-optimized Split search. So it delivers high accuracy on Tabular data quickly and resists Overfitting, which is why it was often used in top Kaggle solutions, and today it's used alongside LightGBM·CatBoost.

## Unsupervised Learning

#### K-means Clustering and Its Limits
ONE: **K-means** splits data into **K groups** by repeatedly assigning each point to **the nearest center (Centroid)** and recomputing the centers. It's simple and fast but assumes **round groups**.
LIKE: Say you're splitting students scattered across a field into K teams. Place K team captains anywhere, and the students go to the nearest captain. Each captain then moves to the middle of their team. Repeat this and the teams settle down. But the teams always end up shaped like round balls, so a group stretched out along a river doesn't get split well.
STEP:
1. **Choose K**: You have to decide in advance how many groups to split into.
2. **Initial centers**: Pick K centers. Picking at random can lead to bad results, so **k-means++**, which picks them far apart from each other, is used.
3. **Assign**: Put each point in the group of its nearest center.
4. **Update**: Move each center to the mean position of its group.
5. Repeat 3–4 until the assignments stop changing.
6. **Limits**: It assumes **round** groups of similar size, so it splits elongated or crescent-shaped groups wrongly. It must put every point into some group, so it can't filter out noise separately. The mean gets pulled by Outliers. Results vary with the initial values, and it's sensitive to Feature Scale.
KEY: To choose K, increase K and look for the point where the sum of within-group distances bends (Elbow Method), or look at the Silhouette Score.
END: K-means = repeatedly assigning to the nearest center and moving the centers. Fast, but only finds round groups well.

#### Other Clustering: Hierarchical · DBSCAN · GMM
ONE: Methods that make up for K-means' limits. **Hierarchical** finds a hierarchy without K, **DBSCAN** uses density to find arbitrary shapes and noise, and **GMM** uses probability to find elliptical groups.
LIKE: Hierarchical is pairing up the closest people, then those pairs gather again… building a tree like a family tree. DBSCAN treats places where people are packed tightly as groups and leaves out people standing alone as "noise."
STEP:
1. **Hierarchical Clustering**: Repeatedly merges the two closest groups to build a tree (Dendrogram). You don't decide K in advance; you cut the tree at whatever height you want. But it needs O(n²) Memory, which makes it hard for large data.
2. **DBSCAN**: Connects "dense" points that have at least minPts neighbors within radius ε into groups. It finds groups of arbitrary shape and classifies points that belong nowhere as noise. No K needed. It struggles when groups of different densities are mixed.
3. **GMM (Gaussian Mixture Model)**: Treats the data as a sum of several Gaussian (bell-shaped) distributions and trains with the EM Algorithm. It represents elliptical groups and gives the **probability** that each point belongs to each group (Soft Assignment).
4. K-means can be seen as a special case of GMM where every group has the same round shape and each point is assigned to just one group instead of given probabilities.
| Method | Pros | Cons |
| Hierarchical | No K needed, hierarchical structure | Slow on large data |
| DBSCAN | Arbitrary shapes, detects noise, no K needed | Struggles with groups of different density |
| GMM | Elliptical, membership probabilities | Needs K, sensitive to initial values |
END: Curious about hierarchy, Hierarchical; odd shapes with noise, DBSCAN; need probabilistic membership, GMM.

#### Association Rules: Support · Confidence · Lift
ONE: A method for finding **patterns that occur together**, like "people who buy A also buy B." **Support** measures how common it is, **Confidence** how reliable it is, and **Lift** how much more often than chance.
LIKE: If 20 out of 100 grocery receipts bought bread and milk together, Support is 20%; if 20 of the 40 receipts with bread also have milk, Confidence is 50%. But if 50% of people buy milk anyway, bread didn't increase milk purchases at all (Lift 1).
STEP:
1. **Support(A → B)** = P(A ∩ B). The fraction where both appear together. Combinations that are too rare are meaningless.
2. **Confidence(A → B)** = P(B | A) = P(A ∩ B) / P(A). The probability that someone who bought A also buys B.
3. **The Confidence trap**: If B is very common to begin with (milk), any A gets a high Confidence.
4. **Lift(A → B)** = Confidence / P(B) = P(A ∩ B) / (P(A)P(B)). How many times A multiplies the probability of B.
5. Lift > 1 means they sell well together (positive association), = 1 means they're unrelated (independent), and < 1 means they actually tend not to sell together.
6. **Apriori Algorithm**: Finds rules quickly by pruning candidates using the property that if a combination has low Support, any bigger combination containing it is low too.
USE: Market basket analysis ("frequently bought together"), store shelf layout, planning bundle discounts.
END: Support is commonness, Confidence is conditional probability, Lift is the multiple over chance. Only Lift > 1 means a real association.

### What is the main semantic drawback of K-means? (Not computational cost)
E: K-means is "people close to a center form one team," so the teams always end up shaped like round balls. It splits a town stretched along a river or a crescent-shaped group in odd ways.
A: You have to decide K in advance, and because it assigns points to Centroids by Euclidean Distance, it assumes round (spherical·convex) Clusters of similar size, so it splits elongated or non-convex shapes and Clusters of different density·size wrongly. It must put every point into some Cluster, so it can't classify noise (Noise) separately, the mean gets pulled by Outliers, it falls into Local Optima where results vary with initial values (mitigated by k-means++), and it's sensitive to Feature Scale.

### How do Hierarchical Clustering, DBSCAN, and GMM differ from K-means?
| Method | Approach | Advantage over K-means | Drawback |
| Hierarchical Clustering | Merge nearby Clusters to build a tree (Dendrogram) | No need to fix K in advance; cut after viewing the tree | O(n²) Memory, unsuited to large scale |
| DBSCAN | Connect dense regions with at least minPts neighbors within radius ε | Arbitrary shapes, Noise detection, no K needed | Struggles when Clusters of different density mix |
| GMM (Gaussian Mixture Model) | Treat data as a sum of Gaussians, trained with EM | Elliptical Clusters, membership probabilities (Soft Assignment) | Needs K, sensitive to initial values |
A: Hierarchical Clustering shows the hierarchy of Clusters without K, DBSCAN is Density-based so it finds Clusters of arbitrary shape and Noise, and GMM gives the probability that each point belongs to each Cluster and represents elliptical Clusters. K-means can be seen as a special case of GMM where every Cluster has the same spherical variance and each point is assigned to just one Cluster instead of given probabilities (Hard Assignment).

### What are Support, Confidence, and Lift in Association Rules?
E: If 20 out of 100 grocery receipts bought bread and milk together, Support is 20%; if 20 of the 40 receipts with bread also have milk, Confidence is 50%. But if 50% of people buy milk anyway, bread didn't increase milk purchases at all (Lift 1).
A: For a rule A → B, Support = P(A ∩ B) is the fraction where both appear together, Confidence = P(B | A) = P(A ∩ B)/P(A) is the probability that someone who bought A also buys B, and Lift = Confidence/P(B) = P(A ∩ B)/(P(A)P(B)) is how many times A multiplies the probability of B. Lift > 1 means a positive association, = 1 means Independent, and < 1 means a negative association, and the Apriori Algorithm reduces candidates by Pruning combinations with low Support.

## Dimensionality and Dimensionality Reduction

#### The Curse of Dimensionality
ONE: The phenomenon where, as the number of Features (dimensions) grows, the space **expands exponentially**, the data becomes **nearly empty**, distances lose meaning, and learning gets harder.
LIKE: Put 10 points on a line and they're packed close, but put the same 10 in a square, then a cube, and they end up in more and more empty space. With hundreds of dimensions, every point is far from every other, and the distances become almost the same.
STEP:
1. To fill 10 cells in 1 dimension, 10 points are enough. 2 dimensions (10×10) need 100, 3 dimensions need 1,000, and 10 dimensions need 10 billion.
2. So even with the same amount of data, as dimensions grow the data becomes extremely sparse (Sparse).
3. When it's sparse, the model easily memorizes even chance patterns (Overfitting).
4. In high dimensions, the difference between the distance to the nearest point and the distance to the farthest point shrinks. Everything ends up about equally far away.
5. So methods that rely on distance, like KNN and Clustering, lose their power.
6. **Remedies**: Pick only the Features you need (Feature Selection), reduce dimensions (Dimensionality Reduction), Regularization.
END: As dimensions grow, the space empties out and distances become meaningless. Respond by selecting Features or reducing dimensions.

#### Dimensionality Reduction and PCA
ONE: Methods that summarize many Features with **a small number of new axes**. The best known, **PCA**, finds mutually orthogonal axes starting from **the direction in which the data is most spread out** and keeps only the top few.
LIKE: Picture a football-shaped cloud of points. Looking along its long direction explains almost all of its shape. Even if you throw away the small wobbles in the thickness direction, the shape stays almost the same.
STEP:
1. **Feature Selection**: Pick some of the original Features (L1 Regularization, etc.).
2. **Feature Extraction**: Mix the original Features to make new axes. PCA is the classic example.
3. **The PCA process**: Shift the data to mean 0 (Centering), build the Covariance Matrix, and find its Eigenvectors (or use SVD). The Symmetric Matrices and SVD of topic 12 are used here.
4. The Eigenvectors in order of largest Eigenvalue are **the directions of largest variance (Principal Components)**. They're orthogonal to each other.
5. Keep only the top k axes and project the data onto them. Each point is represented with k numbers instead of d.
6. **Why it compresses**: Axes chosen this way give the smallest error when reconstructing the original data.
7. **Why it removes Noise**: The real signal gathers in a few directions of large variance, while random Noise is spread evenly and thinly across all directions. Throw away the small-variance axes and most of the Noise disappears with them.
KEY: Other methods: **LDA (Linear Discriminant Analysis)**, which maximizes Class separation; **t-SNE·UMAP**, nonlinear methods for visualization; and the **Autoencoder**, which compresses with a Neural Network.
END: PCA keeps the most spread-out directions and throws away the rest. That's why it's dimensionality reduction, compression, and Noise removal at once.

#### Extracting Topics from Text: LSA · LDA
ONE: Methods for finding **hidden themes (Topics)** across many documents. **LSA** **decomposes** the word-document Matrix **with SVD**, and **LDA (Latent Dirichlet Allocation)** solves the same goal **with a probability model**.
LIKE: Without reading thousands of news articles, it automatically finds bundles where "election·candidate·vote" often appear together (a politics Topic) and bundles where "goal·player·game" appear together (a sports Topic).
STEP:
1. **Preprocessing**: Lowercasing, splitting into words (Tokenization), removing common words with no meaning (Stopwords), converting to base forms (Lemmatization).
2. **Word-document Matrix**: Build a big Matrix recording word frequencies (BoW) or TF-IDF for each document (topic 16).
3. **LSA (Latent Semantic Analysis)**: Applies Truncated SVD to this Matrix to find latent axes of meaning (Topics). It's a Linear Algebra method.
4. **LDA (Latent Dirichlet Allocation)**: A probability model that treats a document as a mix of several Topics, and a Topic as a probability distribution over words. It evolved LSA → pLSA → LDA.
5. **Name warning**: LDA has two meanings. Latent Dirichlet Allocation in NLP (a Topic Model) and Linear Discriminant Analysis in statistics·classification (finding axes that separate Classes).
6. **Recent methods**: Turn sentences into Embeddings, cluster them, and extract key words for each group (the BERTopic approach).
7. The number of Topics is decided with the Coherence Score and human review, and Topics are named by looking at their top words and representative documents.
END: LSA finds hidden Topics with SVD, LDA with a probability model. These days Embedding + Clustering is widely used too.

### What is the Curse of Dimensionality?
E: Put 10 points on a line and they're packed close, but put the same 10 in a square·cube and they end up in more and more empty space. With hundreds of dimensions, every point is far from every other, and the distances become almost the same.
A: As dimensions grow, the volume of the space grows exponentially, so the data needed to maintain the same density grows exponentially, and the data becomes Sparse and easy to overfit. Also, the difference between the distance to the nearest point and the distance to the farthest point shrinks, so methods that depend on distance like KNN·Clustering lose their power. You respond with Feature Selection, Dimensionality Reduction, and Regularization.

### What Dimensionality Reduction techniques are there?
A: There's Feature Selection, which picks some of the original Features (Filter·Wrapper·Embedded approaches like L1), and Feature Extraction, which makes new axes. Extraction includes PCA, which preserves as much variance as possible; LDA (Linear Discriminant Analysis), a Supervised method that maximizes Class separation; Truncated SVD·NMF, which decompose the Matrix directly; t-SNE·UMAP, nonlinear methods for visualization; and the Autoencoder, which compresses with a Neural Network.

### PCA is a Dimensionality Reduction technique, but also a data compression and Noise removal technique. Why?
E: Looking at a football-shaped cloud of points along its long direction explains almost everything. Even if you throw away the small wobbles in the thickness direction, the shape stays almost the same.
A: PCA (Principal Component Analysis) uses the Eigenvectors of the Covariance Matrix of Centered data (or SVD) to find mutually Orthogonal axes (Principal Components) in order of largest variance, and keeps only the top k. The axes are chosen so that storing just k scores and the axes instead of d values gives the smallest Reconstruction Error (in the squared sense), which is why it compresses; and since the signal gathers in a few directions of large variance while random Noise is spread evenly and thinly across all directions, throwing away the small-variance axes removes most of the Noise along with them.

### What do LSA, LDA, and SVD mean, and how are they related?
A: SVD (Singular Value Decomposition) is a Linear Algebra tool that decomposes a Matrix into UΣVᵀ, and LSA (Latent Semantic Analysis) is a method that applies Truncated SVD to a Term-Document Matrix (TF-IDF) to find latent axes of meaning (Topics). LDA has two meanings: in NLP, it's Latent Dirichlet Allocation, a probabilistic Topic Model that treats a document as a mix of Topics and a Topic as a distribution over words, with a Dirichlet Prior; in statistics·classification, it's Linear Discriminant Analysis, which finds axes that separate Classes well. To sum up the relationship, LSA is a Linear Algebra method that finds Topics with SVD, and Latent Dirichlet Allocation is a later method that solves the same goal with a probability model (LSA → pLSA → LDA).

### You need to extract topics from a pile of text. How would you approach it?
A: First, preprocess with Normalization·Tokenization·Stopword removal·Lemmatization and build a BoW or TF-IDF Matrix. Then extract Topics with LDA·NMF·LSA, or build Sentence Embeddings, cluster them, and extract key words for each Cluster (the BERTopic approach). Decide the number of Topics with the Coherence Score and human review, name each Topic by looking at its top words·representative documents, and refine iteratively.

## Optimization

#### Gradient Descent and Local Minima
ONE: **Gradient Descent** finds the minimum by moving **a little at a time in the direction in which the Loss decreases most steeply**. If the terrain is bumpy, it can get stuck in **a small dip (Local Minimum)**.
LIKE: It's walking down a foggy mountain one step at a time, feeling only the slope under your feet. If you fall into a small dip, you might stop, thinking it's the lowest point, even though the lowest valley on the whole mountain is somewhere else.
STEP:
1. **Gradient**: The derivative of the Loss with respect to each Weight. It points in the direction of steepest **increase** (topic 12).
2. **Update**: θ ← θ − η × ∇Loss. Move by η (the **Learning Rate**) in the opposite direction of the Gradient.
3. **Learning Rate**: Too large and it overshoots the valley and bounces around; too small and it's far too slow.
4. **Global Minimum**: The lowest point overall. **Local Minimum**: A point lower than its surroundings but not the lowest overall.
5. **Saddle Point**: A point like a horse saddle, going down in some directions and up in others. The Gradient is 0 there, so descent can stall.
6. In high-dimensional Neural Networks, **Saddle Points are more of a problem** than bad Local Minima, and many Local Minima are known to perform about the same.
7. **Remedies**: The noise of Mini-Batches (SGD), Momentum, Learning Rate Schedules, and starting several times from different initial values (topic 15).
END: Gradient Descent walks down the slope. When the terrain is bumpy, watch out for dips and saddle points.

#### Convex Functions: Bowls That Make Optimization Easy
ONE: A **Convex Function** is **bowl-shaped**, so it has only one dip. That means if you just keep going down, you're guaranteed to arrive at **the global minimum**.
LIKE: Place a marble in a smooth bowl and, wherever it starts, it eventually rolls to the single point at the bottom. On a bumpy mountain it could get caught in a small dip, but a bowl has no such dips.
STEP:
1. **Definition**: The line segment connecting any two points on the graph always lies above the graph.
2. **Key property**: Every Local Minimum is a Global Minimum. A point where the Gradient is 0 is the optimal solution.
3. So Gradient Descent with a suitable Learning Rate is guaranteed to converge to the global optimum.
4. **Examples of Convex problems**: The MSE of Linear Regression, the Cross-Entropy of Logistic Regression, SVM.
5. **Non-Convex problems**: A Neural Network's Loss is bumpy, so the global optimum isn't guaranteed. Even so, in practice it finds good-enough solutions.
END: Convex = bowl-shaped = only one dip. Just keep going down and you'll reach the answer.

#### Newton's Method vs Gradient Descent
ONE: Gradient Descent looks only at **the slope (first derivative)** and goes down with a fixed step size, while **Newton's Method** also looks at **how curved it is (second derivative, the Hessian)**, computes where the bottom is, and takes a big leap.
LIKE: Gradient Descent is going down with a fixed step size while looking only at the slope under your feet; Newton's Method also looks at how curved the terrain is, calculates "the valley floor is about there," and leaps there in one big jump.
STEP:
1. **Gradient Descent**: θ ← θ − η∇f. Uses only the first derivative, so each computation is cheap. You have to pick a Learning Rate, and convergence can be slow.
2. **Newton's Method**: θ ← θ − H⁻¹∇f. Uses the Hessian H (second derivative) to converge very quickly near the optimum (Quadratic Convergence). It takes few iterations.
3. **Newton's problem**: With d Parameters, storing the Hessian takes O(d²) and inverting it takes O(d³). That's impossible for Neural Networks with hundreds of millions of Parameters.
4. Also, if the Hessian isn't Positive Definite, it can head toward a Saddle Point.
5. **A compromise: L-BFGS**: Approximates the Hessian cheaply. Widely used for medium-sized problems.
6. That's why Deep Learning uses the Gradient Descent family (SGD, Adam).
END: Looking only at the slope is Gradient Descent (cheap and scalable); also looking at curvature is Newton (fast but expensive).

### What are a Local Minimum and a Global Minimum?
E: If you just follow the downhill slope on a foggy mountain, you can get stuck in a small dip (Local Minimum) and never reach the lowest valley on the whole mountain (Global Minimum).
A: A Local Minimum is a point lower than its surroundings but not the lowest overall, and the Global Minimum is the point with the lowest Loss overall. For a Convex Function, every Local Minimum is the Global Minimum, but Neural Network Loss is Non-Convex, so Gradient Descent can settle at a Local Minimum or a Saddle Point. However, in high-dimensional Neural Networks, Saddle Points are known to be more of a problem than bad Local Minima, and many Local Minima perform about the same; the remedies are SGD's noise, Momentum, Learning Rate Schedules, and multiple Initializations.

### What are Newton's Method and Gradient Descent?
E: Gradient Descent is going down with a fixed step size while looking only at the slope under your feet, and Newton's Method also looks at how curved the terrain is (Curvature), calculates "the valley floor is about there," and leaps there in one big jump.
A: Gradient Descent, θ ← θ − η∇f(θ), uses only the first derivative, so each computation is cheap and it's used for large-scale models, but you have to pick the Learning Rate η, and convergence can be slow. Newton's Method, θ ← θ − H⁻¹∇f(θ), uses the Hessian (second derivative) and achieves Quadratic Convergence near the optimum with few iterations, but with d Parameters, storing the Hessian takes O(d²) and inverting it O(d³), and if the Hessian isn't Positive Definite it can head toward a Saddle Point. That's why Deep Learning uses the Gradient Descent family (Adam), and medium-sized problems use L-BFGS, which approximates the Hessian.

### Why is Optimization easy for Convex Functions?
A: A Convex Function is bowl-shaped, with the line segment connecting any two points on the graph always lying above the graph, so every Local Minimum is a Global Minimum and a point where the Gradient is 0 is the optimal solution. So Gradient Descent with a suitable Learning Rate is guaranteed to converge to the Global Optimum, and the MSE of Linear Regression, the Cross-Entropy of Logistic Regression, and SVM are Convex problems.

## Stochastic Processes and Recommendation

#### Markov Chains
ONE: A random process where **the next state depends only on the current state** (the past doesn't matter). Over a long time, it can also converge to **a fixed set of proportions (the Stationary Distribution)** regardless of where it started.
LIKE: It's a game of guessing tomorrow's weather from today's weather alone. "If it's sunny today, there's a 90% chance tomorrow is sunny too and a 10% chance of rain. If it rains today, there's a 50% chance tomorrow is sunny and a 50% chance it rains again." It doesn't care what yesterday or the day before was like.
STEP:
1. **States**: Sunny, rainy.
2. **Markov Property**: The probability of the next state depends only on the current state. It has no memory.
3. **Transition Matrix**: A table of the probabilities of moving between states.
4. Multiplying by this Matrix repeatedly lets you compute the weather probabilities several days later.
5. **Stationary Distribution**: In the example above, after a long time it converges to sunny 5/6, rainy 1/6, regardless of the starting weather. It's the balance point where the flow from sunny to rainy (5/6 × 0.1) equals the flow from rainy to sunny (1/6 × 0.5).
6. This distribution is the Eigenvector of the Transition Matrix for Eigenvalue 1 (topic 12).
USE: Google PageRank (the fraction of time a web surfer spends on each page), n-gram Models for text generation, HMMs, MCMC Sampling, and the MDPs of reinforcement learning.
END: Markov Chain = a process that "decides the next step looking only at now." Run it long enough and it can reach a balance independent of where it started.

#### Recommendation Systems
ONE: Recommendations are made from **"what people similar to me liked" (Collaborative Filtering)** or **"things similar to what I liked" (Content-Based)**. In practice, the two are mixed.
LIKE: Collaborative Filtering recommends "movies that friends with tastes like mine enjoyed," and Content-Based recommends "movies whose director·genre is similar to movies I liked."
STEP:
1. **Collaborative Filtering**: Finds similar users or items in a users × items behavior (ratings, clicks) Matrix.
2. **Matrix Factorization**: Decomposes this big Matrix into "user taste Vectors" and "item characteristic Vectors" to fill in the blanks (the predicted ratings for movies not yet watched) (the SVD idea from topic 12).
3. Pros: It doesn't need to know item content, and unexpected discoveries (Serendipity) are possible.
4. Cons: It can't recommend for new users·new items with no history (**Cold Start**).
5. **Content-Based**: Compares item attributes (genre, director, keywords) with a user's taste Profile. It can recommend new items too.
6. Cons: It keeps recommending similar things, narrowing your view (**Filter Bubble**).
7. **Hybrid**: In practice the two are mixed, and recently Deep Learning and Embedding-based recommendation are widely used.
END: Based on similar people is Collaborative; based on similar things is Content-Based. Mixing them covers each other's weaknesses.

### How would you explain a Markov Chain to a high school student?
E: It's a game of guessing tomorrow's weather from today's weather alone. "If it's sunny today, there's a 90% chance tomorrow is sunny too and a 10% chance of rain. If it rains today, there's a 50% chance tomorrow is sunny and a 50% chance it rains again." It doesn't care what yesterday or the day before was like.
A: A Markov Chain is a Stochastic Process where the probability of the next state depends only on the current state (Markov Property), and the probabilities of moving between states are represented by a Transition Matrix. In the weather example above, after a long time it converges to a Stationary Distribution of sunny 5/6, rainy 1/6 regardless of the starting weather, which is the Eigenvector of the Transition Matrix for Eigenvalue 1. PageRank, n-gram Models for text generation, HMMs, and MCMC use this idea.

### What are Recommendation Systems (Collaborative Filtering vs Content-Based)?
E: Collaborative Filtering recommends "movies that friends with tastes like mine enjoyed," and Content-Based recommends "movies whose director·genre is similar to movies I liked."
A: Collaborative Filtering recommends by finding similar users or items in a User-Item behavior Matrix or by learning Latent Factors with Matrix Factorization, so it doesn't need to know item content and unexpected discoveries (Serendipity) are possible, but it has the Cold Start problem for new users·new items with no history. Content-Based compares item attributes with a user's taste Profile, so it can recommend new items too, but it has the limitation of recommending only similar things (Filter Bubble), so in practice a Hybrid of the two is used.

## Reinforcement Learning

#### Reinforcement Learning and MDPs
ONE: **Reinforcement Learning** is a method where an Agent **acts in an environment and receives rewards (Reward)**, learning on its own **the action strategy (Policy) that maximizes cumulative reward** over the long run. The mathematical description of this situation is the **MDP**.
LIKE: It's just like training a puppy. When it sits, it gets a treat; when it chews things up, it doesn't. At first the puppy tries all sorts of things, but gradually it learns "what to do in which situation to get a treat."
STEP:
1. **Agent**: The one doing the learning (a puppy, game AI, robot).
2. **Environment**: The world the Agent lives in.
3. **State**: The current situation. **Action**: What the Agent can do. **Reward**: The score received after an action.
4. **MDP (Markov Decision Process)**: Defined by states, actions, state transition probabilities, and rewards. The next state depends only on the current state and action (Markov).
5. **Policy**: The rule for "which action to take in each state." Learning this is the goal.
6. **Value**: The **expected cumulative reward** to be received from starting in some state (or state-action). Rewards far in the future are discounted (Discount) and count for less.
7. **Value Iteration·Policy Iteration**: Methods for computing the optimal Policy when the environment's rules are known. They repeatedly update values with "current reward + value of the next state" (the Bellman Equation).
KEY: The core of decision theory: assign a satisfaction level (utility) to each outcome, and choose **the action with the largest expected utility**, the probability-weighted average. Gathering more information is worth it only when the improvement in the decision exceeds its cost (the value of information).
END: Reinforcement learning = learning a Policy by acting and getting rewarded. An MDP is the framework that describes that situation with states·actions·probabilities·rewards.

#### Exploration vs Exploitation and Q-Learning
ONE: The central challenge of reinforcement learning is balancing **taking a good action you already know (Exploitation)** against **trying a new action (Exploration)**. **Q-Learning** is the classic method that learns a value for each state-action.
LIKE: If you only go to your favorite restaurant (a known good choice), you'll never be disappointed, but you may never discover a tastier new place. You have to try a new restaurant now and then (exploration) to find better options. But if you only ever try new places, you'll often end up eating somewhere bad.
STEP:
1. **Exploitation**: Take the best action learned so far. Good for short-term reward.
2. **Exploration**: Try actions you've tried less. It might cost you now, but you can discover better actions.
3. **ε-Greedy**: Most of the time (1 − ε) take the best action, and occasionally (ε) take a random action. Start with ε large and make it smaller over time.
4. **Q-Learning**: Learns Q(state, action) = "the estimated cumulative reward from taking this action in this state."
5. After acting and observing the reward r and next state s′, it nudges Q(s, a) toward "r + γ × (the best Q value at s′)."
6. **Policy Gradient**: Instead of values, it adjusts the Policy (action probabilities) itself directly, raising the probability of high-reward actions.
7. **Deep RL**: Represents Q or the Policy with a Neural Network. DQN, which learned Atari games from the screen alone, and AlphaGo for Go are the classic examples.
KEY: **If the reward function is designed badly**, the Agent finds "loopholes" that differ from the intent (Reward Hacking). Example: in a boat racing game, an Agent that circles around collecting point items instead of heading for the finish line. That's why reward design is so important. RLHF, which aligns LLMs with human preferences, also uses reinforcement learning.
END: The balance between using what you know (Exploitation) and trying something new (Exploration) is the key, and Q-Learning learns the values of state-actions from experience.

### What is an MDP? What are a Policy and a Value Function?
E: In puppy training, the framework laying out "in this situation (State), which action (Action) earns a treat (Reward)" is the MDP, the rule for "what to do in each situation" is the Policy, and "the expected treats to come from this situation" is the Value.
A: An MDP (Markov Decision Process) is a sequential decision-making model defined by a set of States, a set of Actions, transition probabilities P(s′ | s, a), Rewards R(s, a), and a Discount Factor γ, where the next state depends only on the current state and action (Markov Property). A Policy π(a | s) is the action rule in each state, and Value Functions V(s)·Q(s, a) are the expected discounted cumulative reward when following that Policy. The optimal values satisfy the Bellman Equation, and when the environment model is known, the optimal Policy is found with Value Iteration·Policy Iteration.

### What is the Exploration-Exploitation Trade-off?
E: If you only go to your favorite restaurant, you'll never be disappointed but you'll never discover a tastier new place, and if you only ever try new places, you'll often end up eating somewhere bad.
A: Exploitation is taking the action that's best under current estimates to get short-term reward, and Exploration is trying uncertain actions to gain information that could reveal better ones. Doing only one gets you stuck with a suboptimal Policy or wastes reward, so the balance is struck with methods like ε-Greedy (a random action with probability ε), UCB (a bonus for actions with high uncertainty), and Thompson Sampling (Sampling from the Posterior). The Multi-Armed Bandit in recommendation systems is the same problem.

### What is Q-Learning?
E: It's keeping a score sheet for every combination of situation and action, "if I do this, how many treats will I get from here on," and adjusting the scores a little each time you try something and see the actual result.
A: Q-Learning is a Model-Free, Off-Policy reinforcement learning Algorithm that learns Q(s, a) directly without an environment model. After observing the reward r and next state s′, it updates Q(s, a) ← Q(s, a) + α[r + γ maxₐ′ Q(s′, a′) − Q(s, a)], and with enough exploration it converges to the optimal Q*. When there are too many states, Q is approximated with a Neural Network (DQN), and a badly designed reward can lead it to learn unintended loopholes (Reward Hacking).

## The Bridge to Deep Learning

#### Problems with Early Neural Networks
ONE: The idea of Neural Networks is old, but in the past there were problems: **stacking layers deep made training fail (Vanishing Gradient)**, **there was too little data, so models memorized it (Overfitting)**, and **computation was too slow**.
LIKE: In the game of Telephone, if there are too many people, the original message doesn't make it to the end intact. In old deep neural networks, the training signal (Gradient) didn't make it back to the early layers.
STEP:
1. **Vanishing Gradient**: Activation functions like Sigmoid·tanh have slopes near 0 when the input is very large or small (Saturation). When such small numbers were multiplied at every layer, the Gradients of the early layers vanished and they didn't learn (the Chain Rule of topic 12).
2. **Overfitting**: Without large-scale data, big models memorized the training data.
3. **Computational cost**: Training large Networks on CPUs was far too slow.
4. **Non-Convex**: The Loss was bumpy, so training was sensitive to initial values and the Learning Rate, and results were hard to interpret.
END: Old Neural Networks were blocked by vanishing gradients, lack of data, and lack of compute.

#### The Three Things That Created the Deep Learning Revolution
ONE: Deep Learning exploded when **large-scale data**, **GPU parallel computation**, and **Algorithm advances** all came together at once. Add Self-Supervised Pre-training and Scaling Laws, and you get today's LLMs.
LIKE: Once a good chef (Algorithm), fresh ingredients (data), and a powerful oven (GPU) were all in place at once, dishes that had been impossible before became possible.
STEP:
1. **Data**: ImageNet (millions of labeled images), the vast text of the web.
2. **Compute**: GPUs·TPUs process Matrix multiplication with massive parallelism.
3. **Algorithms**: ReLU (eases vanishing gradients), good initialization, Dropout, Batch Normalization, Residual Connections (make very deep Networks possible), the Adam Optimizer, Attention and the Transformer (topic 15).
4. **Self-Supervised Pre-training**: First learn general knowledge from large amounts of unlabeled data, then adapt to a specific task with a small amount of data.
5. **Scaling Laws**: The observation that performance rises predictably as the model·data·compute grow. That's why models kept getting bigger.
6. **Tools**: Open-source Frameworks like PyTorch sped up research and development.
END: Data + GPUs + Algorithms all arriving at once opened up Deep Learning, and Pre-training and Scaling carried it all the way to LLMs.

### What are the general problems of traditional Neural Networks?
A: When Layers were stacked deep, the Saturation of Sigmoid·tanh made the Backpropagation Gradient shrink progressively (Vanishing Gradient) so the early Layers didn't learn, there was little data so models overfit easily, and computation on CPUs was far too slow. It was also Non-Convex Optimization, so it was sensitive to Initialization·Learning Rate and the results were hard to interpret.

### What do you think is the foundation of today's Deep Learning innovations?
A: I'd say three things came together at once. First, large-scale data like ImageNet·web text; second, GPU·TPU parallel computation; and third, Algorithm advances like ReLU, good Initialization, Dropout, Batch Normalization, Residual Connections, Adam, Attention, and the Transformer. Add Self-Supervised Pre-training, which learns from data without Labels, Scaling Laws, the observation that performance rises predictably as the model·data·compute grow, and open-source tools like PyTorch, and you arrive at today's LLMs.

## Practice and Responsibility

#### Model Bias and Fairness
ONE: Even with high overall accuracy, a model can behave **unfavorably toward particular groups**. So you measure performance and outcomes **broken down by group**, and mitigate at the data·training·Threshold stages.
LIKE: Even if a loan approval model has high overall accuracy, if it often rejects people from one particular neighborhood even when they qualify, it isn't fair. Looking only at the average hides problems like this.
STEP:
1. **Causes**: Past prejudices captured in the training data, a lack of data for certain groups, biased labels.
2. **Measuring**: Break down the outcomes by group.
3. **Demographic Parity**: Is the rate of positive decisions the same across groups?
4. **Equal Opportunity**: Among the truly positive (qualified people), is the fraction found (TPR) the same across groups?
5. **Equalized Odds**: Are both TPR and FPR the same?
6. It's **usually impossible to satisfy several criteria at once**, so you have to pick the criterion that fits the situation.
7. **Mitigation**: At the data stage (Resampling, Reweighting), the training stage (adding fairness constraints), and the outcome stage (adjusting Thresholds per group).
KEY: Simply deleting sensitive attributes like gender·race isn't enough. Other Features like ZIP code carry that information in their place (Proxy Variables), so the Bias remains.
END: Measure the group differences hidden behind average accuracy, and mitigate with a fairness criterion that fits the situation.

#### Performance Degradation After Deployment: Data Drift
ONE: After a model is deployed, performance drops **when the world changes**. Causes include **Data Drift**, where the inputs change, and **Concept Drift**, where the relationship between inputs and answers changes. You have to keep watching and retrain.
LIKE: A translator trained on last year's slang can't understand this year's new slang. When the world changes, the model has to learn again too.
STEP:
1. **Data (Covariate) Drift**: The input distribution P(x) changes. Example: a large influx of users from a new age group.
2. **Concept Drift**: The relationship between inputs and answers P(y | x) changes. Example: shifts in spending patterns after COVID, fraudsters changing their tactics.
3. **Label Drift**: The proportion of answers itself changes.
4. **Training-Serving Skew**: A gap caused by the Feature-building code differing between training and serving. A very common mistake.
5. **Monitoring**: Keep watching changes in the input distribution (metrics like PSI and the KS Test) and actual performance.
6. When a threshold is crossed, **retrain (Retraining)** on recent data. Automating this process is **MLOps**.
KEY: Consider AI safety and ethics too. There are privacy, job changes from automation, accountability, and the value alignment (Alignment) problem: "a system that perfectly optimizes a fixed objective can be dangerous."
END: Deployment isn't the end but the beginning. Watch whether the world changes, and retrain when it does.

### How do you measure and mitigate model Bias and Fairness?
E: Even if a loan approval model has high overall accuracy, if it often rejects people from one particular neighborhood even when they qualify, it isn't fair.
A: Break down performance and outcomes by group. The main Metrics are whether the rate of positive decisions is the same across groups (Demographic Parity), whether the fraction of the truly positive that's found is the same (Equal Opportunity), and whether both TPR and FPR are the same (Equalized Odds); since satisfying several criteria at once is usually impossible, you have to pick the one that fits the situation. Mitigation is done with data Resampling·Reweighting (Pre-processing), Fairness Constraints during training (In-processing), and per-group Threshold adjustment (Post-processing), keeping in mind that even if you delete only the sensitive attributes, Bias remains through Proxy Variables like ZIP code.

### Why does performance drop after a model is deployed (Data Drift)?
E: A translator trained on last year's slang can't understand this year's new slang. When the world changes, the model has to learn again too.
A: There's Data (Covariate) Drift, where the input distribution P(x) changes; Concept Drift, where the relationship between inputs and answers P(y | x) changes (spending patterns after COVID); and Label Drift, where the proportion of answers changes; Training-Serving Skew, where Feature processing differs between the training and serving Pipelines, is also a common cause. Monitor the input distribution (PSI, KS Test) and performance, and retrain on recent data when a threshold is crossed.
