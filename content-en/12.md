@intro Linear Algebra is the math of viewing and handling data as "tables of numbers (Matrices)," and it is the language of Machine Learning·Deep Learning. This topic is easy if you **think of a drawing on a rubber sheet**. A Matrix is the action of stretching, rotating, flipping, and squashing the rubber sheet, and a Vector is an arrow drawn on it. An Eigenvector is a special arrow whose direction doesn't change even when the sheet is stretched.
@one Linear Algebra studies Linear Transformations with Vectors and Matrices, providing ways to solve systems of equations (Gaussian Elimination), the structure of spaces (Basis·Rank), the essence of transformations (Eigenvalue·SVD), and approximate solutions (Least Squares), which form the foundation of Regression·PCA·Neural Network computation.

## Vector and Matrix Operations

#### Vectors and Matrices: Seeing Data as Bundles of Numbers
ONE: A **Vector** is numbers bundled **in a single row**, and a **Matrix** is numbers bundled **in a grid of rows and columns**. AI turns photos, sentences, and customer info all into bundles of numbers like these to compute with them.
LIKE: A Vector is an arrow drawn on the rubber sheet. (3, 2) is an arrow that goes "3 to the right, 2 up." A Matrix is a table of numbers that records the action of stretching, rotating, and flipping that rubber sheet.
STEP:
1. **Scalar**: A single number. Example: 5.
2. **Vector**: Numbers listed in order. Example: (height 68 in, weight 150 lb, age 20) is a 3-dimensional Vector representing one person. The count of numbers is the **Dimension**.
3. Geometrically, a Vector is an **arrow** starting at the origin. It has a direction and a length.
4. **Adding Vectors**: Add position by position. (1, 2) + (3, 1) = (4, 3). It's the same as placing the arrows end to end.
5. **Scalar multiplication**: Multiply every number by the same number. 2 × (1, 2) = (2, 4). The direction stays the same; only the length doubles.
6. **Matrix**: Numbers laid out in a table of m Rows × n Columns. Collect (height, weight, age) for 100 people and you get a 100 × 3 Matrix.
7. **Norm (length)**: ‖(3, 4)‖ = √(3² + 4²) = 5. It's the Pythagorean theorem.
WHY: Computers can't directly understand a "cat photo" or a "like." Only after turning them into bundles of numbers can they compare, compute, and learn.
USE: A photo is a Matrix of pixel numbers, a word is a Vector carrying meaning (an Embedding), and one layer of a Neural Network is a Matrix multiplication.
END: A Vector is a row of numbers (an arrow), and a Matrix is a table of numbers (a transformation). All AI data is ultimately expressed in these two forms.

#### Dot Product and Cosine Similarity
ONE: The **Dot Product** measures **how much two Vectors point in the same direction × their lengths**, and **Cosine Similarity** drops the lengths and compares **only direction**.
LIKE: Imagine two people pushing a cart. Pushing in the same direction combines their force, pushing at right angles doesn't help each other, and pushing in opposite directions works against each other. The Dot Product puts "how much they push in the same direction" into a number.
STEP:
1. **How to compute it**: Multiply position by position and add everything up. (1, 2)·(3, 4) = 1×3 + 2×4 = 11.
2. **Geometric meaning**: a·b = ‖a‖ × ‖b‖ × cosθ (θ is the angle between the two arrows).
3. In the same direction (θ = 0°), cos = 1, so it's largest. At a right angle (θ = 90°), cos = 0, so the **Dot Product = 0**. This is called **Orthogonal**. In opposite directions, it's negative.
4. **The problem**: The Dot Product is also affected by length. A long document has many words, so its Vector is long, and the value can be large even if the content is less similar.
5. **Cosine Similarity**: Divide the Dot Product by the two lengths. cosθ = a·b / (‖a‖ × ‖b‖). The effect of length disappears and **only direction** remains, giving a value between −1 and 1.
6. 1 means exactly the same direction (very similar), 0 means unrelated, and −1 means exactly opposite.
USE: Comparing the similarity of document·word Embeddings, recommendation systems ("movies like this one"), search engines, and finding the documents most similar to a question in AI search (RAG).
END: Dot Product = degree of same direction × length; Cosine Similarity = comparing direction only. 0 means orthogonal.

#### Matrix Multiplication and Transpose
ONE: **Matrix multiplication** is "**applying transformations in order**," so changing the order changes the result (AB ≠ BA). The **Transpose** swaps rows and columns.
LIKE: Just as "put on socks, then shoes" and "put on shoes, then socks" give different results, the order of Matrix multiplication matters. And when taking them off, the shoes have to come off first. That's the feel of (AB)ᵀ = BᵀAᵀ and (AB)⁻¹ = B⁻¹A⁻¹.
STEP:
1. **How to multiply**: The (i, j) entry of AB = the Dot Product of A's i-th row and B's j-th column.
2. **Size condition**: You can multiply only when A is m×n and B is n×p, and the result is m×p. A's number of columns must equal B's number of rows.
3. **Matrix × Vector**: Ax is "the result of applying transformation A to Vector x."
4. **What ABx means**: Apply B first, then apply A (right to left).
5. **No commutative law**: Rotating then reflecting gives a different result from reflecting then rotating. On top of that, the sizes may not match, so BA might not even be defined.
6. **The associative and distributive laws hold**: (AB)C = A(BC), A(B + C) = AB + AC.
7. **Transpose (Aᵀ)**: Swaps rows and columns. The Transpose of a 2×3 Matrix is 3×2. And as in (AB)ᵀ = BᵀAᵀ, the order flips.
8. **Identity Matrix (I)**: A Matrix with 1s on the diagonal and 0s elsewhere. Like the number 1, AI = IA = A.
KEY: Written as Matrices, the Dot Product is aᵀb. In Neural Network Backpropagation, computing by multiplying Gradients backward by Transposed Matrices comes up constantly.
END: Matrix multiplication applies transformations in order, so order matters. The Transpose swaps rows·columns, and the Transpose of a product flips the order.

### What does the Dot Product of Vectors measure? How does it relate to Cosine Similarity?
E: If two people push in the same direction their force combines, at right angles they don't help each other, and in opposite directions they work against each other. The Dot Product is "how much two arrows point in the same direction × their lengths."
A: The Dot Product is a·b = Σ aᵢbᵢ = ‖a‖‖b‖cosθ, measuring both the lengths (Norms) of two Vectors and how well their directions agree; 0 means Orthogonal. Cosine Similarity is the Dot Product divided by the two lengths, cosθ = a·b / (‖a‖‖b‖), removing the effect of length and comparing only direction, with values between −1 and 1. It is widely used to measure the similarity of document·word Embeddings.

### Why doesn't Matrix Multiplication satisfy the commutative law (AB = BA)?
E: Just as "put on socks, then shoes" and "put on shoes, then socks" give different results, Matrix multiplication applies actions in order, so order matters.
A: A Matrix is a Linear Transformation, and ABx is the Composition of applying "B first, then A," so changing the order, as with rotating then reflecting vs reflecting then rotating, can change the result. Also, if A is m×n and B is n×p, AB is defined but BA may not even be defined because the sizes don't match. However, the associative law (Associativity) (AB)C = A(BC) and the distributive law (Distributivity) do hold.

### What do the Transpose and (AB)ᵀ = BᵀAᵀ mean?
A: The Transpose Aᵀ is the Matrix with Rows and Columns swapped (Aᵀᵢⱼ = Aⱼᵢ). (AB)ᵀ = BᵀAᵀ means flipping reverses the order of the product, and it appears constantly, for example when writing the Dot Product as xᵀy and when Transposing Gradients to multiply backward in Backpropagation. The Inverse Matrix has the same form, (AB)⁻¹ = B⁻¹A⁻¹ (if you put on socks and then shoes, the shoes come off first).

## Systems of Equations and Gaussian Elimination

#### Gaussian Elimination
ONE: A way to solve a system of equations by **subtracting and adding equations to eliminate variables one at a time**. Organized as a Matrix, a computer can solve it systematically.
LIKE: Given "2 apples + 1 pear = $5" and "1 apple + 1 pear = $3," subtracting the second equation from the first immediately gives "1 apple = $2." Plug that in and a pear is $1. The key is subtracting equations to eliminate variables.
STEP:
1. Write the system of equations as a Matrix. 2x + y = 5, x + y = 3 is the coefficient Matrix A = [[2, 1], [1, 1]] and b = (5, 3), i.e., Ax = b.
2. Build the **Augmented Matrix** [A | b] by placing A and b side by side.
3. **The 3 elementary row operations**: ① swap two rows ② multiply a row by a nonzero number ③ add a multiple of one row to another row. These operations don't change the solution.
4. Use these operations to make the lower left zeros, forming a staircase shape (**Echelon Form**).
5. The first nonzero number in each row is called a **Pivot**.
6. Going further, making every Pivot 1 and every entry above and below each Pivot 0 gives the **RREF (Reduced Row Echelon Form)**. You can read the solution directly, and a Matrix has exactly one RREF.
7. Example: Reducing [[2, 1 | 5], [1, 1 | 3]] gives x = 2, y = 1.
WHY: Even systems with thousands of variables can be solved by the same procedure. It's the basic method computers use to solve systems of equations.
END: Subtract equations to eliminate variables and build a staircase shape, and the solution appears. Row operations don't change the solution.

#### No Solution · One Solution · Infinitely Many
ONE: The Echelon Form tells you how many solutions there are. **If "0 = a nonzero number" appears, there's no solution**; **if every variable has a Pivot, there's one solution**; **if some variable has no Pivot, there are infinitely many solutions**.
LIKE: Think of two lines. If they're parallel, they never meet (no solution); if they cross, they meet at one point (one solution); if they lie exactly on top of each other, they meet at every point (infinitely many solutions).
STEP:
1. Reduce the Augmented Matrix to Echelon Form.
2. **No solution (Inconsistent)**: If a row like [0 0 … 0 | c] (c ≠ 0) appears, it's the contradiction "0 = c." There's no solution.
3. If there's no contradiction, a solution exists (**Consistent**).
4. **One solution**: Every variable Column has a Pivot. Each variable is fixed to one value.
5. **Infinitely many solutions**: Some variable Column has no Pivot. Such a variable is called a **Free Variable**, and since it can take any value, there are infinitely many solutions.
| Case | What the Echelon Form looks like |
| No solution | A row [0 0 … 0 \| c] (c ≠ 0) appears |
| One solution | No contradiction + a Pivot for every variable |
| Infinitely many solutions | No contradiction + a Free Variable exists |
END: A contradictory row means no solution, a Free Variable means infinitely many, and neither means one solution.

#### How Ax = 0 and Ax = b Are Related
ONE: Take **the solutions of Ax = 0** and **shift them by one solution of Ax = b**, and you get all the solutions of Ax = b.
LIKE: Pick up the whole line through the origin (the solutions of Ax = 0) and move it so it passes through the point p; that's the set of solutions of Ax = b. Same shape, just a different position.
STEP:
1. **Homogeneous Ax = 0**: x = 0 is always a solution (the **Trivial Solution**).
2. A nonzero solution (**Nontrivial Solution**) exists only when there is a Free Variable.
3. **Non-Homogeneous Ax = b**: If it has a solution, call one of them p (a particular solution).
4. Then every solution of Ax = b is **x = p + vₕ**, where vₕ is any solution of Ax = 0.
5. Why: A(p + vₕ) = Ap + Avₕ = b + 0 = b.
6. Geometrically, it's the solution set of Ax = 0 through the origin (a line or plane) shifted by p.
END: Solutions of Ax = b = one particular solution + all solutions of Ax = 0. Same shape, just moved.

### What are Gaussian Elimination and RREF (Reduced Row Echelon Form)?
E: Just as subtracting "1 apple and 1 pear cost $3" from "2 apples and 1 pear cost $5" immediately gives 1 apple = $2, it's a method of subtracting and adding equations to eliminate variables one at a time.
A: Gaussian Elimination uses three Elementary Row Operations, swapping Rows, multiplying by a nonzero number, and adding a multiple of one Row to another, to turn a Matrix into a staircase shape (Echelon Form) without changing the solution. RREF (Reduced Row Echelon Form) is the form where each Pivot (the first nonzero number of a Row) is 1 and the rest of each Pivot's Column is 0; the solution can be read directly, and it is unique for a given Matrix.

### How do you tell no solution / one solution / infinitely many solutions from the Pivots?
| Case | What the Augmented Matrix [A \| b] looks like in Echelon Form |
| No solution (Inconsistent) | A Row [0 0 … 0 \| c] (c ≠ 0) appears = a Pivot in the last (b) Column |
| One solution | A solution exists, and every variable Column has a Pivot (no Free Variables) |
| Infinitely many solutions | A solution exists, and one or more variable Columns have no Pivot (Free Variables) |
A: When the Augmented Matrix is reduced to Echelon Form, a Row of the form "0 = a nonzero number" is a contradiction, so there's no solution; otherwise a solution exists (Consistent). When a solution exists, if every variable has a Pivot the solution is unique, and if there's a Free Variable without a Pivot, that variable can be set to any value, so there are infinitely many solutions.

### How are the solutions of Ax = 0 (Homogeneous) and Ax = b (Non-Homogeneous) related?
A: Ax = 0 always has the Trivial Solution x = 0, and has a Nontrivial Solution only when there is a Free Variable. If Ax = b has one solution p, the complete solution is x = p + vₕ (vₕ is any solution of Ax = 0). Geometrically, it's the Homogeneous solution set through the origin shifted (Translation) by p.

## Linear Independence·Span·Basis·Dimension

#### Linear Combinations and Span
ONE: A **Linear Combination** is Vectors multiplied by numbers and added up, and the **Span** is **the collection of all Vectors** you can make that way.
LIKE: Mixing different amounts of red·blue·yellow paint gives many colors. Each way of mixing is a Linear Combination, and the set of all colors you can make from those three paints is the Span.
STEP:
1. **Linear Combination**: c₁v₁ + c₂v₂ + … + cₖvₖ. Multiply each Vector by a number (coefficient) and add.
2. Example: If v₁ = (1, 0) and v₂ = (0, 1), then 3v₁ + 2v₂ = (3, 2). In fact, these two Vectors can make every point in the plane.
3. **Span{v₁, …, vₖ}**: The set of all possible Linear Combinations.
4. The Span of a single Vector is a **line** through the origin (since you can only change its length).
5. The Span of two Vectors pointing in different directions is a **plane** through the origin.
6. "Does Ax = b have a solution?" is the same question as "Is b inside the Span of A's Columns?"
END: Multiplying and adding is a Linear Combination, and everything you can make that way is the Span.

#### Linear Independence
ONE: If **none of the Vectors can be made by mixing the others**, they're Linearly Independent; if even one can be, they're Dependent.
LIKE: An east arrow and a north arrow can't stand in for each other, so they're independent. Add a northeast arrow, and since it can already be made from east + north, it's redundant (dependent).
STEP:
1. **Definition**: They're independent if the only coefficients satisfying c₁v₁ + … + cₖvₖ = 0 are **all zero**.
2. If you can make 0 with nonzero coefficients, one of the Vectors can be expressed using the others, so they're dependent.
3. **How to check**: Put the Vectors as Columns of a Matrix and reduce it to Echelon Form. If **every Column has a Pivot**, they're independent.
4. **Quick check ①**: If there's even one zero Vector, they're dependent.
5. **Quick check ②**: In m-dimensional space, more than m Vectors are always dependent. Put 3 arrows in a plane (2D), and one of them can always be made from the others.
WHY: Keeping only independent Vectors makes the information clean with no duplication. If a dataset's Features are dependent on each other (e.g., including both height in inches and height in feet), Regression becomes unstable.
END: If they can't stand in for each other, they're independent; if even one can be made from the rest, they're dependent.

#### Basis and Dimension
ONE: A **Basis** is a bundle of Vectors that can make the whole space (Span) **with no redundancy (independent)**, in other words, **a minimal set of coordinate axes**. The number of Basis Vectors is the **Dimension**.
LIKE: On a map, you can describe any location as "so many blocks east, so many blocks north." The two directions east·north are a Basis of the plane, which is why the plane is 2-dimensional.
STEP:
1. **The two conditions for a Basis**: ① it Spans the whole space (it can make every Vector) ② it's Linearly Independent (no redundancy).
2. So every Vector in the space can be expressed with the Basis in **exactly one way**. Those coefficients are the **coordinates**.
3. **Standard Basis**: In R², (1, 0) and (0, 1); in R³, (1,0,0), (0,1,0), (0,0,1).
4. There can be many Bases. (1, 1) and (1, −1) are also a Basis of R².
5. But **the number of Basis Vectors is always the same**. That number is the Dimension. R³ is 3-dimensional.
WHY: Picking a good Basis can express the same data much more simply. PCA is a method for finding a new Basis that best explains the data.
END: Basis = the minimal set of coordinate axes that can make the whole space; Dimension = how many there are.

### What is Linear Independence? How do you check it?
E: East and north arrows can't stand in for each other, so they're independent. Add a northeast arrow, and since it can already be made from east + north, it's redundant (Dependent).
A: Vectors v₁, …, vₖ are Linearly Independent when the only coefficients making c₁v₁ + … + cₖvₖ = 0 are all zero, that is, when no Vector can be made as a Linear Combination of the others. Put the Vectors as Columns of a Matrix and reduce it to Echelon Form; if every Column has a Pivot, they're independent, and in Rᵐ more than m Vectors are always dependent.

### What are a Basis and Dimension?
A: A Basis is a set of Vectors that Spans the space and is Linearly Independent, the "minimal set of coordinate axes" that lets every Vector in the space be expressed in exactly one way. There can be many Bases, but the number of Basis Vectors is always the same, and that number is the Dimension (R³ is 3-dimensional).

## Linear Transformations

#### Linear Transformations and Matrices
ONE: A **Linear Transformation** stretches, rotates, and flips space while keeping **lines as lines and the origin in place**. Every Linear Transformation can be expressed **as a single Matrix**.
LIKE: It's a deformation of a grid-patterned rubber sheet where, even after stretching or rotating, the grid lines stay straight, parallel, and evenly spaced, and the origin stays put. That's why knowing where a couple of basic arrows (axes) land determines everything else.
STEP:
1. **Conditions**: T(u + v) = T(u) + T(v), T(cu) = cT(u). Transforming after adding gives the same result as adding after transforming.
2. Every Vector is a Linear Combination of the basic axes (Standard Basis) e₁, e₂, …. Example: (3, 2) = 3e₁ + 2e₂.
3. So T(3e₁ + 2e₂) = 3T(e₁) + 2T(e₂). **Knowing only where the basic axes go**, you know the result for every Vector.
4. Build a Matrix A with T(e₁), T(e₂), … side by side as Columns, and **T(x) = Ax**.
5. Example: A 90° counterclockwise rotation sends e₁ = (1, 0) to (0, 1) and e₂ = (0, 1) to (−1, 0). So the Matrix is [[0, −1], [1, 0]].
USE: Rotating·scaling·moving in Computer Graphics (game screens), image transformations, and each layer of a Neural Network (multiplying the input Vector by a Weight Matrix).
END: A Linear Transformation is a deformation that keeps the grid straight, and gathering the landing spots of the basic axes as Columns gives its Matrix.

#### One-to-One and Onto
ONE: A transformation T(x) = Ax is **One-to-One if it never sends different inputs to the same output**, and **Onto if it can produce every output**. You tell them apart by Pivot positions.
LIKE: One-to-One is a seating assignment where "no two people sit in the same seat," and Onto is a seating assignment where "not a single seat is empty" (the same meaning as for Functions in topic 01).
STEP:
1. If A is an m×n Matrix, T sends n-dimensional inputs to m-dimensional outputs.
2. **One-to-One**: Ax = 0 has only the solution x = 0. That is, the Columns are Linearly Independent, with **a Pivot in every Column**.
3. Why: If two inputs u, v give the same output, then A(u − v) = 0. If there's no nonzero solution, u must equal v.
4. **Onto**: Ax = b has a solution for every b. That is, the Columns Span all of Rᵐ, with **a Pivot in every Row**.
5. If there are more columns than rows (n > m), it can't be One-to-One, and if there are more rows than columns (m > n), it can't be Onto.
END: A Pivot in every Column means One-to-One (no overlaps), and a Pivot in every Row means Onto (no empty seats).

### What is a Linear Transformation? Why can every Linear Transformation be expressed as a Matrix?
E: A Linear Transformation is a deformation of a grid-patterned rubber sheet where, even after stretching or rotating, the grid lines stay straight, parallel, and evenly spaced, and the origin stays put. That's why knowing where a couple of basic arrows (axes) land determines everything.
A: A Linear Transformation is a function satisfying T(u + v) = T(u) + T(v) and T(cu) = cT(u). Every Vector is a Linear Combination of the Standard Basis e₁, …, eₙ, so by Linearity T(x) is determined by T(e₁), …, T(eₙ) alone, and with the Standard Matrix A = [T(e₁) … T(eₙ)] that has these as Columns, T(x) = Ax.

### How do you tell from a Matrix whether a Transformation is One-to-One·Onto?
A: For an m×n Matrix A, T(x) = Ax being One-to-One (different inputs never go to the same output) is equivalent to Ax = 0 having only the Trivial Solution, that is, the Columns being Linearly Independent with a Pivot in every Column. Being Onto (every Vector in Rᵐ appears as an output) is equivalent to the Columns Spanning Rᵐ, that is, a Pivot in every Row.

## Subspaces and Rank

#### Column Space and Null Space
ONE: The **Column Space** is **all the outputs** transformation A **can produce**, and the **Null Space** is **the inputs the transformation squashes down to 0**.
LIKE: All the colors you can make by mixing three paints (the Matrix's Columns) are the Column Space, and the mixing recipes whose result is "no color at all" are the Null Space.
STEP:
1. **Subspace**: A collection of Vectors that contains the origin and stays inside itself under addition and Scalar multiplication. Things like lines or planes through the origin.
2. **Column Space (Col A)**: The Span of A's Columns. It's the set of b for which Ax = b has a solution.
3. A Basis for Col A is the **Pivot Columns of the original A** (note: the Columns of the original Matrix, not of the Echelon Form).
4. **Null Space (Nul A)**: All solutions of Ax = 0. The directions that become 0 when transformed.
5. If the Null Space is only {0}, nothing disappears, so the transformation is One-to-One.
END: The Column Space is "what can be made," and the Null Space is "what vanishes to 0."

#### Rank and the Rank-Nullity Theorem
ONE: The **Rank** is **the number of dimensions that survive** the transformation (the number of Pivots), the **Nullity** is **the number of dimensions squashed to 0 and lost** (the number of Free Variables), and together they add up to **the number of input dimensions (the number of Columns)**.
LIKE: Shine a light down on a 3D object to cast a shadow on the floor, and the 2 dimensions of width and depth survive in the shadow (Rank 2) while the 1 dimension of height disappears (Nullity 1). Together they make the original 3 dimensions.
STEP:
1. **Rank**: The Dimension of the Column Space = the number of Pivots.
2. **Nullity**: The Dimension of the Null Space = the number of Free Variables.
3. Every Column is either a Pivot Column or a Free Variable Column.
4. So **rank + nullity = n (the number of Columns)**.
5. Example: If a 3×5 Matrix has Rank 3, its Nullity is 5 − 3 = 2.
6. Meaning: It's a **law of dimension conservation**: of the n input dimensions, some survive into the output (Rank) and the rest are squashed to 0 and lost.
WHY: The Rank tells you "how many dimensions of truly independent information the data has." It's a core concept for compressing data (Low-Rank approximation).
END: Surviving dimensions (Rank) + lost dimensions (Nullity) = input dimensions (number of Columns).

### What are the Column Space and the Null Space?
E: All the colors you can make by mixing three paints (the Matrix's Columns) are the Column Space, and the mixing recipes whose result is "no color at all" are the Null Space.
A: The Column Space Col A is all Linear Combinations of A's Columns, that is, the set of b for which Ax = b has a solution, and its Basis is the Pivot Columns of the original A. The Null Space Nul A is all solutions of Ax = 0, the directions the transformation sends to 0. If the Null Space is only {0}, the transformation is One-to-One.

### What does the Rank-Nullity Theorem (rank + nullity = number of Columns) mean?
A: The Rank is the Dimension of the Column Space (the number of Pivots), and the Nullity is the Dimension of the Null Space (the number of Free Variables); since every Column is either a Pivot Column or a Free Variable Column, their sum is the number of Columns n. It's a law of dimension conservation saying that of the n input dimensions, some survive into the output (Rank) and the rest are squashed to 0 and lost (Nullity). For example, if a 3×5 Matrix has Rank 3, its Nullity is 2.

## Inverse Matrices and Determinants

#### Inverse Matrices and the Invertible Matrix Theorem
ONE: The **Inverse Matrix A⁻¹** is the transformation that **undoes** transformation A (AA⁻¹ = I). A transformation that squashes space flat can't be undone, so it has no Inverse.
LIKE: A rubber sheet that's been stretched or rotated can be put back, but one that's been squashed flat like paper can't, because there's no way to know its original shape.
STEP:
1. **Invertible**: There exists A⁻¹ with AA⁻¹ = A⁻¹A = I. This is considered only for square Matrices.
2. If the Inverse exists, the solution of Ax = b can be found directly as x = A⁻¹b.
3. **Singular**: There's no Inverse, because the space was squashed into a lower dimension and information was lost.
4. **Invertible Matrix Theorem**: For an n×n Matrix A, the following **all say the same thing**. Check just one and all the rest follow.
5. A is Invertible ⇔ det A ≠ 0 ⇔ there are n Pivots ⇔ the Columns are Linearly Independent ⇔ the Columns Span all of Rⁿ ⇔ Ax = 0 has only the solution 0 ⇔ Ax = b has a unique solution for every b ⇔ rank A = n ⇔ 0 is not an Eigenvalue.
KEY: In actual computation, solving Ax = b directly with methods like Gaussian Elimination is faster and more accurate than computing A⁻¹ explicitly.
END: An Inverse is "undo," and only transformations that don't squash flat (det ≠ 0) can be undone.

#### The Determinant
ONE: The **Determinant** is a number that tells you **by what factor a transformation changes area (or volume)**. If it's 0, space is squashed flat and can't be undone.
LIKE: Draw a square of area 1 on the rubber sheet and transform it. If the shape's area becomes 6 after the transformation, the Determinant is 6. If it's flipped like a mirror image, a − sign is attached.
STEP:
1. **Computing a 2×2**: The Determinant of [[a, b], [c, d]] = ad − bc.
2. Example: [[2, 0], [0, 3]] stretches width 2x and height 3x, so area grows 6x. det = 2×3 − 0×0 = 6.
3. **Sign**: Negative means the orientation was flipped (a mirror image).
4. **det = 0**: The area became 0. The plane was squashed into a line (or a point) and information was lost. So there's no Inverse (Singular), and Ax = b has either no solution or infinitely many.
5. **Multiplicative property**: det(AB) = det A × det B. It means "stretch twice and the factors multiply."
WHY: One det tells you "can this transformation be undone?", and it's also used in the equation for finding Eigenvalues (det(A − λI) = 0).
END: Determinant = the area (volume) scaling factor. If it's 0, things are flattened and can't be undone.

### Which Matrices are Invertible? (the Invertible Matrix Theorem)
E: Invertible means "can be undone." A rubber sheet that's been stretched or rotated can be put back, but one that's been squashed flat like paper can't, because there's no way to know its original shape.
A: An n×n Matrix A being Invertible means there exists A⁻¹ with AA⁻¹ = A⁻¹A = I, and the following all say the same thing: det A ≠ 0, there are n Pivots, the Columns are Linearly Independent, the Columns Span Rⁿ, Ax = 0 has only the Trivial Solution, Ax = b has a unique solution for every b, rank A = n, and 0 is not an Eigenvalue. Checking just one makes all the rest follow.

### What is the geometric meaning of the Determinant? What if det = 0?
A: The Determinant tells you by what factor a transformation changes area (2D) or volume (3D), and its sign tells you whether the Orientation was flipped (a mirror image). If det = 0, space is squashed into a lower dimension (plane → line) and information is lost, so there's no Inverse (Singular), and Ax = b has either no solution or infinitely many. det(AB) = det A · det B means "stretch twice and the factors multiply."

## Eigenvalue·Eigenvector·Diagonalization

#### Eigenvalues and Eigenvectors
ONE: An **Eigenvector** is a special Vector whose **direction doesn't change and only its length changes** under a transformation, and that length factor is the **Eigenvalue**. They tell you the transformation's "main axes."
LIKE: When you stretch the rubber sheet, most arrows get twisted to new directions. But some arrows keep their direction and only change length. Those arrows are Eigenvectors, and how many times longer they got is the Eigenvalue.
STEP:
1. **Definition**: Av = λv (v ≠ 0). Multiplying by A leaves v's direction unchanged and only scales its length by λ.
2. If λ > 1 it stretches, if 0 < λ < 1 it shrinks, if λ < 0 it flips to the opposite direction, and if λ = 0 it's squashed to 0.
3. Example: A = [[2, 1], [1, 2]]. A(1, 1) = (3, 3) = 3(1, 1), so (1, 1) is an Eigenvector with Eigenvalue 3.
4. For the same A, A(1, −1) = (1, −1), so (1, −1) is an Eigenvector with Eigenvalue 1.
5. Then this transformation can be understood very simply as "stretch 3x in the (1, 1) direction and leave the (1, −1) direction alone."
WHY: You can understand a complex transformation by breaking it down into "stretching along axes." It also tells you where things converge when a transformation is repeated.
USE: PCA (the direction the data is most spread out = an Eigenvector of the Covariance Matrix), the long-run state of Google PageRank and Markov Chains (the Eigenvector with Eigenvalue 1), vibration·stability analysis.
END: Eigenvector = an arrow whose direction doesn't change; Eigenvalue = how much that arrow is stretched.

#### The Characteristic Equation: Finding Eigenvalues
ONE: Eigenvalues are found by solving **det(A − λI) = 0**. This equation is the condition that "A − λI squashes space flat."
LIKE: "There's an arrow whose direction doesn't change" means there's a transformation (A − λI) that sends that arrow to 0, and to send something to 0, space has to be squashed (det = 0).
STEP:
1. Rearranging Av = λv gives Av − λv = 0, i.e., **(A − λI)v = 0**.
2. For this equation to have a solution with v ≠ 0, A − λI must send a nonzero Vector to 0. That is, A − λI must be Singular.
3. Singular ⇔ the Determinant is 0. So **det(A − λI) = 0**. This is the **Characteristic Equation**.
4. The roots of this degree-n polynomial in λ are the Eigenvalues.
5. Plugging each λ into (A − λI)v = 0 and solving gives the Eigenvectors.
6. Example: If A = [[2, 1], [1, 2]], then det(A − λI) = (2 − λ)² − 1 = 0 → λ = 3 or 1.
KEY: Properties for checking: the **sum** of the Eigenvalues **= the Trace (sum of the diagonal)**, and their **product = the Determinant**. In the example above, 3 + 1 = 4 = 2 + 2, and 3 × 1 = 3 = 2×2 − 1×1.
END: Solve det(A − λI) = 0 for the Eigenvalues, then solve (A − λI)v = 0 with that λ for the Eigenvectors.

#### Diagonalization
ONE: If there are enough independent Eigenvectors, you can write **A = PDP⁻¹**. Viewed in the Eigenvector coordinate system, the transformation becomes **a diagonal Matrix D that only stretches along each axis**, which makes computation very easy.
LIKE: Looked at straight on, a tilted rubber sheet seems to stretch in complicated ways, but rotate the sheet to line up with its special directions and it's just a simple transformation of "3x horizontally, 1x vertically."
STEP:
1. **P**: The Matrix with the Eigenvectors as Columns. **D**: The Matrix with the Eigenvalues on the diagonal.
2. **What A = PDP⁻¹ means**: P⁻¹ switches to the Eigenvector coordinate system → D stretches along each axis → P returns to the original coordinate system.
3. **Condition**: An n×n Matrix must have n Linearly Independent Eigenvectors.
4. If all the Eigenvalues are different, it's always possible. It can still be possible when Eigenvalues repeat, but not always.
5. **Why it's useful**: Aᵏ = PDᵏP⁻¹. Raising a diagonal Matrix to a power just raises the diagonal numbers to that power, so even A¹⁰⁰ is easy to compute.
USE: Long-term prediction of repeated processes (population change, Markov Chains), solving differential equations, finding the closed-form formula for the Fibonacci sequence.
END: Diagonalization is "rotate to the special directions and it's just stretching." Thanks to it, powers become easy.

### What are Eigenvalues and Eigenvectors, and why do they matter?
E: When you stretch the rubber sheet, most arrows get twisted to new directions, but some arrows keep their direction and only change length. Those arrows are Eigenvectors, and how many times longer they got is the Eigenvalue. They tell you the transformation's "main axes."
A: An Eigenvector is a Vector satisfying Av = λv (v ≠ 0), that is, a Vector whose direction stays the same under the transformation and is only scaled by λ, and λ is the Eigenvalue. Because they break a complex transformation down into simple stretching along its own axes, they're used in PCA (Eigenvectors of the Covariance Matrix = Principal Components), the Steady State of PageRank·Markov Chains (the Eigenvector with Eigenvalue 1), computing powers of Matrices, and system stability analysis.
M: If A = [[2, 1], [1, 2]], then det(A − λI) = (2 − λ)² − 1 = 0 gives λ = 3, 1. The Eigenvector for λ = 3 is (1, 1) and for λ = 1 is (1, −1), and indeed A(1, 1) = (3, 3) and A(1, −1) = (1, −1).

### Where does the Characteristic Equation det(A − λI) = 0 come from?
A: Rearranging Av = λv gives (A − λI)v = 0, and for this equation to have a solution with v ≠ 0, A − λI must be Singular, so det(A − λI) = 0. The roots of this degree-n polynomial in λ are the Eigenvalues, and plugging in each λ and solving (A − λI)v = 0 gives the Eigenvectors. The sum of the Eigenvalues equals the Trace, and their product equals the Determinant.

### When is Diagonalization (A = PDP⁻¹) possible, and why is it useful?
A: An n×n Matrix is Diagonalizable when it has n Linearly Independent Eigenvectors, and it's always possible if all the Eigenvalues are different (it may still be possible with repeated Eigenvalues). The Columns of P are Eigenvectors and the diagonal of D holds the Eigenvalues; viewed in the Eigenvector coordinate system, the transformation simplifies to stretching along each axis. That's why powers can be computed easily as powers of the diagonal entries, as in Aᵏ = PDᵏP⁻¹.

## Orthogonality and Least Squares

#### Orthogonal Projection
ONE: A Projection is a Vector's **shadow dropped straight down** onto some line or plane. That shadow is **the point on the plane closest to the original Vector**.
LIKE: It's the shadow a stick casts on the ground when the midday sun shines straight down. The tip of the shadow is the point on the ground closest to the tip of the stick.
STEP:
1. **Orthogonal**: If two Vectors' Dot Product is 0, they're perpendicular.
2. Projecting a Vector y onto a Subspace W splits it as y = ŷ + z, where ŷ is the shadow inside W and z is the remainder perpendicular to W.
3. There is **exactly one** way to split it like this.
4. **ŷ is the point in W closest to y**, because the perpendicular distance is the shortest.
5. **Projection onto one direction u**: ŷ = (y·u / u·u) u. Example: Projecting y = (3, 4) onto the direction u = (1, 0) gives (3, 0).
6. If W has a Basis of mutually perpendicular Vectors (an Orthogonal Basis), you just add up the Projections onto each Basis direction.
WHY: It's the geometric principle behind every problem of finding "the closest answer when you can't match exactly" (Least Squares).
END: A Projection is a shadow dropped straight down, and the shadow is the closest point on the plane.

#### The Gram-Schmidt Process and QR Decomposition
ONE: Gram-Schmidt is the method of starting from any Basis and building **a Basis of mutually perpendicular Vectors**, and expressing it as Matrices gives the **A = QR** decomposition.
LIKE: It's like standing up tilted pillars one at a time, shaving off the part of each that leans toward the pillars already stood up, so that every pillar ends up at right angles to the others.
STEP:
1. Use the first Vector as is for the first axis.
2. From the second Vector, subtract its component in the first axis's direction (its Projection). What remains is perpendicular to the first axis.
3. From the third Vector, subtract its components in the directions of both earlier axes. Keep going.
4. The resulting Vectors are mutually perpendicular (an **Orthogonal Basis**) and Span the same space as the originals.
5. Make each Vector's length 1 (Normalize) and you get an **Orthonormal Basis**.
6. **QR Decomposition**: Write a Matrix A with independent Columns as A = QR. Q has Orthonormal Columns, and R is an Upper Triangular Matrix.
USE: Used to solve Least Squares in a numerically stable way and in the QR Algorithm for computing Eigenvalues.
END: Subtract the components along earlier directions in turn and you get mutually perpendicular axes (Gram-Schmidt). Written as Matrices, it's A = QR.

#### Least Squares: The Closest Solution
ONE: A method for finding, when Ax = b has no solution, **the x with the smallest sum of squared errors**. **Linear Regression**, drawing the line that best fits the data, is exactly this problem.
LIKE: There's no line that passes exactly through every point, but there is a line for which the sum of the squared vertical distances from each point to the line is smallest.
STEP:
1. With lots of data, Ax = b has far more equations than variables, so most of the time there's no exact solution. It means b lies outside A's Column Space.
2. So we find the x̂ that makes ‖Ax − b‖² (the sum of squared errors) as small as possible. This is the **Least Squares Solution**.
3. Geometrically, Ax̂ is the point where b is **Projected** onto the Column Space (the closest point).
4. Then the error b − Ax̂ is perpendicular to the Column Space, so Aᵀ(b − Ax̂) = 0.
5. Rearranged, it's the **Normal Equation: AᵀAx̂ = Aᵀb**. If the Columns are independent, x̂ = (AᵀA)⁻¹Aᵀb.
6. **OLS Regression**: With A as the data Matrix X and b as the targets y, β̂ = (XᵀX)⁻¹Xᵀy. It's the most basic formula for "predicting test scores from study hours" (topic 14).
KEY: Why use squares: positive and negative errors don't cancel each other out, and it's easy to differentiate, which yields a clean formula like the one above. In exchange, it's sensitive to large errors (outliers).
END: When there's no exact solution, minimize the sum of squared errors. The answer is a Projection, and the formula is the Normal Equation AᵀAx̂ = Aᵀb.

### What is Orthogonal Projection?
E: It's the shadow a stick casts on the ground when the midday sun shines straight down. The shadow is the point on the ground closest to the tip of the stick.
A: Orthogonally Projecting a Vector y onto a Subspace W splits it uniquely as y = ŷ + z (ŷ inside W, z perpendicular to W), and ŷ is the point in W closest to y. The Projection onto a single Vector u's direction is ŷ = (y·u / u·u)u, and if W has an Orthogonal Basis, you add up the Projections onto each Basis direction.

### What do the Gram-Schmidt Process and QR Decomposition produce?
A: Gram-Schmidt builds an Orthogonal Basis for the same space by successively subtracting from each Basis Vector its components (Projections) along the Vectors built so far, and making their lengths 1 (Normalize) gives an Orthonormal Basis. Applying this to a Matrix A with Linearly Independent Columns decomposes it as A = QR (Q has Orthonormal Columns, R is an Invertible Upper Triangular Matrix), which is used to solve Least Squares in a numerically stable way.

### For Ax = b with no solution, what is "the closest solution"? How do the Normal Equation and OLS relate?
E: There's no line that passes exactly through every point, but there is a line for which the sum of the squared vertical distances from each point to the line is smallest.
A: If b lies outside the Column Space, we find the Least Squares Solution x̂ that minimizes ‖Ax − b‖², and Ax̂ is the point where b is Projected onto the Column Space. Then the Residual b − Ax̂ is perpendicular to every Column, so Aᵀ(b − Ax̂) = 0, giving the Normal Equation AᵀAx̂ = Aᵀb, and if the Columns are independent, x̂ = (AᵀA)⁻¹Aᵀb. OLS (Ordinary Least Squares) Regression is the same problem with A as the Data Matrix X and b as the targets y, giving β̂ = (XᵀX)⁻¹Xᵀy (see 14 Machine Learning).

## Symmetric Matrices·SVD·Positive Definite

#### Symmetric Matrices and the Spectral Theorem
ONE: A **Symmetric Matrix (Aᵀ = A)** has all real Eigenvalues and can always be diagonalized with **mutually perpendicular Eigenvectors** (A = PDPᵀ).
LIKE: A symmetric rubber-sheet deformation can always be described as stretching and shrinking only along two (or three) mutually perpendicular directions. No slanted axes are needed.
STEP:
1. **Symmetric Matrix**: It's mirror-symmetric across the diagonal. Aᵢⱼ = Aⱼᵢ.
2. **Spectral Theorem**: A real Symmetric Matrix ① has all real Eigenvalues ② has mutually perpendicular Eigenvectors for different Eigenvalues ③ always admits a choice of n mutually perpendicular Eigenvectors of length 1.
3. So it can be written as **A = PDPᵀ**. P is an Orthogonal Matrix, so P⁻¹ = Pᵀ (computing the Inverse is just a Transpose).
4. Conversely, any Matrix that can be Orthogonally Diagonalized like this must be Symmetric.
5. A **Covariance Matrix** (a Matrix holding how much a dataset's variables vary together) is always Symmetric.
USE: PCA decomposes the Covariance Matrix with this theorem to find mutually perpendicular Principal Components (topic 14).
END: A symmetric Matrix always decomposes cleanly along mutually perpendicular special directions. That's why PCA works.

#### SVD (Singular Value Decomposition)
ONE: **Every Matrix** can be decomposed into three steps: **"rotate → stretch along each axis → rotate again."** That's SVD, A = UΣVᵀ.
LIKE: It says that any strange rubber-sheet deformation at all can be reproduced exactly with three actions: "first rotate the sheet, stretch it horizontally and vertically, then rotate it again."
STEP:
1. **A = UΣVᵀ**. Vᵀ is the first rotation (·reflection), Σ stretches along each axis, and U is the second rotation (·reflection).
2. U and V are Orthogonal Matrices (rotations·reflections), and Σ is a Matrix with nonnegative **Singular Values** σ₁ ≥ σ₂ ≥ … on its diagonal.
3. The Singular Values are the square roots of the Eigenvalues of AᵀA.
4. **Difference from Eigendecomposition**: Diagonalization works only for square Matrices that meet the conditions, but SVD exists for **every Matrix, including rectangular ones**.
5. **Low-Rank Approximation**: Keep only the k largest Singular Values and set the rest to 0, and you get the Rank-k Matrix closest to the original (the Eckart-Young Theorem). It compresses by keeping only the important information.
USE: Image compression (reconstructing a photo almost perfectly from just a few large Singular Values), recommendation systems (decomposing a users × movies rating Matrix), computing PCA, LSA in search, and noise removal.
END: SVD breaks every Matrix into "rotate·stretch·rotate." Keeping only the large Singular Values gives the best possible compression.

#### Positive Definite Matrices
ONE: A Symmetric Matrix A is Positive Definite if **xᵀAx > 0** for every nonzero x. It's shaped like **a bowl that curves upward** in every direction, so it **definitely has a minimum**.
LIKE: Put a marble in a bowl and, whichever way it rolls, it ends up gathering at the single lowest point. Positive Definite is this shape: "a bowl that goes up in every direction."
STEP:
1. **Definition**: xᵀAx > 0 for every nonzero x.
2. **Equivalent statement**: All of A's Eigenvalues are positive.
3. Plotting f(x) = xᵀAx gives a bowl curving upward in every direction. There's exactly one bottom (minimum).
4. **What it means in Optimization**: If at a point where a function's Gradient is 0 the **Hessian** (the second-derivative Matrix) is Positive Definite, that point is a **Local Minimum**.
5. If the Hessian is Positive Definite everywhere, the function is **Strictly Convex**, so there's only one minimum. Wherever you start, Gradient Descent finds that one point.
6. If the Eigenvalues are a mix of positive and negative, it's shaped like a saddle (**Saddle Point**).
WHY: Training in Machine Learning is Optimization that minimizes Loss. Whether it's bowl-shaped or not decides whether training is easy or hard.
END: Positive Definite = all Eigenvalues positive = a bowl that goes up in every direction. The minimum is guaranteed.

### Why can a Symmetric Matrix always be Orthogonally Diagonalized?
A: By the Spectral Theorem, a real Symmetric Matrix (Aᵀ = A) has all real Eigenvalues, its Eigenvectors for different Eigenvalues are mutually Orthogonal, and n Orthonormal Eigenvectors can always be chosen, so it can be written as A = PDPᵀ (P⁻¹ = Pᵀ). Conversely, any Matrix that can be Orthogonally Diagonalized must be Symmetric. Because the Covariance Matrix is Symmetric, this theorem is what makes PCA, which obtains mutually perpendicular Principal Components, possible.

### What is SVD (Singular Value Decomposition)? How is it different from Eigendecomposition?
E: It says any transformation can be split into three steps: "rotate → stretch along each axis → rotate again."
A: SVD decomposes every m×n Matrix as A = UΣVᵀ, where U·V are Orthogonal Matrices (rotations·reflections) and Σ is a Matrix with nonnegative Singular Values σᵢ (the square roots of AᵀA's Eigenvalues) on its diagonal. Eigendecomposition works only for square Matrices that are Diagonalizable, but SVD always exists, including for rectangular Matrices. Keeping only the k largest Singular Values gives the closest Rank-k approximation (Low-Rank Approximation) (the Eckart-Young Theorem), which is used in PCA, image compression, recommendation systems, and LSA.

### What is a Positive Definite Matrix? Why does it matter in Optimization?
A: A Symmetric Matrix A is Positive Definite if xᵀAx > 0 for every nonzero x, which is equivalent to all its Eigenvalues being positive. Since xᵀAx is shaped like a bowl curving upward in every direction, if the Hessian is Positive Definite at a point where the Gradient is 0, that point is a Local Minimum, and if the Hessian is Positive Definite everywhere, the function is Strictly Convex and has only one minimum.

## Matrix Calculus (The Basis of Backpropagation)

#### Gradient · Jacobian · Hessian
ONE: The **Gradient** is the **direction in which a function increases most steeply**, the **Jacobian** is **a Matrix collecting the rates of change** of a Vector function, and the **Hessian** is the second-derivative Matrix representing **curvature**.
LIKE: On a foggy mountain, feeling the slope under your feet is the Gradient (the direction of steepest climb), and feeling how quickly that slope changes (whether it's concave or convex) is the Hessian.
STEP:
1. **Partial Derivative**: With several variables, the rate of change when you nudge just one while holding the rest fixed.
2. **Gradient ∇f**: The Vector of partial derivatives of f: Rⁿ → R (several inputs, one output). It points in the direction of steepest increase.
3. So to reduce Loss, you move a little at a time **in the opposite direction of the Gradient**. This is **Gradient Descent**.
4. **Jacobian**: For f: Rⁿ → Rᵐ (several inputs, several outputs), the m×n Matrix with Jᵢⱼ = ∂fᵢ/∂xⱼ. It's a linear approximation of how the output changes when you nudge the input.
5. **Hessian**: The n×n Matrix of second partial derivatives. It tells you whether things are concave like a bowl or curved like a saddle.
USE: The Gradient is the foundation of all Neural Network training, and the Hessian is used for analyzing Optimizers and in second-order optimization methods (Newton's Method).
END: Gradient = the direction of steepest climb, Jacobian = a table of multivariable rates of change, Hessian = curvature.

#### The Chain Rule and Backpropagation
ONE: The derivative of a function composed several times is **the product of the derivatives (Jacobians) of each step** (the Chain Rule). A Neural Network is a function made by composing layers many times, and computing this **backward from the output** is Backpropagation.
LIKE: With three gears meshed together, if turning the first gear once turns the second twice, and turning the second once turns the third three times, then one turn of the first turns the third 2 × 3 = 6 times. Multiplying the per-step ratios is the Chain Rule.
STEP:
1. **Single-variable Chain Rule**: If h(x) = g(f(x)), then h'(x) = g'(f(x)) × f'(x).
2. **Matrix version**: J_h(x) = J_g(f(x)) × J_f(x). Matrix-multiply each step's Jacobian.
3. A Neural Network is a long composite function: input → layer 1 → layer 2 → … → output → Loss.
4. To know how much each Weight affects the Loss (the Gradient), you have to apply the Chain Rule backward from the Loss to that Weight.
5. **Backpropagation**: Starting from the output side, it multiplies each layer's Jacobian (Transposed) in turn, passing the Gradient back to earlier layers. It reuses intermediate results once computed, which makes it very efficient.
6. When the network is deep and numbers smaller than 1 keep getting multiplied in this product, the Gradient becomes almost 0 (**Vanishing Gradient**), and when numbers larger than 1 keep getting multiplied, it blows up (**Exploding Gradient**) (topic 15).
END: The derivative of a composite function is the product of per-step derivatives, and passing it backward from the output is Backpropagation.

### What are the Gradient, Jacobian, and Hessian?
E: On a foggy mountain, feeling the slope under your feet is the Gradient (the direction of steepest climb), and feeling how quickly the slope changes (whether it's concave or convex) is the Hessian.
A: The Gradient ∇f of a Scalar function f: Rⁿ → R is the Vector of Partial Derivatives and points in the direction of steepest increase, and the Hessian is the n×n Matrix of second partial derivatives representing Curvature. The Jacobian of a Vector function f: Rⁿ → Rᵐ is the m×n Matrix with Jᵢⱼ = ∂fᵢ/∂xⱼ, giving a linear approximation of how the output changes when you nudge the input.

### What does the Chain Rule look like written with Matrices?
A: The Jacobian of a composite function h(x) = g(f(x)) is Jₕ(x) = J_g(f(x)) · J_f(x), the Matrix Multiplication of each step's Jacobian. A Neural Network is a function composed of many Layers, so passing the Gradient of the Scalar Loss backward from the output side by successively multiplying the Transpose of each Layer's Jacobian is Backpropagation (see 15 Deep Learning).
